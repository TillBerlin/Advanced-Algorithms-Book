\chapter{Preliminaries}
\label{app:preliminaries}

This appendix is the background the rest of the book stands on. It is not a
course in mathematics and it is not a summary of the course: everything here is
assumed silently in the chapters, and nothing here is examined for its own
sake. It is short because the list of things genuinely needed is short. What is
long is the list of things a reader might reasonably \emph{expect} to be needed
and which are not; those have been left out.

Every fact stated below is used somewhere in the book, and the cross-references
say where. If a definition here looks unmotivated, follow the reference: the
place it is used is the reason it is here.

\section{How to use this appendix}
\label{sec:prelim-how-to-use}
%==============================================================================

Readers arrive at a course like this one from very different places, and the
useful question is not how much mathematics somebody knows in general but
whether a specific short list of things is fluent. The list is below, as ten
questions. They are not a test to pass; they are a way of finding out, in
fifteen minutes and in private, which of the sections of this appendix are
worth your time.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Order these by growth: $n\log n$, $2^n$, $n^{1.5}$, $n!$, $1.5^n$,
        $n^{\log n}$. Which two are closest?
  \item Write out what $f(n) = \bigoh{g(n)}$ means, with its quantifiers.
  \item A graph on $n$ vertices has no cycle. How many edges can it have, and
        why?
  \item What do breadth-first and depth-first search compute, in what running
        time, and what does each need to store?
  \item State what it means for a problem to be \textrm{NP}-complete, and name
        one. If $A$ reduces to $B$ in polynomial time, what does that tell you
        about $A$, and what about $B$?
  \item Solve $T(n) = 2\,T(n/2) + \bigoh{n}$.
  \item Flip $n$ fair coins. What is the expected number of heads, and does the
        answer change if the coins are glued together in pairs?
  \item What is $\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}$, and roughly how big is
        $\binom{n}{n/2}$?
  \item What is a linear program, and what does it mean to \emph{relax} an
        integer program to one?
  \item Prove that a connected graph on $n$ vertices has at least $n-1$ edges.
\end{enumerate}

Questions~1, 2, 6, 7, 8 and~10 are mathematics; questions~3, 4, 5 and~9 are the
vocabulary of algorithms. The split matters more than the score.

\paragraph{If nearly all of them were routine.}
Read \cref{sec:prelim-notation} for the notation, and
\cref{sec:prelim-asymptotics} for the $\Ostar$ convention, which is used from
\cref{ch:exact-i} onwards and is probably new even if everything else is not.
Then start the book and come back only when a chapter sends you here.

\paragraph{If the mathematics was routine and the algorithmics was not.}
This is the common profile for a reader whose degree is in mathematics,
physics, or another quantitative subject, and it is the easiest gap to close,
because what is missing is vocabulary and one model of computation rather than
technique. Read \cref{sec:prelim-graphs,sec:prelim-asymptotics,%
sec:prelim-machine,sec:prelim-np} properly --- perhaps two evenings --- and
keep \citet{CLRS2022} or \citet{KleinbergTardos2005} within reach for the
standard algorithms named in passing: breadth-first and depth-first search,
Dijkstra, minimum spanning trees, sorting, hash tables, and dynamic programming
on arrays. The mathematical maturity you already have is the part that cannot
be acquired in two evenings, and it is the part this book leans on hardest.

\paragraph{If several items in both halves were unfamiliar.}
Then what is missing is a full bachelor-level algorithms course, and that is a
statement about sequencing, not about ability. It is worth being concrete about
what that means for reading this book. The chapters are arguments, and each one
assumes that the standard algorithms and the class \textrm{NP} are already
familiar objects that can be used without being explained. Read without that,
the book turns into a list of facts to memorise, which is both much harder and
much less interesting than what is actually going on. Three honest options:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \emph{Take the prerequisite first and this course later.} This is the
        cheapest route by a wide margin, and it is a common one. An
        introductory algorithms course plus a discrete mathematics course is
        roughly one semester of work and makes the whole of this book
        accessible rather than half of it.
  \item \emph{Close the gap in parallel.} Possible, and people do it, but be
        realistic about the size: working through Parts I and~IV of
        \citet{CLRS2022}, or Chapters~1--6 of \citet{KleinbergTardos2005},
        alongside the course is a second course's worth of effort on top of
        this one. \citet{LehmanLeightonMeyer2018}, which is free, covers the
        discrete mathematics.
  \item \emph{Read it without the exam.} Several parts of the book were written
        to be readable on their own. \Cref{ch:stable-marriage} needs almost
        nothing; the opening sections of \cref{ch:planar,ch:approx-i} need
        little; and the \emph{Intuition} boxes throughout are written to say
        what an argument is really doing, in words, and can be read
        independently of the proofs around them.
\end{itemize}

None of this is a judgement about who should be in the room. The variable is
preparation and it is finite, which is exactly why it is worth measuring
honestly at the start rather than discovering it in week six.

\section{Notation}
\label{sec:prelim-notation}
%==============================================================================

Standard mathematical notation is used without comment. What follows is only
the part where books differ from one another, so that no chapter has to stop
and say which convention it is using.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
Notation & Meaning \\
\midrule
$\N, \Z, \Q, \R$ & the naturals (including $0$), integers, rationals, reals \\
$[n]$ & the set $\{1, 2, \ldots, n\}$ \\
$|S|$ & the number of elements of a finite set $S$ \\
$2^{S}$ & the family of all subsets of $S$; hence $|2^S| = 2^{|S|}$ \\
$S \setminus T$ & the elements of $S$ not in $T$ \\
$S \subseteq T$ & containment, not necessarily strict \\
$\binom{S}{k}$ & the family of $k$-element subsets of $S$ \\
$\log$ & the logarithm to base $2$; $\ln$ is the natural one \\
$a \bmod b$ & the remainder of $a$ on division by $b$ \\
$\lfloor x \rfloor, \lceil x \rceil$ & rounding down and up \\
$\poly(n)$ & some fixed polynomial in $n$, whose degree does not matter \\
$\OPT$ & the value of an optimal solution to the instance at hand \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Two conventions about size. Throughout, $n$ is the size of the instance, and in
a graph problem specifically $n = |V|$ and $m = |E|$; when a chapter needs a
different meaning for $n$ it says so on the spot. And an algorithm's input is
always a finite string: when we say a number is part of the input, we mean its
binary representation is, which is what makes the distinction between
polynomial and pseudo-polynomial running times in \cref{ch:approx-ii} a real
one.

\section{Graphs}
\label{sec:prelim-graphs}
%==============================================================================

Graphs are the single most common object in the book, and this section fixes
their vocabulary once. Nothing here is deep; the point is that
\cref{ch:matching} and \cref{ch:treewidth} should be able to say ``the closed
neighbourhood of $v$'' without pausing.

A \emph{graph} $G = (V,E)$ has a finite vertex set $V$ and an edge set $E$. In
an \emph{undirected} graph an edge is an unordered pair $\{u,v\}$; in a
\emph{directed} graph, or digraph, it is an ordered pair $(u,v)$, and we call
it an \emph{arc}. Unless a chapter says otherwise, graphs are undirected,
\emph{simple} --- no loops, no repeated edges --- and finite. Where multiple
edges are allowed, as in \cref{ch:randomized}, the object is called a
\emph{multigraph} and the text says so.

Vertices $u$ and $v$ are \emph{adjacent}, or \emph{neighbours}, if
$\{u,v\} \in E$; the edge is then \emph{incident} to both. The
\emph{neighbourhood} of $v$ is $N(v) = \{u : \{u,v\} \in E\}$ and the
\emph{closed neighbourhood} is $N[v] = N(v) \cup \{v\}$. The \emph{degree}
$\deg(v)$ is $|N(v)|$, and $\deg_H(v)$ is the degree of $v$ counted only inside
a subgraph $H$. Summing degrees counts every edge twice, so
$\sum_{v} \deg(v) = 2m$; this two-line observation is used more often than any
other fact about graphs.

A \emph{path} is a sequence of distinct vertices consecutively adjacent, a
\emph{cycle} is a path whose two ends are also adjacent, and a \emph{walk} is
the same without the requirement that the vertices be distinct --- a
distinction that matters exactly once, in \cref{sec:ie-hamilton}. A graph is
\emph{connected} if every two vertices are joined by a path, a \emph{tree} if
it is connected and has no cycle, and a \emph{forest} if it has no cycle. A
tree on $n$ vertices has $n-1$ edges, a forest has at most that many, and a connected graph
has at least $n-1$; all three are the same induction.

A \emph{subgraph} keeps some vertices and some of the edges among them; the
\emph{induced} subgraph $G[S]$ on $S \subseteq V$ keeps \emph{all} edges with
both ends in $S$. We write $G - v$ for $G[V \setminus \{v\}]$ and, for a set,
$G \setminus S$ for $G[V \setminus S]$. A \emph{clique} is a set of pairwise
adjacent vertices, an \emph{independent set} one of pairwise non-adjacent
vertices, a \emph{vertex cover} a set meeting every edge, and a
\emph{dominating set} a set $D$ with $N[D] = V$. A graph is \emph{bipartite} if
its vertices split into two independent sets; equivalently, if it has no cycle
of odd length.

Two further notions are defined where they are needed rather than here, because
both take a page to do properly: planarity in \cref{ch:planar} and treewidth in
\cref{ch:treewidth}.

\section{Asymptotics, and the \texorpdfstring{$\Ostar$}{O*} convention}
\label{sec:prelim-asymptotics}
%==============================================================================

Running times in this book are always asymptotic, and one of the four notations
below is specific to the exponential-time literature and will be new to most
readers. It is worth ten minutes even if the other three are old friends.

For functions $f, g : \N \to \R_{\ge 0}$:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item $f = \bigoh{g}$ if there are $c > 0$ and $n_0$ with
        $f(n) \le c\,g(n)$ for all $n \ge n_0$;
  \item $f = \Omega(g)$ if $g = \bigoh{f}$;
  \item $f = \Theta(g)$ if both;
  \item $f = o(g)$ if $f(n)/g(n) \to 0$.
\end{itemize}
The equals sign is the traditional abuse: $\bigoh{g}$ is a set of functions and
$f = \bigoh{g}$ means $f$ belongs to it. Read $2^{o(n)}$, which is the shape of
the hypotheses in \cref{ch:lower-bounds}, as ``$2^{f(n)}$ for some $f$ with
$f(n)/n \to 0$'' --- it is a statement about the exponent, and it is
\emph{not} the same as $\bigoh{2^{\varepsilon n}}$ for one fixed $\varepsilon$.

Now the fourth. Once running times are exponential, a polynomial factor is
noise: $\bigoh{2^n \cdot n^{5}}$ and $\bigoh{2^n}$ differ by less than
$\bigoh{2^{1.001n}}$ does from either. Carrying the polynomial through every
proof costs attention and buys nothing, so it is suppressed.

\begin{definition}[$\Ostar$ notation]\label{def:prelim-ostar}
$f(n) = \Ostar(g(n))$ if $f(n) = \bigoh{g(n) \cdot \poly(n)}$.
\end{definition}

So $\Ostar(2^n)$ means $2^n$ up to a polynomial factor, and $\Ostar(1)$ is
exactly ``polynomial''. The convention is safe only because the base of the
exponential is what everything turns on; it would be indefensible for
polynomial running times, and the book never uses it there. Two warnings that
have caught people out. First, $\Ostar$ hides $\poly(n)$ and nothing else: a
factor of $2^{n/2}$ is not polynomial no matter how it arose. Second, when a
bound is stated as $\bigoh{1.3803^n}$ rather than $\Ostar(\tau^n)$, the base has
usually been rounded up so as to swallow the polynomial factor, which is why
such bases are quoted with a strict inequality; \cref{sec:branching}
does this explicitly.

\section{The machine model}
\label{sec:prelim-machine}
%==============================================================================

Every running time is a count of steps of some machine, and it is worth saying
once which machine, because Part~II is about what happens when the choice is
made differently.

Unless stated otherwise we count steps of a \emph{word RAM}: memory is an array
of cells holding integers of $w$ bits, with $w$ at least $\log n$ so that an
index into the input fits in one cell, and reading a cell, writing a cell, and
doing one arithmetic operation or comparison on cell contents each cost one
step. This is the model in which sorting is $\bigoh{n \log n}$ and hashing is
constant time per operation, and it is close enough to a real machine that the
counts mean something.

Two departures from it appear in the book, and both are deliberate. In
\cref{ch:real-models} the cells hold arbitrary real numbers and arithmetic
on them is exact and unit-cost; that is the \emph{real RAM}, and Part~II is
about what it can and cannot do. In \cref{ch:lower-bounds} the model recedes
entirely, because the hypotheses there are about the existence of algorithms in
any reasonable model at all, and the polynomial differences between models are
below the resolution of the statements.

\section{Problems, reductions, P and NP}
\label{sec:prelim-np}
%==============================================================================

The whole book is a response to one fact --- that certain problems have no
known efficient algorithm --- so the machinery for stating that fact precisely
has to be available from the first page. This section is that machinery, in its
standard form; \cref{ch:introduction} uses it and does not repeat it.

A \emph{decision problem} is a set of instances, each labelled yes or no, with
instances encoded as finite strings. \textrm{P} is the class of decision
problems solvable in time polynomial in the length of the instance.
\textrm{NP} is the class of decision problems whose yes-instances have short
certificates: $A \in \mathrm{NP}$ if there is a polynomial-time algorithm $V$
and a polynomial $p$ such that $x$ is a yes-instance exactly when
$V(x,c)$ accepts for some certificate $c$ with $|c| \le p(|x|)$. A satisfying
assignment, a Hamiltonian cycle, a $k$-clique: each is a certificate, and each
can be checked quickly. Whether \textrm{P} $=$ \textrm{NP} is open, and the
whole of this book is written on the assumption that it is not.

Hardness is transported between problems by reduction, and the direction of
the implication is the thing to keep straight.

\begin{definition}[Polynomial-time reduction]\label{def:prelim-reduction}
$A$ \emph{reduces} to $B$, written $A \le_p B$, if there is a polynomial-time
computable map $f$ taking instances of $A$ to instances of $B$ such that $x$ is
a yes-instance of $A$ exactly when $f(x)$ is a yes-instance of $B$.
\end{definition}

Read $A \le_p B$ as ``$B$ is at least as hard as $A$''. It gives one
implication in each direction: an efficient algorithm for $B$ yields one for
$A$, and a proof that $A$ is hard shows that $B$ is hard. A problem $B$ is
\emph{\textrm{NP}-hard} if $A \le_p B$ for every $A \in \mathrm{NP}$, and
\emph{\textrm{NP}-complete} if in addition $B \in \mathrm{NP}$. Since $\le_p$
is transitive, showing $B$ \textrm{NP}-hard needs only one reduction, from a
single problem already known to be \textrm{NP}-hard --- which is what every
hardness proof in this book does. The first such problem was supplied by
Cook and Levin: \lang{SAT} is \textrm{NP}-complete. \Citet{GareyJohnson1979} is
still the standard catalogue of the rest.

Three habits of speech are worth naming, because the book uses all three.
An \emph{optimisation} problem is called \textrm{NP}-hard when its decision
version is; a problem is \emph{\textrm{NP}-hard already for} some restricted
class of instances when the reduction outputs only instances of that class,
which is a stronger and more useful statement; and a running time is
\emph{pseudo-polynomial} when it is polynomial in the numbers appearing in the
instance rather than in their encoding length, a distinction that
\cref{ch:approx-ii} turns into an algorithm.

Everything else in the way of complexity classes --- $\ER$ in
\cref{ch:etr}, $\mathrm{FPT}$ and $\mathrm{W}[1]$ in \cref{ch:fpt-i}, and the
hypotheses ETH and SETH in \cref{ch:lower-bounds} --- is defined where it is
introduced, and each is a variation on the pattern above: a class, a notion of
reduction that preserves the resource in question, and a problem complete for
it.

\section{Counting and estimating}
\label{sec:prelim-counting}
%==============================================================================

Exponential running times are counted rather than derived, and the counting
uses a handful of estimates over and over. They are collected here so that the
chapters can use them in one line.

The number of subsets of an $n$-element set is $2^n$, the number of
$k$-element subsets is $\binom{n}{k}$, and the number of orderings is $n!$.
The three identities used most are
\[ \sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k} = 2^n, \qquad
   \binom{n}{k} \le \Bigl(\frac{en}{k}\Bigr)^{k}, \qquad
   \binom{n}{n/2} = \Theta\Bigl(\frac{2^n}{\sqrt n}\Bigr). \]
The middle one is the workhorse: it is what makes a bound like
$\binom{n}{k} \le n^k$ unnecessary when $k$ is a parameter rather than a
constant. The third says that the central binomial coefficient is, up to a
polynomial factor, the whole of $2^n$ --- so under $\Ostar$ they are the same,
which is used without comment in \cref{ch:exact-ii}.

Two further estimates get their own statements in the text where they are
needed, and are named here only so that a reader arriving from
\cref{sec:prelim-how-to-use} knows they exist: Stirling's approximation
$n! = \Ostar((n/e)^n)$, in \cref{prop:stirling}, and the entropy bound on a
partial sum of binomial coefficients, in the proof of \cref{thm:schoening}.
The inequality $1 + x \le e^{x}$, valid for every real $x$, is used throughout
\cref{ch:randomized} and is worth memorising.

Recurrences arising from divide and conquer, such as
$T(n) = 2\,T(n/2) + \bigoh{n}$, are assumed familiar. Recurrences arising from
branching, such as $T(n) \le T(n-1) + T(n-4)$, are not: they are solved by the
method of \cref{sec:branching}, which is part of the course.

\section{Probability}
\label{sec:prelim-probability}
%==============================================================================

Probability appears in one chapter, and only finite probability spaces are
needed --- coins, uniform choices from a finite set, and independent
repetitions. Four facts carry all of it.

Write $\Pr[E]$ for the probability of an event and $\mathbb{E}[X]$ for the
expectation of a random variable.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \emph{Linearity of expectation.}
        $\mathbb{E}[X + Y] = \mathbb{E}[X] + \mathbb{E}[Y]$, always, whether or
        not $X$ and $Y$ are independent. This is the single most useful fact in
        the subject, and its indifference to independence is the reason.
  \item \emph{Independence.} Events are independent when the probability of
        their conjunction is the product of their probabilities; the
        probabilities of $n$ independent choices multiply, which is where a
        factor like $(2/3)^n$ in \cref{sec:randomized-3col} comes from.
  \item \emph{The union bound.} $\Pr[E_1 \cup \cdots \cup E_t] \le
        \sum_i \Pr[E_i]$, for any events at all.
  \item \emph{Amplification.} An algorithm that says yes correctly with
        probability at least $p$ and never says yes wrongly can be run
        $\lceil 1/p \rceil$ times to fail with probability at most $1/e$. This
        is proved as \cref{lem:amplification}, and it is the reason a success
        probability of $2^{-n}$ is a running time of $\Ostar(2^n)$ rather than
        a defeat.
\end{itemize}

Nothing heavier is used: no concentration inequalities, no continuous
distributions, no martingales. A reader wanting the subject properly will find
it in \citet{MitzenmacherUpfal2017}.

\section{Linear algebra and linear programming}
\label{sec:prelim-linear}
%==============================================================================

Two pieces of linear machinery appear, each in a small number of places, and
both are used as black boxes rather than developed.

From linear algebra, only this: two $n \times n$ matrices can be multiplied in
$\bigoh{n^{\omega}}$ arithmetic operations, where $\omega < 2.373$ is the
\emph{matrix multiplication exponent}. The value of $\omega$ is not known; the
bound quoted is the one used in \cref{sec:bisection-width}, it has been improved
repeatedly, and any statement whose truth depends on the third decimal place of
$\omega$ should be read with that in mind. Rank over the two-element field
$\mathrm{GF}(2)$ makes one appearance, in the closing notes of
\cref{ch:treewidth}, and nothing is assumed about it beyond the definition.

A \emph{linear program} asks for a vector $x \in \R^{d}$ maximising a linear
objective $c^{\!\top} x$ subject to finitely many linear inequalities
$Ax \le b$. Over the rationals it is solvable in time polynomial in the bit
size of the input; the algorithms that do this are not in this book, and we use
the fact and not the method. If the variables are additionally required to be
integers, the problem is an \emph{integer} linear program and is
\textrm{NP}-hard --- so integrality is exactly where the difficulty sits.

That observation is used twice, in opposite directions. Dropping the
integrality requirement gives the \emph{relaxation}, whose optimum bounds the
integer optimum and whose solution can then be rounded, which is the
$2$-approximation for \lang{Vertex Cover} in \cref{ch:approx-i}. And a
relaxation whose optimal solutions happen to be almost integral gives more than
a bound: it gives a decision, which is the Nemhauser--Trotter kernel of
\cref{ch:kernelization}. Duality appears only in the form in which
\cref{ch:max-flow} already presents it, as max-flow min-cut.

\section{What the proofs look like}
\label{sec:prelim-proofs}
%==============================================================================

The last preliminary is not a fact but a habit. The proofs in this book are
built from a small number of moves, and a reader who recognises the move is
halfway through the proof. Naming them here also serves as a map of where to
look for a worked instance of each.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \emph{Induction}, usually on the size of the instance or the number of
        steps an algorithm has taken. An invariant that holds initially and is
        preserved by every step is the same argument wearing different clothes;
        \cref{ch:max-flow} is full of them.
  \item \emph{Exchange.} Take an optimal solution, show it can be modified to
        look like the one the algorithm produces without getting worse, and
        conclude the algorithm is optimal. \Cref{ch:stable-marriage} and
        \cref{ch:matching} both turn on this.
  \item \emph{Extremal choice.} Pick the largest, the smallest, or the
        deepest object with some property and derive a contradiction from its
        extremality. \Cref{prop:tw-forest} is a short example.
  \item \emph{Counting two ways.} Bound one quantity by pairing its elements
        with those of another and counting the pairs from both sides. Every
        kernel bound in \cref{ch:kernelization} is this.
  \item \emph{Reduction.} Transform an instance of one problem into an instance
        of another so that the answers agree, and argue \emph{both} directions
        --- yes maps to yes, and no maps to no. Half the errors in this
        subject are a missing second direction, and every reduction in
        \cref{ch:lower-bounds,ch:hardness} states it explicitly.
\end{itemize}

Finally, a proof in this book is expected to be a paragraph of prose that a
reader can check, not a formal derivation. If a step feels like arithmetic, it
should be written out; if it feels like bookkeeping, it should be summarised.
Getting that line in the right place is what mathematical maturity means in
practice, and it is the one prerequisite that no appendix can supply.

\section{Where to read more}
\label{sec:prelim-reading}
%==============================================================================

The books below are the ones to reach for when something above needs to be
learned rather than recalled. Each chapter's own notes point to the specialised
literature; these are the general ones.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \citet{CLRS2022} and \citet{KleinbergTardos2005} for the algorithms
        course this book assumes. The first is the reference, the second the
        better read.
  \item \citet{LehmanLeightonMeyer2018} for the discrete mathematics ---
        proofs, induction, counting, elementary probability. It is free.
  \item \citet{Sipser2013} for \textrm{P}, \textrm{NP} and reductions done
        slowly, and \citet{GareyJohnson1979} for the catalogue of what is known
        to be \textrm{NP}-hard.
  \item \citet{Diestel2017} for graph theory, and \citet{MitzenmacherUpfal2017}
        for probability in the service of algorithms.
\end{itemize}
