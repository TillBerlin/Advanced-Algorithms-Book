\chapter{Treewidth}
\label{ch:treewidth}

Trees are easy. Independent set, dominating set, colouring, Hamiltonian cycle
--- all \textrm{NP}-hard in general --- are solved on a tree by one bottom-up
pass, and the reason is always the same: a vertex separates its subtree from the
rest of the graph, so what happens above it and what happens below it interact
only through the vertex itself. Treewidth is what you get when you insist on
that property and ask how far a graph is from having it. It is the most useful
structural parameter in this book, and this chapter is about both halves of its
use: what it is, and how to compute with it.

\begin{goals}
  \poolgoal{define tree decompositions and treewidth}
  \poolgoal{determine the treewidth of trees, cycles and series--parallel
        graphs}
  \poolgoal{show that for any tree decomposition of $G$ some bag contains every
        clique of $G$, and conclude that the treewidth of $G$ is at least
        $|C|-1$}
  \poolgoal{show, by constructing a tree decomposition, that the treewidth of a
        $p$ by $q$ grid graph with $p \le q$ is at most $p$}
  \poolgoal{define a nice tree decomposition and describe the transformation of
        an arbitrary tree decomposition into one}
  \poolgoal{design dynamic programming algorithms over a nice tree
        decomposition, and analyse them}
  \poolgoal{state Courcelle's theorem and explain what it does and does not give
        you}
  \goalitem{state the two alternative characterisations of treewidth --- by
        $k$-boundary graphs and by the cops-and-robber game}
  \goalitem{say what is known about computing a tree decomposition}
  \goalitem{bound the treewidth of series--parallel graphs, of graphs of
        maximum degree three and of planar graphs, and use those bounds to get
        algorithms}
\end{goals}

%==============================================================================
\section{Dynamic programming on trees}
\label{sec:dp-on-trees}
%==============================================================================

Before defining anything, it is worth seeing the algorithm the whole chapter
generalises. The problem is the weighted version of independent set.

\begin{probdef}{Maximum Weight Independent Set}
  \instance A graph $G = (V,E)$ and a weight $\omega(v) \in \N$ for each vertex.
  \question An independent set --- a set of vertices no two of which are
  adjacent --- of maximum total weight.
\end{probdef}

Suppose $G$ is a tree, rooted anywhere. Write $T[v]$ for the subtree rooted at
$v$ and $\mathrm{ch}(v)$ for the children of $v$. The one thing worth knowing
about $T[v]$, as far as the rest of the tree is concerned, is the best weight
achievable inside it --- and that depends on the outside only through whether
$v$ itself is taken. So define
\[ A[v] = \text{the maximum weight of an independent set of } T[v], \]
and split on the two cases at $v$. If $v$ is not taken, the children are
unconstrained. If $v$ is taken, the children are excluded and the
grandchildren are unconstrained. That gives

\begin{equation}\label{eq:tw-tree-dp}
  A[v] \;=\;
  \begin{cases}
    \omega(v) & \text{if } T[v] \text{ is a single node,} \\[4pt]
    \max\Bigl\{\;
      \displaystyle\sum_{c \,\in\, \mathrm{ch}(v)} A[c],
      \quad
      \omega(v) + \!\!\!\displaystyle\sum_{\substack{c_1 \in \mathrm{ch}(v) \\
                                          c_2 \in \mathrm{ch}(c_1)}}\!\!\! A[c_2]
    \;\Bigr\} & \text{otherwise.}
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}

Evaluating this bottom-up touches every vertex a constant number of times, so
the running time is $\bigoh{n}$.

\begin{intuition}
The table $A$ is small because the interface between $T[v]$ and the rest of the
graph is a single vertex, so there are only two cases to remember: $v$ in, $v$
out. Everything in this chapter follows from asking what happens when the
interface is a set of $k$ vertices instead of one. The table then has $2^k$
entries rather than $2$ --- still a constant if $k$ is --- and the algorithm
still runs in linear time in $n$.
\end{intuition}

\section{From trees to graphs: four operations}
\label{sec:tw-four-operations}
%==============================================================================

Take an arbitrary graph $G$ and imagine building it up a vertex at a time. At
every moment the vertices fall into three classes: those already \emph{done},
those still \emph{to do}, and a \emph{separator} between them --- a set $S$ such
that no edge joins a done vertex to a to-do vertex. The partial solutions worth
remembering are indexed by what the solution does on $S$, and nothing else:
two partial solutions that agree on $S$ are interchangeable as far as the rest
of the graph is concerned.

So we carry a table indexed by the $2^{|S|}$ subsets of $S$, whose entry is the
best value of a partial solution on the done part that meets $S$ in that
subset. Four operations suffice to build any graph this way, and each one says
what happens to the table.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.2em,style=nextline]
  \item[New graph (leaf).] Start with a single vertex, which forms the
        separator by itself. The table has two entries: the vertex in the
        solution, or not.
  \item[Introduce a vertex.] Move a vertex $v$ from ``to do'' into the
        separator. Every existing entry splits in two, according to whether $v$
        joins the solution, and the entries that would put $v$ next to a
        separator vertex already in the solution are struck out.
  \item[Forget a vertex.] Move a vertex $v$ from the separator into ``done''.
        This is only legal when every neighbour of $v$ is already done or in the
        separator, since afterwards $v$ can never be looked at again. The two
        entries that differ only in $v$ are merged by taking the better one.
  \item[Join.] Two parts of the graph that share the same separator are merged.
        Entries with the same subset of $S$ are combined, taking care not to
        count the separator twice.
\end{description}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\figplaceholder{6cm}{Four panels showing one small graph part-way built: the
done part shaded, the to-do part in outline, the separator ringed, and the table
beside it. One panel per operation --- new graph, introduce, forget, join ---
with the table before and after.}
\caption{The four operations. The graph grows and the separator moves across it;
the table is always indexed by what the solution does on the separator.}
\label{fig:tw-four-operations}
\end{figure}

Repeat until every vertex is done: nothing is left to do, the separator is
empty, and the single remaining table entry is the answer. The tree structure
along which the operations were applied is exactly what the next section
defines, and \cref{sec:dp-on-trees} was this algorithm on a tree, with the
separator a single vertex, all along.

%==============================================================================
\section{Tree decompositions}
\label{sec:tree-decompositions}
%==============================================================================

The bookkeeping of the previous section needs a name. What we were carrying was
a tree whose nodes hold sets of vertices, and the conditions those sets must
satisfy are exactly the ones that make the separator argument valid.

\begin{definition}[Tree decomposition]\label{def:tree-decomposition}
A \emph{tree decomposition} of a graph $G = (V,E)$ is a tree $T$ together with a
set $X_i \subseteq V$ for every node $i$ of $T$, called the \emph{bag} of $i$,
such that
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=*]
  \item every vertex of $G$ is in some bag;
  \item for every edge $\{v,w\} \in E$ there is a bag containing both $v$ and
        $w$;
  \item for every vertex $v \in V$, the nodes whose bags contain $v$ form a
        connected subtree of $T$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\figplaceholder{5.5cm}{A graph on eight vertices $a,\dots,h$ beside a tree
decomposition of it: a tree of bags, with one vertex highlighted in the graph
and the connected subtree of bags containing it highlighted alongside.}
\caption{A graph and a tree decomposition of it. Condition~(iii) is the one to
check on a picture: the bags containing any fixed vertex must form a connected
piece of the tree.}
\label{fig:tw-decomposition}
\end{figure}

\begin{definition}[Width, treewidth]\label{def:treewidth}
The \emph{width} of a tree decomposition is $\max_{i \in T} |X_i| - 1$. The
\emph{treewidth} $\tw(G)$ of a graph $G$ is the minimum width over all its tree
decompositions.
\end{definition}

The $-1$ looks arbitrary and is not. It is there so that trees come out with
treewidth exactly $1$, which is the convention everyone wants; without it every
statement about treewidth would carry a stray $+1$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:tw-trees}
Every tree has treewidth $1$, and every graph with at least one edge has
treewidth at least $1$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $T$ be a tree, rooted at any node $r$. Put $X_r = \{r\}$ and, for every
other node $i$, $X_i = \{i, \mathrm{parent}(i)\}$. Every bag has size at most
$2$, so the width is $1$. The three conditions hold: every vertex is in its own
bag; every edge $\{i,\mathrm{parent}(i)\}$ is inside $X_i$; and the bags
containing a fixed vertex $v$ are $X_v$ together with the bags of the children
of $v$, which form a connected piece of the tree around $v$.

Conversely a decomposition of width $0$ has all bags of size at most one, so by
condition~(ii) the graph has no edges.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Cycles]\label{ex:tw-cycle}
A cycle $v_1 v_2 \cdots v_n v_1$ has treewidth $2$. For the upper bound take a
path of $n-2$ nodes with bags $\{v_1, v_i, v_{i+1}\}$ for $i = 2, \ldots, n-1$:
every edge of the cycle appears in some bag, including the closing edge
$\{v_n,v_1\}$, and the bags containing $v_1$ are all of them. For the lower
bound, a graph of treewidth $1$ is a forest, by the argument of
\cref{prop:tw-trees}, and a cycle is not.
\end{example}

%==============================================================================
\section{Bags are separators}
\label{sec:bags-separators}
%==============================================================================

Condition~(iii) of \cref{def:tree-decomposition} is the one that does the work,
and this section says what it buys. Everything else in the chapter --- the
correctness of the dynamic programming, the lower bounds on treewidth --- is a
consequence.

\begin{lemma}[Separator property]\label{lem:tw-separator}
Let $(T, \{X_i\})$ be a tree decomposition of $G$ and let $i$ be a node of $T$.
Removing $i$ from $T$ leaves several subtrees. If $v$ and $w$ are vertices
outside $X_i$ occurring in bags of two different subtrees, then every path from
$v$ to $w$ in $G$ passes through a vertex of $X_i$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
It is enough to prove that $v$ and $w$ are not adjacent, and then apply that to
each edge of the path in turn.

Suppose $\{v,w\} \in E$. By condition~(ii) some bag $X_j$ contains both. The
node $j$ lies in one of the subtrees, say the one containing $v$'s bags. Then
$w \in X_j$, so the subtree of bags containing $w$ meets that subtree; but by
assumption it also meets a different one, and by condition~(iii) it is
connected, so it must pass through the node $i$ --- that is, $w \in X_i$,
contradicting the assumption.

For a general path $v = u_0, u_1, \ldots, u_r = w$, let $u_s$ be the first
vertex on it lying in a bag outside $v$'s subtree or in $X_i$. If $u_s \in X_i$
we are done; otherwise $u_{s-1}$ and $u_s$ are adjacent and lie in different
subtrees, which the previous paragraph forbids.
\end{proof}

\begin{intuition}
Deleting a bag from the tree and its vertices from the graph splits both at
once: the graph falls apart into pieces, and each piece inherits a tree
decomposition from one of the subtrees. That is precisely the situation the
four operations of \cref{sec:tw-four-operations} exploit, and it is why a
bounded-width decomposition makes dynamic programming possible: the bag is the
interface, and it has at most $k+1$ vertices.
\end{intuition}

Now two consequences, both of which are pool exercises.

\begin{proposition}[Cliques live in one bag]\label{prop:tw-clique}
Let $C$ be a clique of $G$. Then every tree decomposition of $G$ has a bag $X_i$
with $C \subseteq X_i$. Consequently $\tw(G) \ge |C| - 1$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For each $v \in C$ let $T_v$ be the subtree of nodes whose bags contain $v$; by
condition~(iii) it is a subtree, and by condition~(i) it is non-empty. For any
two $v,w \in C$ the edge $\{v,w\}$ lies in some bag, so $T_v$ and $T_w$
intersect. Subtrees of a tree have the \emph{Helly property}: a family of
pairwise intersecting subtrees has a node common to all of them. Hence some node
$i$ lies in $T_v$ for every $v \in C$, which says $C \subseteq X_i$.

The Helly property itself is a short induction. Root $T$ arbitrarily and let
$i_v$ be the node of $T_v$ closest to the root. Take $v$ with $i_v$ deepest.
Every other $T_w$ meets $T_v$, and since $T_w$ contains a node in the subtree
below $i_v$ and its own topmost node $i_w$ is no deeper than $i_v$, it contains
$i_v$ as well.

Finally the bag containing $C$ has size at least $|C|$, so the width of the
decomposition is at least $|C|-1$, and this holds for every decomposition.
\end{proof}

This is the standard way of proving a lower bound on treewidth: exhibit a large
clique. It is also the reason a graph on $n$ vertices can have treewidth
$n-1$ --- the complete graph does, and nothing has more.

\begin{proposition}[Grids]\label{prop:tw-grid}
Let $GR_{p \times q}$ be the $p \times q$ grid graph, with vertices $v_{i,j}$
for $1 \le i \le p$ and $1 \le j \le q$, and edges between vertically and
horizontally consecutive vertices. If $p \le q$ then
$\tw(GR_{p \times q}) \le p$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Sweep a window of $p+1$ vertices across the grid in column-major order. Number
the vertices $u_1, u_2, \ldots, u_{pq}$ column by column, so that
$u_{(j-1)p + i} = v_{i,j}$, and take a path of $pq - p$ nodes with bags
\[ X_t = \{u_t, u_{t+1}, \ldots, u_{t+p}\}, \qquad t = 1, \ldots, pq-p. \]
Each bag has $p+1$ vertices, so the width is $p$.

The conditions hold. Every vertex is in some bag, and the bags containing a
fixed $u_t$ are those with index in $[t-p, t]$, a contiguous stretch of the
path, hence connected. For the edges: a vertical edge joins $u_t$ and $u_{t+1}$,
which lie together in $X_t$; a horizontal edge joins $v_{i,j}$ and $v_{i,j+1}$,
that is $u_t$ and $u_{t+p}$, which lie together in $X_t$ as well. Every edge of
the grid is of one of these two kinds.
\end{proof}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\figplaceholder{4.5cm}{A $4 \times 6$ grid with the sweeping window of five
vertices drawn as a shaded band cutting across it in three successive
positions.}
\caption{The window of $p+1$ consecutive vertices sweeping across a $p \times q$
grid. At every moment it separates the part already passed from the part still
ahead.}
\label{fig:tw-grid}
\end{figure}

The bound is tight --- the $p \times q$ grid has treewidth exactly $\min(p,q)$
--- but the lower bound is harder and we do not prove it. It matters more than
it looks: grids are the canonical graphs of large treewidth, and
\cref{sec:tw-minors} makes that precise.

\section{Nice tree decompositions}
\label{sec:nice-decompositions}
%==============================================================================

An arbitrary tree decomposition is awkward to program against: a node may have
any number of children and its bag may differ from its children's bags in any
way at all. It is convenient to insist that each node does exactly one of the
four things of \cref{sec:tw-four-operations}, and it costs nothing to do so.

\begin{definition}[Nice tree decomposition]\label{def:nice-decomposition}
A \emph{nice tree decomposition} is a rooted tree decomposition in which every
node $i$ is of one of four types:
\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.2em,style=nextline,nosep]
  \item[Leaf:] a leaf of $T$ with $|X_i| = 1$.
  \item[Introduce:] one child $j$, with $X_i = X_j \cup \{v\}$ for a vertex
        $v \notin X_j$.
  \item[Forget:] one child $j$, with $X_i = X_j \setminus \{v\}$ for a vertex
        $v \in X_j$.
  \item[Join:] two children $j, j'$, with $X_i = X_j = X_{j'}$.
\end{description}
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:nice-decomposition}
Every tree decomposition of width $k$ with $n$ nodes can be transformed in
$\bigoh{n}$ time into a nice tree decomposition of the same width, with
$\bigoh{kn}$ nodes.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Construction]
Five steps, each preserving the three conditions of
\cref{def:tree-decomposition} and never enlarging a bag.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Choose an arbitrary node as the root.
  \item Give every node at most two children. A node $i$ with children
        $j_1,\ldots,j_q$ and $q > 2$ is replaced by a binary caterpillar of
        $q-1$ copies of $i$, all with the bag $X_i$, each carrying one of the
        $j_t$ as its other child.
  \item Every node with exactly two children now has the same bag as its two
        copies, so it is already a join node.
  \item A node $i$ with one child $j$ is replaced by a path: first forget, one
        at a time, the vertices of $X_j \setminus X_i$, then introduce, one at
        a time, the vertices of $X_i \setminus X_j$. Every intermediate bag is
        contained in $X_i \cup X_j$ --- in fact in $X_j$ during the forgetting
        and in $X_i$ during the introducing --- so no bag grows.
  \item A leaf with bag $\{v_1,\ldots,v_r\}$ is replaced by a path of introduce
        nodes below it, starting from the bag $\{v_1\}$.
\end{enumerate}
Each step adds $\bigoh{k}$ nodes per original node, giving $\bigoh{kn}$ in
total, and each is a linear scan.
\end{proof}

forget, join.}

\section{Dynamic programming over a tree decomposition}
\label{sec:tw-dp}
%==============================================================================

Now the payoff. The recipe is always the same, and it is worth stating before
the example so that the example reads as an instance of it rather than as a
trick.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \emph{Find a tree decomposition} of width bounded by some small $k$ ---
        either because the graphs in hand are known to have small treewidth
        (\cref{sec:tw-graph-classes}), or by running an algorithm or heuristic
        (\cref{sec:computing-treewidth}). Make it nice.
  \item \emph{Decide what a partial solution must remember about a bag.} This
        is the creative step, and it determines the size of the tables and
        hence the running time.
  \item \emph{Give the four rules}, one per node type, and prove each correct.
  \item \emph{Read the answer off the root}, whose bag is empty.
\end{enumerate}

For independent set, step~2 is answered by a subset of the bag: which of its
vertices are in the solution. For dominating set it is not, and that is why the
following is the better example.

\begin{probdef}{Weighted Dominating Set}
  \instance A graph $G = (V,E)$ with vertex weights $\omega : V \to \N$.
  \question A set $S \subseteq V$ of minimum total weight such that every
  vertex is in $S$ or adjacent to a vertex of $S$.
\end{probdef}

The complication is that a vertex may be dominated by a neighbour that has not
been looked at yet. On a tree this already shows up, and it is resolved by
giving each vertex three states rather than two: let $A_I[v]$ be the minimum
weight of a dominating set of $T[v]$ containing $v$; $A_D[v]$ the minimum weight
of a set that dominates all of $T[v]$ without containing $v$; and $A_U[v]$ the
minimum weight of a set dominating $T[v] \setminus \{v\}$, leaving the fate of
$v$ to the parent. The recurrences are
\begin{align*}
  A_I[v] &= \omega(v) + \sum_{c \in \mathrm{ch}(v)} A_U[c], \\
  A_U[v] &= \sum_{c \in \mathrm{ch}(v)} \min\bigl(A_I[c], A_D[c]\bigr), \\
  A_D[v] &= \min_{c \in \mathrm{ch}(v)} \Bigl( A_I[c] +
            \!\!\sum_{c' \in \mathrm{ch}(v) \setminus \{c\}}\!\!
            \min\bigl(A_I[c'], A_D[c']\bigr) \Bigr),
\end{align*}
with $A_I[v] = \omega(v)$, $A_U[v] = 0$ and $A_D[v] = \infty$ at a leaf --- the
last because a leaf not in the set has no child to dominate it. The formula for
$A_D$ says: somebody has to dominate $v$, so pick which child does.

\subsection{The same three states on a bag}

Over a tree decomposition, each vertex of the bag independently carries one of
the three states, so the table is indexed by an ordered partition of the bag.

\begin{definition}\label{def:tw-ds-table}
For a node $i$ with bag $X_i$ and a partition of $X_i$ into three sets
$I, D, U$, let $T_i[I,D,U]$ be the minimum weight of a set $S$ of \emph{done}
vertices such that $S \cap X_i = I$, every done vertex outside $U$ is dominated
by $S$, and no vertex of $U$ is required to be dominated yet.
\end{definition}

There are $3^{|X_i|} \le 3^{k+1}$ entries per node --- three states per bag
vertex, not two, which is the whole cost of the complication. The four rules:

\paragraph{Leaf, $X_i = \{v\}$.}
\[ T_i[\{v\},\emptyset,\emptyset] = \omega(v), \qquad
   T_i[\emptyset,\{v\},\emptyset] = \infty, \qquad
   T_i[\emptyset,\emptyset,\{v\}] = 0, \]
exactly as at a leaf of a tree: a single vertex cannot already be dominated by
somebody else.

\paragraph{Introduce $v$, $X_i = X_j \cup \{v\}$.} Three cases by the state of
$v$. If $v \in I$ then $v$ joins the solution and dominates its neighbours in
the bag, so the vertices $Y = N(v) \cap D$ may have been in state $U$ before:
\[ T_i[I,D,U] = T_j[I \setminus \{v\},\, D \setminus Y,\, U \cup Y] +
   \omega(v). \]
If $v \in D$ then $v$ claims to be dominated already, which is only possible if
one of its bag neighbours is in the solution:
\[ T_i[I,D,U] = \begin{cases}
     T_j[I, D \setminus \{v\}, U] & \text{if } N(v) \cap I \ne \emptyset, \\
     \infty & \text{otherwise.}
   \end{cases} \]
If $v \in U$ then nothing changes: $T_i[I,D,U] = T_j[I,D,U \setminus \{v\}]$.

\paragraph{Forget $v$, $X_i = X_j \setminus \{v\}$.} The vertex leaves the bag
and can never be looked at again, so its state must be settled: it is either in
the solution or dominated. State $U$ is not allowed to survive.
\[ T_i[I,D,U] = \min\bigl\{\, T_j[I \cup \{v\}, D, U],\;
                            T_j[I, D \cup \{v\}, U] \,\bigr\}. \]

\paragraph{Join, $X_i = X_j = X_{j'}$.} Both children have solved their side of
the graph. A bag vertex in state $I$ is in both solutions and its weight would
be counted twice; a bag vertex in state $U$ is unresolved on both sides; but a
vertex in state $D$ need only be dominated on \emph{one} of the two sides, so we
must try both ways round. Writing $D = D_j \uplus D_{j'}$ for a partition of $D$
into two disjoint parts,
\[ T_i[I,D,U] \;=\; \min_{D = D_j \uplus D_{j'}}
   \Bigl( T_j[I, D_j, U \cup D_{j'}] + T_{j'}[I, D_{j'}, U \cup D_j] \Bigr)
   \;-\; \omega(I). \]

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:tw-dominating-set}
Given a tree decomposition of width $k$, \lang{Weighted Dominating Set} can be
solved in $\bigoh{4^k \poly(k)\, n}$ time and $\bigoh{3^k \poly(k)\, n}$ space.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Correctness is the four rules above, each an immediate consequence of
\cref{lem:tw-separator}: a vertex that has been forgotten has all its
neighbours among the done vertices and the bag, so its domination status can
never change again.

For the running time, every node but the join nodes does $\bigoh{3^k}$ work.
A join node loops, for each of the $3^{k+1}$ entries, over all partitions of $D$
into two parts. Summing over all entries, the number of (entry, partition) pairs
is the number of ways to give each bag vertex one of the states
$I, U, D_j, D_{j'}$, that is $4^{k+1}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{intuition}
The gap between $3^k$ space and $4^k$ time sits entirely in the join. It looks
unavoidable --- the sum over subsets is a convolution and the naive way to
compute a convolution is to enumerate the pairs --- but it is not:
\citet{VanRooijBodlaenderRossmanith2009} evaluate the join by fast subset
convolution and bring the running time to $\Ostar(3^k)$, matching the space.
And that is the end of the road: \citet{LokshtanovMarxSaurabh2018} show that an
$\bigoh{(3-\varepsilon)^k}$ algorithm for dominating set would refute the strong
exponential time hypothesis. The three states are not an artefact of the
presentation; they are the truth about the problem.
\end{intuition}

\section{Two other characterisations}
\label{sec:tw-characterisations}
%==============================================================================

Treewidth has several equivalent definitions that look nothing like each other,
which is usually the sign of a robust notion. Two are worth knowing, one because
it is how the dynamic programming really works and one because it is how people
think about lower bounds.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Building the graph: $k$-boundary graphs}{Building the graph: k-boundary graphs}}

A \emph{$k$-boundary graph} is a triple $G = (V,E,B)$ with $B \subseteq V$ and
$|B| = k$; the vertices of $B$ are the \emph{boundary}. Four operations build
boundary graphs out of smaller ones:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item take the $1$-boundary graph consisting of a single boundary vertex;
  \item add a new boundary vertex, with edges only to existing boundary
        vertices;
  \item make a boundary vertex normal;
  \item glue two $l$-boundary graphs along their boundaries.
\end{enumerate}
These are the leaf, introduce, forget and join of
\cref{def:nice-decomposition}, in that order, and the correspondence is exact:
the treewidth of $G$ is the least $k$ such that $G$ can be built using only
$l$-boundary graphs with $l \le k+1$. Operation~2 is where the requirement that
new edges go only to the boundary bites, and it is the reason a nice tree
decomposition may introduce a vertex only after all of its future neighbours
are in the bag or still to come.

\subsection{Cops and robber}

The second characterisation replaces the bags by a pursuit game. It is the one
people reach for when they want to \emph{see} a bound rather than prove one.

\begin{definition}[The cops-and-robber game]\label{def:cops-robber}
$k$ cops chase a robber on a graph $G$. Everyone sees everything. In each turn
the cops announce which vertices they will occupy and fly there by helicopter;
the robber, who moves infinitely fast along edges but may not pass through an
occupied vertex, then moves anywhere still reachable. The cops win if they
eventually land on the robber.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[\citet{SeymourThomas1993}]\label{thm:cops-robber}
$k+1$ cops can win on $G$ if and only if $\tw(G) \le k$.
\end{theorem}

We do not prove this; the hard direction is genuinely hard. But it is a useful
way to \emph{estimate} treewidth, because the strategies are concrete. On a
tree two cops suffice: one stands on a vertex, the other on the neighbour into
whose component the robber has fled, and the robber's territory shrinks every
turn. On the $l \times l$ grid, $l$ cops can hold one row and sweep, and $l$
cops are needed --- which recovers the lower bound of \cref{prop:tw-grid}
without any bag-counting.

%==============================================================================
\section{Computing a tree decomposition}
\label{sec:computing-treewidth}
%==============================================================================

Step~1 of the recipe assumed a decomposition of small width was available.
Getting one is a problem in its own right.

\begin{theorem}[\citet{ArnborgCorneilProskurowski1987}]\label{thm:treewidth-npc}
Deciding whether a given graph has treewidth at most $k$, with $k$ part of the
input, is \textrm{NP}-complete.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[\citet{Bodlaender1996}]\label{thm:bodlaender}
For every fixed $k$ there is an algorithm that, in $\bigoh{n}$ time, either
outputs a tree decomposition of $G$ of width at most $k$ or reports that
$\tw(G) > k$.
\end{theorem}

So treewidth is fixed-parameter tractable in itself, and the linear running
time is best possible in $n$. The catch is the constant: it is
$2^{\Theta(k^3)}$, which makes the algorithm unusable for any $k$ one would
actually meet. In practice one uses something else --- exact algorithms that are
fast on the instances that arise, of which the best current one is due to
Tamaki, or simple heuristics such as repeatedly eliminating a vertex of minimum
degree, which give a decomposition with no guarantee but often a good one. For
$k \le 4$ there are direct practical algorithms.

\section{Courcelle's theorem}
\label{sec:courcelle}
%==============================================================================

Every dynamic programming algorithm in this chapter followed the same recipe,
which raises the question of whether the recipe can be automated. It can.

\begin{theorem}[\citet{Courcelle1990}]\label{thm:courcelle}
Every graph property definable in monadic second-order logic can be decided in
time $f(k) \cdot n$ on graphs given with a tree decomposition of width $k$.
\end{theorem}

Monadic second-order logic allows quantification over vertices, edges, sets of
vertices and sets of edges, together with adjacency and incidence. That is
enough to express $3$-colourability ($\exists$ three vertex sets, partitioning
$V$, each independent), Hamiltonicity, dominating set, and essentially every
property in this book. So the theorem says: if a problem can be \emph{stated}
in that language, it is linear-time solvable for fixed treewidth, and no
algorithm needs to be designed at all.

Two warnings, and they are why the rest of the chapter is not redundant. First,
linear time means linear in $n$, not in $k$: the function $f$ is a tower of
exponentials in the size of the formula, and unavoidably so. Second, the
theorem gives existence, not a usable algorithm. The
$\Ostar(3^k)$ dominating set algorithm mentioned in
\cref{thm:tw-dominating-set} is not a consequence of Courcelle's theorem and
could not be: every efficient treewidth algorithm anyone actually runs is
hand-designed.

\section{Graph classes of bounded treewidth}
\label{sec:tw-graph-classes}
%==============================================================================

Treewidth is only useful if the graphs one cares about have small treewidth, or
at least treewidth small enough to beat the trivial algorithm. Three examples,
of increasing strength.

\subsection{Series--parallel graphs}

A \emph{series--parallel graph} is built from single edges by two operations:
placing two such graphs in series, identifying the second terminal of one with
the first terminal of the other, and placing them in parallel, identifying both
terminals. The name comes from electrical circuits, where the two operations are
exactly the two rules for combining resistances, and the tree recording how the
circuit was built is what one evaluates to compute the total resistance.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:tw-series-parallel}
Series--parallel graphs have treewidth at most $2$.
\end{proposition}

The proof is the natural one --- turn the construction tree into a tree
decomposition, with the two terminals of each subgraph in the corresponding bag
--- and is left as \cref{ex:tw-series-parallel}. The same bound holds for
\emph{generalised} series--parallel graphs, which allow trees to grow out of any
vertex. Consequently everything that is linear-time on trees is linear-time on
series--parallel graphs.

\subsection{Graphs of maximum degree three}

Bounded degree does not bound treewidth outright --- there are graphs of degree
three with treewidth $\Theta(n)$ --- but it does push it below $n$ by a
constant factor, and that is already worth an algorithm.

\begin{theorem}[\citet{FominHoie2006}]\label{thm:fomin-hoie}
For every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is an $n_\varepsilon$ such that every graph on
$n > n_\varepsilon$ vertices with maximum degree at most three has pathwidth at
most $(1/6 + \varepsilon)n$, and a path decomposition of that width can be
computed in polynomial time.
\end{theorem}

A \emph{path decomposition} is a tree decomposition whose tree is a path; a nice
path decomposition is a nice tree decomposition without join nodes. Combining
the theorem with \cref{thm:tw-dominating-set} gives an exact exponential-time
algorithm for a problem that has nothing to do with treewidth on the face of
it: dominating set on a cubic graph is solved in
$\bigoh{3^{(1/6+\varepsilon)n}} = \bigoh{1.2010^n}$ time by choosing
$\varepsilon$ small enough. Compare the trivial $\Ostar(2^n)$ of
\cref{ch:exact-i}.

\subsection{Planar graphs}

The most useful of the three bounds, and the one that pays back the work of
\cref{ch:planar}.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:tw-planar}
Every planar graph on $n$ vertices has treewidth $\bigoh{\sqrt n}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By the planar separator theorem of \cref{ch:planar}, every planar graph on $n$
vertices has a separator of at most $2\sqrt{2n}$ vertices whose removal leaves
components of at most $2n/3$ vertices each. Let $T(n)$ be the largest treewidth
of a planar graph on $n$ vertices; we show $T(n) \le 100\sqrt n$ by induction.

For small $n$ the claim holds outright, since $T(n) \le n-1 \le 100\sqrt n$ for
$n$ up to $10{,}000$. For larger $n$, take the separator $S$, decompose each
component recursively, and add $S$ to every bag: this is a tree decomposition
of the whole graph, and its width is at most $|S| + T(2n/3)$. Hence
\[ T(n) \;\le\; 2\sqrt{2n} + T(2n/3) \;\le\; 2\sqrt{2n} + 100\sqrt{2n/3}
        \;\le\; 100\sqrt{n}, \]
the last step because $2\sqrt2 + 100\sqrt{2/3} \approx 2.83 + 81.6 < 100$.
\end{proof}

With \cref{sec:tw-dp} this gives $2^{\bigoh{\sqrt n}}$ algorithms for
independent set, dominating set and much else on planar graphs --- which is
where \cref{ch:planar} left the matter, now with the algorithm supplied.

\section{Minors and the grid minor theorem}
\label{sec:tw-minors}
%==============================================================================

One last property, and it is the one that makes treewidth a structural notion
rather than an algorithmic convenience.

\begin{definition}[Minor]\label{def:minor}
A graph $H$ is a \emph{minor} of $G$ if $H$ can be obtained from $G$ by deleting
vertices, deleting edges and contracting edges.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:tw-minor-monotone}
If $H$ is a minor of $G$ then $\tw(H) \le \tw(G)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
It suffices to check the three operations. Deleting a vertex $v$: remove $v$
from every bag. Deleting an edge: change nothing. Contracting $\{u,v\}$ into a
new vertex $x$: replace $u$ and $v$ by $x$ in every bag containing either. The
first two conditions are immediate; for the third, the subtrees of $u$ and of
$v$ both contain a bag holding the edge $\{u,v\}$, so they overlap, and their
union is connected. No bag grows.
\end{proof}

So a graph containing a large grid as a minor has large treewidth, by
\cref{prop:tw-grid}. The remarkable fact is the converse.

\begin{theorem}[Grid minor theorem, \citet{RobertsonSeymour1986}]
\label{thm:grid-minor}
For every $l$, every planar graph either contains the $l \times l$ grid as a
minor or has treewidth at most $5l$. Moreover a minor model or a tree
decomposition can be found in polynomial time. For general graphs, a graph with
no $l \times l$ grid minor has treewidth $l^{\bigoh{1}}$.
\end{theorem}

That is a genuine dichotomy: either the graph is tree-like, or it contains a
grid, and grids are the only obstruction. It generalises
\cref{thm:tw-planar}, since an $l \times l$ grid minor of a planar graph needs
$l^2 \le n$ vertices.

\begin{example}[Planar $k$-path]\label{ex:planar-k-path}
Does a planar graph contain a path on $k$ vertices? Note that a $k$-path
survives taking minors --- if $H$ is a minor of $G$ and $H$ has a $k$-path, so
does $G$ --- and that an $l \times l$ grid contains a path on $l^2$ vertices,
snaking up and down its columns. So if $\tw(G) > 5\sqrt k$ then by
\cref{thm:grid-minor} the graph has a $\sqrt k \times \sqrt k$ grid minor,
hence a $k$-path, and the answer is yes without further work. Otherwise the
treewidth is $\bigoh{\sqrt k}$ and dynamic programming settles it. The result is
an algorithm running in $k^{\bigoh{\sqrt k}} n^{\bigoh{1}}$ time.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Planar $k$-dominating set]\label{ex:planar-k-dominating}
The same argument with the inequality reversed. A graph containing an
$l \times l$ grid minor has no dominating set smaller than $l^2/5$, since a
vertex of the grid dominates at most five others. So if $\tw(G) > 5\cdot
5\sqrt k$ the answer is no, and otherwise the treewidth is $\bigoh{\sqrt k}$
and \cref{thm:tw-dominating-set} finishes in $2^{\bigoh{\sqrt k}} n^{\bigoh{1}}$
time.
\end{example}

The pattern in both examples --- either the treewidth is small and we compute,
or it is large and the answer is forced --- is called \emph{bidimensionality},
and it is the standard route to subexponential parameterized algorithms on
planar graphs.

\section{Notes and further reading}
\label{sec:tw-further-reading}
%==============================================================================

This chapter follows Johan van Rooij's lecture slides.
\citet[Chapter~7]{CyganEtAl2015} is the modern textbook treatment of dynamic
programming over tree decompositions, and \citet[Chapter~11]{CyganEtAl2015}
covers bidimensionality.

\Cref{thm:treewidth-npc} is \citet{ArnborgCorneilProskurowski1987} and
\cref{thm:bodlaender} is \citet{Bodlaender1996}.
\Cref{thm:courcelle} is \citet{Courcelle1990} and
\cref{thm:cops-robber} is \citet{SeymourThomas1993}. The
$\bigoh{4^k n}$ dominating set algorithm is
\citet{AlberBodlaenderFernauKloksNiedermeier2002}, improved to $\Ostar(3^k)$ by
\citet{VanRooijBodlaenderRossmanith2009} and shown optimal under the strong
exponential time hypothesis by \citet{LokshtanovMarxSaurabh2018}.
\Cref{thm:fomin-hoie} is \citet{FominHoie2006}.
\Cref{thm:grid-minor} is \citet{RobertsonSeymour1986}; the bound for general
graphs was exponential in $l$ until \citet{ChekuriChuzhoy2016} made it
polynomial.

The lecture closes with a bonus discussion, not examined and not reproduced
here, of how fast \lang{Hamiltonian Cycle} can be solved on graphs of small
treewidth. The naive dynamic program remembers a matching on the bag and costs
$k^{\bigoh{k}}$; the matchings connectivity matrix turns out to have rank
$2^{k/2-1}$ over $\mathrm{GF}(2)$, which brings the running time down to
single-exponential.

\section{Exercises}
\label{sec:ex-treewidth}
%==============================================================================

\begin{exercise}\label{ex:tw-series-parallel}
Prove \cref{prop:tw-series-parallel}: series--parallel graphs have treewidth at
most $2$. Use the construction tree, and put the two terminals of each subgraph
into the corresponding bag.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Determine the treewidth of the complete bipartite graph $K_{2,n}$, and of the
$n$-vertex wheel.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Show that treewidth does not increase when an edge is subdivided, and give a
graph whose treewidth strictly increases when an edge is added.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Write out the four rules of \cref{sec:tw-dp} for \lang{Maximum Weight
Independent Set}, and show that the resulting algorithm runs in
$\bigoh{2^k k n}$ time. Why is the join node easy here and hard for dominating
set?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Give the four rules for $3$-colouring over a nice tree decomposition, and
analyse the running time.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
In \cref{lem:nice-decomposition}, step~2 duplicates a node and gives both
copies the same bag. Check that condition~(iii) of
\cref{def:tree-decomposition} survives this, and explain what would go wrong if
the copies were given different bags.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Show that a graph of treewidth $k$ has a vertex of degree at most $k$, and
conclude that it has at most $kn$ edges. Hint: look at a leaf bag of a
decomposition in which no bag contains another.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Give a winning strategy for three cops on any planar graph on at most $9$
vertices, or find a $9$-vertex planar graph where three cops lose.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\Cref{ex:planar-k-dominating} uses that a vertex of a grid dominates at most
five vertices including itself. Where exactly does the constant $5$ enter the
running time, and what does the argument give for planar
$k$-\lang{Independent Set}?
\end{exercise}
