\chapter{The Open Exam Question Pool}
\label{app:pool}

The course is examined with the
\href{https://openpoolexams.science.uu.nl/}{open pool exam method}. At least
$30\%$ of the questions of the midterm and of the final exam are drawn
\emph{verbatim} from the pool reproduced below. The remaining questions test the
same material on instances you have not seen.

The pool and the learning goals of this book are the same list, written twice. A
learning goal marked \pooltag{} at the head of a chapter is an item of one of the
questions below, and can be set on the exam word for word. Nothing in the pool
is unreachable from the book, and nothing in the book that is examined is
missing from the pool.

The pool is a draft and may change; the version transcribed here is the one in
\texttt{material/pool/open-pool-questions.pdf}, with the amendments recorded in
\cref{sec:pool-amendments}.

\section{Amendments}
\label{sec:pool-amendments}
%==============================================================================

The pool as circulated was uneven. Some parts of the course were represented by
a list of questions covering everything taught, others by a single exercise ---
a grid to escape, a fleet to phase out --- so that whole topics could be
examined only obliquely, or not at all. The questions titled after their chapter
(\emph{Maximum Flow}, \emph{Minimum Cost Flow}, \emph{Matching},
\emph{Branching}, \emph{Subset Dynamic Programming and Inclusion/Exclusion},
\emph{Approximation Algorithms}, \emph{The Landscape of Approximation
Algorithms}, \emph{Treewidth} and \emph{Complexity for Approximation and FPT})
were added afterwards to restore the balance, stating for those parts the
learning goals that no existing question reached.

Four further changes have been made.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \emph{Stable Matchings} lost its first item, on Euler tours: the course
        no longer covers them.
  \item \emph{Planar Graphs}, item~7 asked for $3$-colouring in
        $2^{O(\sqrt n)}$ time. It now asks for maximum independent set, which
        is what \cref{ch:planar} proves.
  \item \emph{Existential Theory of the Reals} lost items~4 and~6 and gained
        items~7--10; see the remark at the question.
  \item \emph{Duality and geometric $\ER$-hardness} lost items~1--3; see the
        remark at the question.
  \item \emph{Approximation Algorithms} and \emph{The Landscape of Approximation
        Algorithms} were rewritten after the lecture slides were read: the first
        had set cover in the wrong half of the material and the second asked for
        the primal--dual method, which is not taught.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Maximum Flow and Minimum Cut (\cref{ch:max-flow})}

\paragraph{Minimum Vertex Cut.}
Show that determining the maximum flow in a network with arc and vertex capacity
constraints can be reduced to an ordinary maximum-flow problem on a flow network
of comparable size.

\paragraph{Escaping a Grid.}
An $n \times n$ grid is an undirected graph consisting of $n$ rows and $n$
columns of vertices. We denote the vertex in the $i$th row and $j$th column by
$v_{i,j}$. All vertices in a grid have exactly four neighbours, except for the
\emph{boundary} points, which are the vertices $v_{i,j}$ with $i = 1$, $i = n$,
$j = 1$ or $j = n$.

Given $m \le n^2$ starting points $v_{x_1,y_1}, \ldots, v_{x_m,y_m}$ in the
grid, the \emph{grid escape problem} is to determine if there are $m$
vertex disjoint paths from the starting points to any $m$ different points in
the boundary.

Describe an algorithm with running time polynomial in $n$ that solves the escape
problem and analyse its running time. (Be careful that $n$ and $m$ describe the
size of the grid and number of starting points, not the number of vertices and
arcs as usual.)

\paragraph{Maximum Flow.}
This set of questions captures the learning goals of the chapter that are not
covered by the two exercises above.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Define flow networks, feasible flows, $s$--$t$ cuts and the value of a
        flow.
  \item Define the residual network and show that augmenting a flow along a
        path in it yields a feasible flow of larger value.
  \item State and prove the max-flow min-cut theorem, and derive from it that
        an integral maximum flow exists when all capacities are integers.
  \item State the Ford--Fulkerson method and explain why its running time
        depends on the capacities rather than on the size of the input.
  \item Define preflows, excess and height functions, state the height
        invariant, and show that a valid height function rules out an
        augmenting path.
  \item State the push and lift operations and verify that each preserves the
        height invariant.
  \item Show that preflow-push performs fewer than $2n^2$ lifts, at most $2nm$
        saturating pushes and $O(n^2m)$ non-saturating pushes, and conclude
        that it runs in $O(n^2m)$ time.
  \item State Goldberg's highest-label rule and show that it runs in $O(n^3)$
        time.
  \item Describe how to find a maximum flow when the arcs also carry lower
        bounds, by reducing first to an admissible circulation and then to two
        ordinary maximum flow computations.
  \item Define the consistent matrix rounding problem, model it as a
        circulation with lower bounds, and explain why a consistent rounding
        always exists.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Minimum Cost Flow (\cref{ch:min-cost-flow})}

\paragraph{Phasing out capital equipment.}
A shipping company wants to phase out a fleet of (homogeneous) general cargo
ships over a period of $p$ years. Its objective is to maximize its cash assets at
the end of the $p$ years by considering the possibility to prematurely selling
ships and temporary replacing them by ships they rent.

The company faces a non-increasing demand for ships: every year, the number of
ships needed is at most the number needed the previous year. Let $d(i)$ be the
number of ships needed in year $i$. The company can possibly have more than
$d(i)$ ships in year $i$, but may not have less than these.

If a ship is sold in year $i$, this yields $s_i$ euros. Each ship the company has
in year $i$ gives $r_i$ euros as profit. If the company has less than $d(i)$
ships in year $i$, it must hire additional ships. This costs $h_i$ in year $i$.
Assume that $h_i > r_i$ for each $i$.

The company wants to meet its commitments (have sufficient ships either owned or
rented each year), and have as much cash assets as possible at the end of the
$p$th year.

Formulate this problem as a minimum cost flow problem.
(Exercise 9.6 from \emph{Network Flows}, Ahuja, Magnanti, Orlin.)

\paragraph{Minimum Cost Flow.}
This set of questions captures the learning goals of the chapter that are not
covered by the exercise above.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Define the minimum cost flow problem and the cost of a flow.
  \item Show that the problem has no optimum when some negative-cost cycle
        consists entirely of arcs of unbounded capacity, and explain how to
        bound the capacities otherwise.
  \item Explain how to convert between the single-source, multiple-source and
        supply/demand formulations.
  \item Show how to transform an instance with negative arc costs into an
        equivalent instance in which all costs are non-negative.
  \item Write both maximum flow and minimum cost flow as linear programs, and
        say what is gained and what is lost by solving them that way.
  \item Show that every circulation is a non-negative linear combination of
        cycles, and that every flow is a non-negative linear combination of
        $s$--$t$ paths and cycles.
  \item Define the residual network with costs.
  \item State the cycle cancelling algorithm and prove that a flow has minimum
        cost if and only if its residual network contains no negative-cost
        cycle.
  \item State the successive shortest paths algorithm and prove it correct.
        Explain what goes wrong if the network has a negative-cost cycle.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Matching (\cref{ch:matching})}

\paragraph{Partition into Cycles.}
Consider the following problem:
\begin{probdef}{Partition into Cycles}
  \instance Undirected graph $G = (V,E)$.
  \question Can we partition $G$ into cycles, i.e., is there a collection of
  simple cycles $C_1, \ldots, C_r$ in $G$, such that each vertex belongs to
  exactly one of the cycles in the collection?
\end{probdef}
Give a polynomial time algorithm that solves this problem.

\paragraph{Matching.}
This set of questions captures the learning goals of the chapter.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Define a matching, and a maximal, maximum and perfect matching, and
        give an example distinguishing the first two.
  \item Show how to find a maximum matching in a bipartite graph using a
        maximum flow algorithm, and explain why the algorithm must return an
        integral flow.
  \item Define $M$-alternating walks, $M$-augmenting paths, $M$-flowers,
        $M$-blossoms and stems.
  \item Show that a matching $M$ is maximum if and only if there is no
        $M$-augmenting path.
  \item Describe the procedure that finds either an $M$-augmenting path or an
        $M$-flower, and explain why a shortest alternating walk never closes an
        even cycle.
  \item Let $B$ be an $M$-blossom in $G$. Show that $G$ has an $M$-augmenting
        path if and only if $G/B$ has an $M/B$-augmenting path.
  \item State Edmonds' algorithm and show that it finds a maximum matching in
        $O(n^2m)$ time.
  \item State Schrijver's algorithm for a perfect matching in a regular
        bipartite graph, prove the invariant that the weights at every vertex
        sum to $d$, and analyse the running time.
  \item Show that a bipartite graph of maximum degree $d$ has an edge colouring
        with $d$ colours, and use this to solve a classroom scheduling problem.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Exact Exponential-Time Algorithms I (\cref{ch:exact-i})}

\paragraph{Exact Algorithm for Exact 3-SAT.}
Suppose we have a set of Boolean variables $x_1, \ldots, x_n$, a set of clauses,
where each clause is a subset of
\[ \{x_1, \ldots, x_n, \lnot x_1, \ldots, \lnot x_n\}, \]
and each clause has at most three literals (elements). In \textsc{Exact 3-Satisfiability}, we want to
determine if there exists a truth assignment such that each clause has
\emph{exactly} one true literal.

For instance, if we have clauses $\{x_1,x_2,x_3\}$, $\{\lnot x_1, x_2, x_4\}$,
then setting $x_1$ to true, $x_2$ to false, $x_3$ to false, and $x_4$ to true is
a solution. Also, setting $x_1$ and $x_2$ to true does not lead to a solution, as
the first clause has two literals that are true.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Show that we can create a reduction rule using polynomial time that
        transforms an instance of \textsc{Exact 3-Satisfiability} into an
        equivalent smaller instance of \textsc{Exact 3-Satisfiability} where all
        clauses have size two or three. (Smaller means, not increasing the number
        of variables or clauses.)
  \item Show that we can create a reduction rule using polynomial time that
        transforms an instance of \textsc{Exact 3-Satisfiability} into an
        equivalent smaller instance of \textsc{Exact 3-Satisfiability} where all
        clauses have size exactly three. (Smaller means, not increasing the
        number of variables or clauses.)
  \item Give an exponential-time algorithm that solves the \textsc{Exact
        3-Satisfiability} problem. Analyse the time of your algorithm. Your
        algorithm should use $\Ostar(c^n)$ time for some $c < 2$.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Bisection Width.}
Consider the following problem.
\begin{probdef}{Bisection Width}
  \instance Graph $G = (V,E)$, with $|V|$ even.
  \goal Find a partition of $V$ into two sets $V_1, V_2$, with
  $|V_1| = |V_2| = |V|/2$, such that the number of edges with one endpoint in
  $V_1$ and one endpoint in $V_2$ is as small as possible.
\end{probdef}
This problem is NP-complete, and an $\Ostar(2^n)$ algorithm is trivial. Find an
efficient exact algorithm for this problem with running time $\Ostar(c^n)$ for
some $c < 2$.

\paragraph{Branching.}
This set of questions captures the learning goals of the chapter that are not
covered by the two exercises above.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Define $O^{*}$ notation and explain why polynomial factors may be
        suppressed.
  \item Explain what a branching algorithm is, define branching vectors and
        branching numbers, and show how to bound the size of the search tree by
        solving the associated recurrence.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Exact Algorithms II: Inclusion--Exclusion (\cref{ch:exact-ii})}

\paragraph{Inclusion/exclusion for set cover.}
Let $U$ be any set of size $m$ ($m = |U|$), and let $F$ be a set of subsets of
$U$. A set cover $C \subseteq F$ is a set of sets that together cover all
elements in $U$, i.e., $\bigcup_{S \in C} S = U$. A minimum set cover is a set
cover using the smallest number of sets from $F$ as possible.

Give an $\Ostar(2^m)$ time and polynomial-space algorithm that counts the number
of minimum set covers of $F$.

\paragraph{Subset Dynamic Programming and Inclusion/Exclusion.}
This set of questions captures the learning goals of the chapter that are not
covered by the exercise above.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Explain what dynamic programming over subsets is, and analyse its time
        and space requirements.
  \item Give the Held--Karp algorithm solving \textsc{TSP} in $O^{*}(2^n)$
        time, and analyse it.
  \item State the inclusion/exclusion principle and explain how it is used to
        trade space for time.
\end{enumerate}

An item asking for the exponential time hypothesis and its strong form used to

\end{remark}

\subsection*{Fine-Grained Lower Bounds (\cref{ch:lower-bounds})}

\paragraph{Dominating Pairs.}
In the \textsc{Dominating Pairs} problem we are given two sets
$A, B \subseteq \{0,1\}^d$ with $|A| = |B| = N$, and must decide whether there
are $a \in A$ and $b \in B$ with $a_i \le b_i$ for every coordinate $i$.

Show that, assuming the strong exponential time hypothesis, \textsc{Dominating
Pairs} has no algorithm running in $O(N^{2-\delta}\,\mathrm{poly}(d))$ time,
for any $\delta > 0$. You may use without proof that \textsc{Orthogonal
Vectors} has no such algorithm.

\paragraph{Fine-Grained Lower Bounds.}
This set of questions captures the learning goals of the chapter that are not
covered by the exercise above.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item State the exponential time hypothesis and the strong exponential time
        hypothesis. Explain how each differs from
        $\mathrm{P} \ne \mathrm{NP}$, and say which of the two is the more
        firmly believed and why.
  \item State the sparsification lemma, and explain what it is needed for in a
        lower bound proof.
  \item Define a linear reduction, and explain why an ordinary polynomial-time
        reduction is not enough to transfer a subexponential lower bound.
  \item Show that, assuming ETH, \textsc{3-Coloring} has no $2^{o(n)}$
        algorithm, where $n$ is the number of vertices.
  \item Show that, assuming ETH, \textsc{Planar 3-Coloring} has no
        $2^{o(\sqrt n)}$ algorithm, and explain why this matches the
        $2^{O(\sqrt n)}$ algorithm for planar graphs.
  \item Define \textsc{Orthogonal Vectors} and prove that SETH implies it has
        no $O(N^{2-\delta}\,\mathrm{poly}(d))$ algorithm.
  \item Define the discrete Fr\'echet distance, give the $O(n^2)$ dynamic
        programming algorithm, and restate the decision problem as reachability
        in a grid of free cells.
  \item Give the coordinate gadget and the vector gadget of the reduction from
        \textsc{Orthogonal Vectors} to the discrete Fr\'echet distance, and
        prove that two vector gadgets are at Fr\'echet distance at most one
        exactly when the two vectors are orthogonal. Explain what the
        separators are for.
  \item State the conditional quadratic lower bounds for edit distance and for
        the longest common subsequence, and explain how the last step of their
        reductions differs from the last step of the Fr\'echet reduction.
  \item Give a problem for which no conditional quadratic lower bound is
        expected, and say what distinguishes it from one for which such a
        bound is known.
  \item State what SETH implies about the base of the exponent in dynamic
        programming over a tree decomposition, for independent set, dominating
        set and $q$-colouring.
  \item State the 3SUM conjecture and the APSP conjecture, and give one
        consequence of each.
  \item Explain what it means for a parameterized problem to have no polynomial
        kernel, name a problem for which this is known, and state the
        hypothesis it rests on.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Approximation Algorithms I (\cref{ch:approx-i})}

\paragraph{Approximation non-symmetric TSP.}
Suppose we have an instance of \textsc{Travelling Salesman Problem} that is not
symmetric but still fulfils the triangle inequality, e.g., battery usage by an
electric car in a mountainous area. We know that for every two cities $i$ and
$j$, we have that $d(i,j) \le 2 d(j,i)$, and of course, $d(j,i) \le 2 d(i,j)$.

Give a $c$-approximation algorithm for this problem that runs in polynomial
time.

\paragraph{Approximation Algorithms.}
This set of questions captures the learning goals of the chapter that are not
covered by the exercise above.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Define the approximation ratio, for both minimisation and maximisation
        problems, and define the class APX.
  \item Give a $2$-approximation algorithm for \textsc{Max SAT} and prove its
        ratio.
  \item Give the $2$-approximation and Christofides' algorithm for metric
        \textsc{TSP}, and prove their ratios.
  \item Describe the general shape of an approximation ratio proof: a quantity
        bounding both $\OPT$ and the value returned by the algorithm.
  \item Give a $2$-approximation algorithm for \textsc{Vertex Cover} and prove
        its ratio.
  \item Formulate the LP relaxation of \textsc{Minimum Weight Vertex Cover},
        give the rounding algorithm, and prove that it is a $2$-approximation.
  \item Explain the gap technique, and use it to show that \textsc{TSP} without
        the triangle inequality admits no $c$-approximation for any constant
        $c$ unless $\mathrm{P} = \mathrm{NP}$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Approximation Algorithms II (\cref{ch:approx-ii})}

\paragraph{Approximating Steiner Tree.}
Let $G = (V,E)$ be an undirected connected graph, let $T \subseteq V$ be a set of
vertices called the \emph{terminals}, and let $N = V \setminus T$ be the
non-terminal vertices. In the \textsc{Steiner Tree} problem, we are asked to
compute a minimum-size set of edges $S \subseteq E$ such that $S$ connects all
vertices in $T$. That is, if we let $V(S)$ be the set of endpoints of edges in
$S$, then $(V(S),S)$ is a tree with $T \subseteq V(S) \subseteq V$ connecting the
vertices in $T$, where it is allowed to use vertices from $N$ as intermediate
vertices in the tree.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Prove that the procedure below is a $2$-approximation to \textsc{Steiner
        Tree}.

        Define the complete graph $G_T = (T,F)$ to be a graph on the terminal
        vertices $T$, with $F = \{\{u,v\} : u \in T, v \in T, u \ne v\}$ and cost
        function $c : F \to \N$, where the cost of edge $\{t_1,t_2\} \in F$
        equals the length of the shortest path from $t_1$ to $t_2$ in the
        original graph $G$. Also, define $P(\{t_1,t_2\})$ to be the set of edges
        underlying such a shortest path from $t_1$ to $t_2$ in $G$.

        Consider the following algorithm for Steiner tree: given $G = (V,E)$ and
        $T$, first compute $G_T$ and a minimum spanning tree $M \subseteq F$ on
        $G_T$ w.r.t.\ the cost function $c$, and then output the edge set
        \[ \bigcup_{m \in M} P(m). \]
        Note that a minimum spanning tree is a minimum cost edge set connecting
        \emph{all} vertices in the graph.
  \item Prove that there is no FPTAS for Steiner tree unless
        $\mathrm{P} = \mathrm{NP}$.

        You do not need part 1 to prove this, and partial points will be awarded
        if you prove the weaker statement that no additive-constant-factor
        approximation exists unless $\mathrm{P} = \mathrm{NP}$.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{The Landscape of Approximation Algorithms.}
This set of questions captures the learning goals of the chapter that are not
covered by the exercise above.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Name the five qualities of polynomial-time approximation --- absolute
        constant difference, FPTAS, PTAS, APX and $f(n)$-APX --- and give an
        example of each.
  \item Give an example of a problem approximable within an absolute constant
        difference, and show that maximum independent set is not one unless
        $\mathrm{P} = \mathrm{NP}$.
  \item Define PTAS and FPTAS, and give an FPTAS for \textsc{Knapsack}: the
        pseudo-polynomial dynamic program, the scaling of the values, the
        choice of the scaling constant, and the proofs of the ratio and the
        running time.
  \item Use the gap technique to show that \textsc{Knapsack} admits no
        absolute constant difference approximation unless
        $\mathrm{P} = \mathrm{NP}$.
  \item Give the PTAS for maximum independent set on planar graphs, and prove

  \item Explain the polynomial-bound technique, and use it to show that maximum
        independent set admits no FPTAS unless $\mathrm{P} = \mathrm{NP}$.
  \item Give the greedy $(\ln n + 1)$-approximation algorithm for

  \item Give the self-reduction from a graph $G$ to $G \times G$ and use it to
        show that maximum independent set is not in APX unless it has a PTAS.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Treewidth (\cref{ch:treewidth})}

\paragraph{Treewidth of grid graphs.}
The $p$ by $q$ grid graph $GR_{p \times q}$ is the graph
$(V_{p \times q}, E_{p \times q})$, with
$V_{p \times q} = \{v_{i,j} \mid 1 \le i \le p \wedge 1 \le j \le q\}$, and
$E_{p \times q} = \{\{v_{i,j}, v_{i+1,j}\} \mid 1 \le i < p \wedge 1 \le j \le q\}
\cup \{\{v_{i,j}, v_{i,j+1}\} \mid 1 \le i \le p \wedge 1 \le j < q\}$.%
\footnote{The pool PDF gives the same expression $\{v_{i,j},v_{i+1,j}\}$ twice;
the second set is clearly meant to contain the horizontal edges, and we have
transcribed it that way.}

Show, by giving a construction of a tree decomposition, that the treewidth of a
$p$ by $q$ grid graph with $p \le q$ is at most $p$.

\paragraph{Treewidth and cliques.}
A clique in a graph $G = (V,E)$ is a subset of the vertices $C \subseteq V$ such
that for any pair of vertices $c_1, c_2 \in C$ there is an edge between the
vertices in $G$: $\{c_1,c_2\} \in E$.

Show that for any tree decomposition $T$ of $G$, $T$ must have a bag $X_i$ for
which $C \subseteq X_i$. Conclude that the treewidth of $G$ is at least
$|C| - 1$.

\paragraph{Treewidth.}
This set of questions captures the learning goals of the chapter that are not
covered by the two exercises above.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Define a tree decomposition and the treewidth of a graph, and explain
        why the definition subtracts one.
  \item Determine the treewidth of trees, of cycles and of series--parallel
        graphs.
  \item State and prove the separator property of a tree decomposition.
  \item Define a nice tree decomposition and describe the transformation of an
        arbitrary tree decomposition into a nice one of the same width.
  \item Give the dynamic programming algorithm for \textsc{Weighted Dominating
        Set} over a nice tree decomposition --- the three states, the four
        rules, and the analysis of the running time.
  \item State the characterisation of treewidth by $k$-boundary graphs and the
        characterisation by the cops-and-robber game.
  \item Say what is known about computing a tree decomposition: the complexity
        of the problem and what is available in practice.
  \item State Courcelle's theorem, and explain what it does and does not give
        you.
  \item Bound the treewidth of planar graphs using the planar separator
        theorem, and derive a $2^{O(\sqrt n)}$ algorithm from it.
  \item Show that treewidth does not increase under taking minors, state the
        grid minor theorem, and use it to give a subexponential parameterized
        algorithm for a problem on planar graphs.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Hardness of Approximation and Parameterized Complexity (\cref{ch:hardness})}

\paragraph{L-reduction for Maximum Cut on Multigraphs.}
We consider the approximation complexity of the \textsc{Max Cut on Multigraphs}
problem and the \textsc{Max Not-All-Equal 3-SAT} problem.

A multigraph $G = (V,E)$ is a graph that can contain multiple identical edges.
Given a multigraph $G = (V,E)$, a cut $(V_1,V_2)$ in $G$ is a partitioning of
the vertices $V$ into two sets $V_1, V_2$ ($V_1 \cap V_2 = \emptyset$,
$V_1 \cup V_2 = V$). The value of the cut $(V_1,V_2)$ is the number of edges
$(u,v) \in E$ with one endpoint in $V_1$ and one endpoint in $V_2$. In the
\textsc{Max Cut on Multigraphs} problem, we are given a multigraph and we are
asked to compute a cut with maximum value.

In the \textsc{Max Not-All-Equal 3-SAT} problem, we are given a set of variables
$X$ and a set of clauses $C$ over the variables $X$ (clauses that can have
literals $x$ and $\lnot x$ for variables $x \in X$), where each clause consists
of \emph{exactly} two or three literals. In this problem, a clause $c \in C$ is
satisfied by a truth assignment $\varphi$, if at least one literal in $c$ is made
true by $\varphi$ and at least one literal in $c$ is made false by $\varphi$. The
question in this problem is to compute a truth assignment that satisfies the
maximum number of clauses. In this question you may use the fact that
\textsc{Max Not-All-Equal 3-SAT} is APX-complete.

Prove that \textsc{Max Cut on Multigraphs} is APX-hard by giving an L-reduction
from \textsc{Max Not-All-Equal 3-SAT}.

\paragraph{Complexity for Approximation and FPT.}
This set of questions captures the learning goals of the chapter that are not

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Explain what a conditional non-existence proof is, and say what an

  \item Define FPT and XP, state the inclusions
