\chapter{Kernelization}
\label{ch:kernelization}

\Cref{ch:fpt-i} introduced kernelisation as preprocessing with a guarantee, and
produced two kernels by hand: a string of $\bigoh{k^2}$ characters for convex
recolouring, and a graph with $\bigoh{k^2}$ edges for vertex cover. Both came
from staring at the problem until some reduction rules suggested themselves.

This chapter does better on both counts. For vertex cover it replaces the ad hoc
rules by linear programming and gets a kernel of $2k$ \emph{vertices}, which is
best possible up to the constant. For cluster editing it gives a quadratic
kernel, by rules whose correctness is easy and whose \emph{sufficiency} --- the
proof that nothing large survives them --- is the interesting part.

\begin{goals}
  \poolgoal{define \lang{Integer Linear Programming} and show how \lang{Vertex
        Cover} is encoded as one}
  \goalitem{prove that the LP relaxation of \lang{Vertex Cover} is
        half-integral}
  \goalitem{state and prove the Nemhauser--Trotter theorem}
  \poolgoal{show that \lang{Vertex Cover} has a kernel with at most $2k$
        vertices}
  \goalitem{give the reduction rules for \lang{Cluster Editing} and prove that
        they leave at most $3k^2$ vertices}
  \goalitem{say what is known about the problems that have no polynomial kernel
        at all}
\end{goals}

%==============================================================================
\section{What a kernel is, again}
\label{sec:kernel-recap}
%==============================================================================

Recall \cref{def:kernel}: a kernelisation of a parameterized problem is a
polynomial-time algorithm mapping an instance $(I,k)$ to an equivalent instance
$(I',k')$ with $k' \le f(k)$ and $|I'| \le g(k)$. The kernel is
\emph{polynomial} when $g$ is, and by \cref{thm:kernel-iff-fpt} some kernel
always exists for a problem in $\mathrm{FPT}$ --- so the whole interest lies in
how small $g$ can be made.

For vertex cover, \cref{thm:fpt-vc-kernel} gave $g(k) = \bigoh{k^2}$ by two
observations about degrees. We now get $g(k) = 2k$ vertices, and from a
completely different direction.

\section{Vertex cover by linear programming}
\label{sec:nemhauser-trotter}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{The program and its relaxation}

\begin{definition}[Integer linear programming]\label{def:ilp}
An \emph{integer linear program} asks for an assignment of integers to
variables $x_1,\ldots,x_n$ minimising a linear objective
$\sum_i c_i x_i$ subject to finitely many linear inequalities
$\sum_i a_{ji} x_i \ge b_j$. Dropping the requirement that the $x_i$ be
integers gives the \emph{linear relaxation}, which is a linear program.
\end{definition}

Solving an integer linear program is \textrm{NP}-hard --- as it must be, since
we are about to encode an \textrm{NP}-hard problem in one. Solving a linear
program is not: the ellipsoid method does it in polynomial time, and the
simplex algorithm does it fast in practice though not in the worst case.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:vc-ilp}
\lang{Vertex Cover} on $G = (V,E)$ is the integer linear program
\[
\begin{array}{lll}
  \text{minimise}   & \displaystyle\sum_{v \in V} x_v & \\[2pt]
  \text{subject to} & x_u + x_v \ge 1 & \text{for each } \{u,v\} \in E, \\[2pt]
                    & x_v \in \{0,1\} & \text{for each } v \in V.
\end{array}
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Read $x_v = 1$ as ``$v$ is in the cover''. The constraint says every edge has an
endpoint in it, and the objective counts the cover.
\end{proof}

The relaxation replaces $x_v \in \{0,1\}$ by $x_v \ge 0$; the upper bound
$x_v \le 1$ may be dropped, since lowering any $x_v$ to $1$ keeps the solution
feasible and does not increase the cost. Write $z^{*}$ for the optimum of the
relaxation. Since every vertex cover is an integral feasible point,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vc-lp-bound}
  z^{*} \;\le\; \OPT .
\end{equation}
\Cref{ch:approx-i} already used this relaxation, rounding at $1/2$ to get a
$2$-approximation. Here we exploit something stronger about it.

\subsection{Half-integrality}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:half-integral}
The relaxation has an optimal solution with $x_v \in \{0, \tfrac12, 1\}$ for
every $v$, and it can be computed in polynomial time.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $x$ be an optimal solution; as noted above we may assume
$0 \le x_v \le 1$ for every $v$. Suppose some coordinate is not in
$\{0,\frac12,1\}$. For $\varepsilon > 0$ define two points $x^{+}$ and $x^{-}$
by
\[ x^{\pm}_v = \begin{cases}
     x_v \pm \varepsilon & \text{if } \tfrac12 < x_v < 1, \\
     x_v \mp \varepsilon & \text{if } 0 < x_v < \tfrac12, \\
     x_v & \text{if } x_v \in \{0, \tfrac12, 1\}.
   \end{cases} \]
Only the coordinates that are not already half-integral move, which is what
keeps the perturbation inside the box: a coordinate strictly between two of the
values $0$, $\frac12$, $1$ stays between them for small enough $\varepsilon$,
and the coordinates sitting on those values do not move at all. Now take an
edge $\{u,v\}$. If $x_u + x_v > 1$ strictly then a small perturbation cannot
break the constraint. If $x_u + x_v = 1$ then either both are $\frac12$, or one
is $0$ and the other $1$ --- in both cases nothing moves --- or one is strictly
between $\frac12$ and $1$ and the other strictly between $0$ and $\frac12$, in
which case the two perturbations have opposite signs and cancel. So $x^{+}$ and
$x^{-}$ are both feasible.

Now $x = \frac12(x^{+} + x^{-})$, so the two costs average to that of $x$; as
$x$ is optimal, neither can be smaller, so both are optimal too. Increase
$\varepsilon$ until some coordinate reaches $0$, $\frac12$ or $1$, and repeat.
Each round fixes at least one coordinate, so the process stops. (Alternatively,
half-integrality follows from the fact that the constraint matrix of the vertex
cover relaxation is totally unimodular after a standard transformation, and a
half-integral optimum is what the simplex method returns at a vertex of the
polytope.)
\end{proof}

So we may split the vertices into three parts:
\[ X_0 = \{v : x_v = 0\}, \qquad
   X_{1/2} = \{v : x_v = \tfrac12\}, \qquad
   X_1 = \{v : x_v = 1\}. \]
Note at once that $X_0$ is an independent set --- an edge inside it would
violate $x_u + x_v \ge 1$ --- and that every neighbour of a vertex of $X_0$ lies
in $X_1$.

\subsection{The Nemhauser--Trotter theorem}

The relaxation does more than bound the optimum: it \emph{decides} part of the
problem outright.

\begin{theorem}[\citet{NemhauserTrotter1975}]\label{thm:nemhauser-trotter}
There is a minimum vertex cover of $G$ that contains every vertex of $X_1$ and
no vertex of $X_0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $S$ be any minimum vertex cover and put
\[ S^{*} \;=\; \bigl(S \cup X_1\bigr) \setminus X_0 . \]
We show that $S^{*}$ is a vertex cover and that $|S^{*}| \le |S|$; since $S$ is
minimum, $S^{*}$ is then a minimum vertex cover of the required shape.

\emph{$S^{*}$ is a vertex cover.} Let $\{u,v\}$ be an edge and suppose neither
endpoint lies in $S^{*}$. If $u \in X_0$ then $x_u = 0$, so $x_v \ge 1$ and
$v \in X_1$; and $v \notin X_0$; so $v \in S^{*}$, a contradiction. So
$u \notin X_0$, and symmetrically $v \notin X_0$. But then $u \notin S^{*}$ with
$u \notin X_0$ forces $u \notin S \cup X_1$, in particular $u \notin S$, and
likewise $v \notin S$ --- contradicting that $S$ covers $\{u,v\}$.

\emph{$|S^{*}| \le |S|$.} Counting,
\[ |S^{*}| \;=\; |S| + \bigl|X_1 \setminus S\bigr|
                  - \bigl|X_0 \cap S\bigr|, \]
so it suffices to prove $|X_1 \setminus S| \le |X_0 \cap S|$. Suppose not, and
perturb the LP solution: for a small $\varepsilon > 0$ define
\[ y_v = \begin{cases}
     x_v + \varepsilon & \text{if } v \in X_0 \cap S, \\
     x_v - \varepsilon & \text{if } v \in X_1 \setminus S, \\
     x_v & \text{otherwise.}
   \end{cases} \]
The cost changes by $\varepsilon\bigl(|X_0 \cap S| - |X_1 \setminus S|\bigr)$,
which is strictly negative by assumption. So it remains only to check that $y$
is feasible for small $\varepsilon$, which contradicts the optimality of $x$.

Non-negativity is clear, since the only decreases happen on $X_1$, where
$x_v = 1$. For an edge $\{u,v\}$, the sum $y_u + y_v$ can only drop if an
endpoint lies in $X_1 \setminus S$; say $u \in X_1 \setminus S$. Since $u
\notin S$ and $S$ covers the edge, $v \in S$. Now either $x_v = 0$, so
$v \in X_0 \cap S$ and the two perturbations cancel exactly; or $x_v \ge
\frac12$, in which case $x_u + x_v \ge 1 + \frac12 > 1$ and there is slack to
absorb $\varepsilon$. Choosing $\varepsilon$ smaller than every such slack, and
smaller than $\frac12$, makes $y$ feasible.
\end{proof}

\begin{intuition}
This is a stronger statement than it may look. A $2$-approximation, which is
what \cref{ch:approx-i} extracted from the same relaxation, gives a cover that
is good; \cref{thm:nemhauser-trotter} gives \emph{certainty} about part of an
optimal cover. The vertices the relaxation is sure about --- those it sets to
$0$ or $1$ --- it is right about. Only where it hedges, at $\frac12$, is there
anything left to decide.
\end{intuition}

\subsection{The kernel}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:vc-2k-kernel}
\lang{Vertex Cover} has a kernel with at most $2k$ vertices, computable in
polynomial time.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Compute a half-integral optimum $x$ by \cref{lem:half-integral} and let
$z^{*} = |X_1| + \frac12|X_{1/2}|$ be its value.

If $z^{*} > k$, report a no-instance: by \eqref{eq:vc-lp-bound} every vertex
cover has at least $z^{*} > k$ vertices.

Otherwise, by \cref{thm:nemhauser-trotter} some minimum vertex cover takes all
of $X_1$ and none of $X_0$. So put $X_1$ into the solution, delete
$X_0 \cup X_1$ from the graph and set $k' = k - |X_1|$; if $k' < 0$, report a
no-instance. Nothing is lost by the deletion: every edge with an endpoint in
$X_0$ has its other endpoint in $X_1$ and is therefore already covered, and
$X_0$ has no internal edges.

What remains is $G[X_{1/2}]$ with budget $k'$, and
\[ \tfrac12 |X_{1/2}| \;=\; z^{*} - |X_1| \;\le\; k - |X_1| \;=\; k', \]
so $|X_{1/2}| \le 2k' \le 2k$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:kernel-vertices-vs-edges}
The kernel has at most $2k$ \emph{vertices} and may still have a quadratic
number of \emph{edges} --- up to $\binom{2k}{2}$. That is not a defect of the
construction: \cref{ch:lower-bounds} would be the place to ask whether the
number of edges can also be made linear, and the answer is that it cannot,
under the usual assumptions. When a kernel size is quoted, always ask in what
unit.
\end{remark}

Compare the two kernels for the same problem. The elementary rules of
\cref{thm:fpt-vc-kernel} gave $\bigoh{k^2}$ edges and hence $\bigoh{k^2}$
vertices; linear programming gives $2k$ vertices. Feeding the smaller kernel to
the branching algorithm of \cref{alg:fpt-vc} gives
$\bigoh{2^k k^2 + \poly(n)}$ time --- the same shape, with a much smaller
instance to branch on.

\section{A kernel for cluster editing}
\label{sec:cluster-editing-kernel}
%==============================================================================

\Cref{ch:fpt-i} solved \lang{Cluster Editing} by branching on an induced path
on three vertices, in $\bigoh{3^k \poly(n)}$ time. Here is a kernel for the
same problem. Recall the problem: make every connected component a clique using
at most $k$ edge additions and deletions, and recall from
\cref{lem:cluster-p3} that a graph is a \emph{cluster graph} --- every component
a clique --- exactly when it has no induced path on three vertices, which we
write $P_3$.

\begin{observation}\label{obs:cluster-rules}
The following rules are sound.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{R\arabic*.},leftmargin=*]
  \item Delete a connected component that is already a clique. It needs no
        modification and can interact with nothing else.
  \item If $G$ is not a cluster graph and $k \le 0$, report a no-instance.
  \item If $\{v,w\} \in E$ and at least $k+1$ vertices are adjacent to exactly
        one of $v$ and $w$, then delete the edge $\{v,w\}$ and set
        $k \gets k-1$.
  \item If $\{v,w\} \notin E$ and at least $k+1$ vertices are adjacent to both
        $v$ and $w$, then add the edge $\{v,w\}$ and set $k \gets k-1$.
  \item If none of R1--R4 applies and $|V| > 3k^2$, report a no-instance.
\end{enumerate}
\end{observation}

\begin{proof}[Soundness of R3 and R4]
For R3, suppose $\{v,w\}$ is an edge and $u_1,\ldots,u_{k+1}$ are adjacent to
exactly one of $v,w$. Each $u_i$ forms an induced $P_3$ with $v$ and $w$, and
these $k+1$ paths share only the edge $\{v,w\}$. If the solution does not delete
$\{v,w\}$, it must repair each of the $k+1$ paths separately, at a cost of at
least one modification each --- more than the budget. So every solution deletes
$\{v,w\}$.

R4 is the mirror image: if $u_1,\ldots,u_{k+1}$ are adjacent to both $v$ and $w$
and $\{v,w\}$ is absent, then each $u_i$ is the centre of an induced $P_3$
through $v$ and $w$, and repairing them separately costs more than $k$. So every
solution adds $\{v,w\}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:cluster-kernel}
\lang{Cluster Editing} has a kernel with at most $3k^2$ vertices.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Apply R1--R4 exhaustively; each application either removes a component or
decreases $k$, so this terminates in polynomially many rounds. It remains to
justify R5, that is, to show that a yes-instance to which R1--R4 no longer apply
has at most $3k^2$ vertices.

\emph{Every vertex lies in an induced $P_3$.} Suppose $v$ lies in none. Then
$N(v)$ is a clique: two non-adjacent neighbours $u,u'$ of $v$ would give the
induced path $u - v - u'$. And no neighbour of a neighbour of $v$ lies outside
$N[v]$: if $u \in N(v)$ and $w \in N(u) \setminus N[v]$ then $v - u - w$ is an
induced $P_3$. So $N[v]$ is a clique and is a whole connected component, and R1
would have removed it.

\emph{There are at most $k^2$ induced $P_3$'s.} Fix a solution using at most $k$
modifications. Every induced $P_3$ of $G$ must be destroyed by at least one of
them, since the final graph is a cluster graph and has none. A given
modification destroys only a limited number:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item deleting an edge $\{v,w\}$ destroys only induced $P_3$'s containing that
        edge, and these are the paths through a vertex adjacent to exactly one
        of $v,w$; since R3 does not apply, there are at most $k$ of them;
  \item adding an edge $\{v,w\}$ destroys only induced $P_3$'s with endpoints
        $v$ and $w$, whose centres are adjacent to both; since R4 does not
        apply, there are at most $k$ of them.
\end{itemize}
So at most $k$ modifications destroy at most $k \cdot k = k^2$ induced $P_3$'s,
and $G$ has at most that many.

\emph{Conclusion.} Every vertex lies in some induced $P_3$, each $P_3$ has three
vertices, so $|V| \le 3k^2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{intuition}
Notice the shape of the argument, because it is the shape of most kernel size
proofs. The rules themselves are easy to justify: each says ``too many witnesses
force a single decision''. The work is in the \emph{converse} --- showing that
once no rule applies, the instance is small. That direction always argues from a
hypothetical solution: assume one exists, observe that it must account for
every witness in the instance, and bound how many witnesses one modification can
account for. Here the witnesses are induced paths on three vertices, and the
bound is exactly what R3 and R4 were designed to give.
\end{intuition}

Linear kernels for cluster editing are known, with a considerably more involved
analysis, but the quadratic one above is the one to remember: it is short, and
the technique generalises.

\section{The limits of kernelisation}
\label{sec:kernel-limits}
%==============================================================================

Every problem in $\mathrm{FPT}$ has a kernel, by
\cref{thm:kernel-iff-fpt}, and the two problems of this chapter have polynomial
ones. Not every problem does.

\Cref{sec:kernel-lower-bounds} showed that \lang{$k$-Path} --- which is in
$\mathrm{FPT}$, by \cref{ch:randomized} --- has no kernel of size polynomial in
$k$ unless $\mathrm{NP} \subseteq \mathrm{coNP}/\poly$, and hence unless the
polynomial hierarchy collapses. The obstruction is informational: a polynomial
kernel would let one compress many independent instances into too few bits.

So the picture for a problem in $\mathrm{FPT}$ has three levels, and all three
occur: a linear kernel, as for vertex cover; a polynomial kernel and no known
linear one, as for cluster editing; or provably no polynomial kernel at all, as
for $k$-path.

\section{Notes and further reading}
\label{sec:kernelization-further-reading}
%==============================================================================

This chapter follows Hans Bodlaender's lecture slides.
\Cref{thm:nemhauser-trotter} is \citet{NemhauserTrotter1975}, who proved it for
the weighted problem and in the language of vertex packings --- independent sets
--- rather than covers. Its use as a kernelisation is later, and is the standard
example of linear programming used for preprocessing rather than for
approximation.

\citet[Chapter~2]{CyganEtAl2015} is the textbook account of kernelisation,
including the crown decomposition and the sunflower lemma, two further general
techniques not covered here; \citet[Chapter~15]{CyganEtAl2015} covers the lower
bound theory sketched in \cref{sec:kernel-limits}.
\citet{ShamirSharanTsur2002} proved \lang{Cluster Editing} \textrm{NP}-complete;
the quadratic kernel above has since been improved to a linear number of
vertices.

\section{Exercises}
\label{sec:ex-kernelization}
%==============================================================================

\begin{exercise}
Write down the integer linear program for \lang{Independent Set} and for
\lang{Dominating Set}. Is either relaxation half-integral?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
In \cref{lem:half-integral} the upper bound $x_v \le 1$ was dropped as
unnecessary. Check this, and say what would change for a \emph{maximisation}
problem.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Compute the half-integral optimum of the vertex cover relaxation on the complete
graph $K_n$ and on an odd cycle $C_{2t+1}$. What does
\cref{thm:nemhauser-trotter} give in each case, and what does the kernel look
like?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Show that on a bipartite graph the vertex cover relaxation has an integral
optimum, so that \cref{thm:vc-2k-kernel} solves the problem outright. Which
classical theorem is this?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
The proof of \cref{thm:nemhauser-trotter} chooses $\varepsilon$ smaller than
several quantities. List them, and check that each is strictly positive.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Give a graph on which the kernel of \cref{thm:vc-2k-kernel} is strictly smaller
than the kernel of \cref{thm:fpt-vc-kernel}, and one on which they agree.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Rule R3 requires $k+1$ vertices adjacent to exactly one of $v,w$. Show that the
rule becomes unsound with $k$ in place of $k+1$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
In the proof of \cref{thm:cluster-kernel} it is claimed that deleting an edge
destroys only induced $P_3$'s containing that edge. Deleting an edge can also
\emph{create} induced $P_3$'s. Why does that not break the counting?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Combine \cref{thm:cluster-kernel} with the branching algorithm of
\cref{alg:fpt-cluster}. What running time does the combination have, and is it
better than either alone?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
A problem has a kernel with $\bigoh{k^2}$ vertices and is solvable in
$\bigoh{2^n}$ time on an $n$-vertex graph. What running time does kernelising
first give, and for which $k$ is that better than $\bigoh{2^n}$?
\end{exercise}
