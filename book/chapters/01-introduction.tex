\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

A first course in algorithms is mostly good news. It shows that sorting,
shortest paths, spanning trees, string matching and a dozen other problems have
fast algorithms, and it teaches the handful of design principles --- greedy,
divide and conquer, dynamic programming --- that produce them. It usually ends
with the bad news, in the last two weeks: most problems are not like that, and
for the great majority of the problems anyone actually wants to solve, no fast
algorithm is known and none is expected.

This book starts where that ends. Its subject is what a person who needs an
answer does next, and the perhaps surprising fact that there is a great deal to
say. \textrm{NP}-hardness is not the end of a conversation; it is the beginning
of a more interesting one, in which the question stops being ``can this be
solved efficiently'' and becomes ``efficiently in which respect, and at what
price''.

\begin{goals}
  \goalitem{state what is assumed as background and what is not}
  \goalitem{use $O$, $\Theta$, $\Omega$ and in particular $\Ostar$ notation correctly}
  \goalitem{explain informally what a word RAM is and why we usually compute in that model}
  \goalitem{recall the definitions of \textbf{P}, \textbf{NP}, NP-hardness and NP-completeness}
  \goalitem{name the four standard responses to NP-hardness and say when each is appropriate}
\end{goals}

\section{Why a second course}
\label{sec:intro-why}
%==============================================================================

The honest motivation for this material is not that the algorithms in it are
beautiful, although several are. It is that the situation it addresses is the
normal one.

Scheduling, routing, packing, layout, assignment under side constraints,
clustering, and almost every optimisation problem that arises from a real
process is \textrm{NP}-hard, usually for a reason that has nothing to do with
the application and everything to do with a small combinatorial core hiding
inside it. A first algorithms course leaves you able to recognise this and
otherwise empty-handed. That is an uncomfortable place to stand, because the
instance on your desk does not stop existing when you prove that its problem is
hard. Somebody still has to route the vehicles.

So there are two motivations, and they point the same way. The practical one is
that the techniques in Parts~III--V are what people use when the problem is
hard and the answer is due on Friday: branch and reduce, dynamic programming
over subsets, approximation with a guarantee, exploiting the small parameter
that the application happens to supply. It is worth doing the arithmetic once,
because a running time of $\bigoh{1.38^n}$ can look like a curiosity rather
than an algorithm. At $n = 60$ it is about $2 \times 10^{8}$ operations, which
is a second or so; the trivial $2^n$ is about $10^{18}$, which is not a wait
but a refusal. The whole of \cref{ch:exact-i} is spent moving a base from $2$
to $1.38$, and that is why.

The intellectual motivation is the one that makes the subject a subject.
Hardness is a mathematical property of a problem, and it can be measured with
the same precision as anything else. We will prove that certain problems need
exponential time under a stated hypothesis; that others admit a $2$-approximation
but no better unless $\mathrm{P} = \mathrm{NP}$; that a problem which is hard in
general becomes easy the moment one number in the input is small; and, in
Part~II, that a family of geometric problems is not merely hard but hard in a
way that the classical theory cannot express at all. Each of these is a
theorem, not a rule of thumb, and the fact that such theorems exist is the
reason this is a course rather than a collection of tricks.

\section{What hardness is a statement about}
\label{sec:intro-hardness}
%==============================================================================

Everything in this book is organised by a single observation, so it is worth
making it slowly. Take the usual sentence and read it one word at a time:
assuming $\mathrm{P} \ne \mathrm{NP}$, an \textrm{NP}-hard problem has no
\emph{algorithm} that is \emph{correct on every instance}, returns an
\emph{exactly optimal} answer, and runs in \emph{polynomial time}. Formal
definitions of the terms are in \cref{sec:prelim-np}; what matters here is that
the sentence contains three separate promises, and hardness only says that they
cannot all be kept at once.

Give up any one of them and the theorem stops applying. That is not a loophole;
it is the design space, and the parts of this book are its coordinates.

\paragraph{Give up polynomial time.} Keep exact answers on all instances and pay
in running time --- but pay as little as possible. The trivial algorithm for
most of these problems is $\Ostar(2^n)$, and the subject of
\cref{ch:exact-i,ch:exact-ii} is that one can often do better: $\bigoh{1.3803^n}$
for maximum independent set, $\Ostar(2^n)$ for graph colouring, and a variant
of the latter that gives up almost no time in exchange for polynomial space. The base of the exponent is the whole game, and
\cref{ch:lower-bounds} shows that the game has a floor: under the exponential
time hypothesis, some of these bases cannot be improved to $1$, and under its
strong form some cannot be improved at all.

\paragraph{Give up exactness.} Keep polynomial time and return a solution
guaranteed to be within a stated factor of optimal.
\Cref{ch:approx-i,ch:approx-ii} build such algorithms and, just as importantly,
classify how good a guarantee is achievable: some problems admit any factor you
like, some admit one specific factor and no better, and some admit no constant
factor at all. \Cref{ch:hardness} proves the negative half.

\paragraph{Give up generality.} Keep exactness and polynomial dependence on the
input size, and let the cost blow up only in some other quantity --- a
parameter $k$ that is small in the instances you care about, or a structural
measure such as treewidth or planarity. This is Part~V. Its central definition
is that a running time of $f(k)\cdot\poly(n)$ is a completely different animal
from $n^{f(k)}$, and its central negative result is that the difference is
believed to be real.

Randomness is deliberately not on this list. It is a fourth technique and it
runs through \cref{ch:randomized}, but it is not a fourth way out: nobody
expects a randomized polynomial-time algorithm for an \textrm{NP}-hard problem.
What randomness buys is a cheaper version of the other three --- a
$\Ostar((2e)^k)$ algorithm where the deterministic one costs $\Ostar(k^k)$ ---
and that is how the chapter uses it.

There is a fifth response, and this course does not cover it: give up the
guarantee entirely and use a heuristic that usually works. Local search, genetic
algorithms and their relatives are important and are somebody else's course.
The distinction is drawn in \cref{fig:intro-landscape}, which is the picture the
lectures open with.

Part~II sits outside this picture entirely, and it is the part of the course
that surprises people. It asks a prior question: what \emph{is} an algorithm,
when the input is a set of real numbers? A computer that stores real numbers
exactly and compares them in one step is not a Turing machine, and the problems
that are complete for it --- deciding whether a system of polynomial
inequalities has a real solution, and a growing list of natural geometric
problems that turn out to be equivalent to it --- are not known to be in
\textrm{NP} at all. The classical theory does not say they are hard; it says it
cannot see them.

\section{The five parts}
\label{sec:intro-map}
%==============================================================================

With that in hand, the shape of the book is short to describe. Each part
answers one question, and the parts are largely independent of one another
after the first.

\paragraph{Part I: combinatorial algorithms.}
The part where the news is still good. Maximum flow, minimum cost flow,
matching and planar graphs are the classical polynomial-time theory, and they
are here for two reasons. They are worth knowing: flows and matchings are the
two most reusable modelling tools in the subject, and every one of these
chapters ends by turning a problem that does not look like a flow problem into
one. And they are used later: the planar separator theorem of
\cref{ch:planar} is what bounds the treewidth of a planar graph in
\cref{ch:treewidth}, and the matching algorithm of \cref{ch:matching} is a
subroutine of Christofides' algorithm in \cref{ch:approx-i}. It also opens with
the cleanest illustration of what an algorithm is good for besides computing:
in \cref{ch:stable-marriage} an algorithm is used to prove a theorem that says
nothing about computation.

\paragraph{Part II: real computation and the existential theory of the reals.}
The prior question above, done properly. \Cref{ch:real-models} builds the real
RAM and says what changes; \cref{ch:etr} introduces the class $\ER$ and the
complete problem it is named after; \cref{ch:geometric-er} shows that a series
of geometric problems --- packing convex shapes, drawing graphs with straight
edges, guarding an art gallery --- are $\ER$-complete, which is to say exactly
as hard as the algebra.

\paragraph{Part III: exact exponential-time algorithms and lower bounds.}
Branching and reduction rules, dynamic programming over subsets,
inclusion--exclusion, meeting in the middle: the toolkit for beating $2^n$, and
the analysis techniques that make the improvements provable. Then
\cref{ch:lower-bounds} turns around and proves that the improvements have to
stop, conditionally on the exponential time hypothesis and its strong form ---
including, in one of the more striking results in the book, that a textbook
quadratic algorithm for comparing two curves cannot be improved to
$\bigoh{n^{1.99}}$.

\paragraph{Part IV: approximation algorithms.}
Guarantees, and the machinery for proving them: bounding the optimum you cannot
compute by something you can, and then bounding what the algorithm produces
against the same thing. The part builds up to approximation schemes, where the
guarantee is a knob the user turns, and to the gap technique, which shows when
no such knob can exist.

\paragraph{Part V: parameterized algorithms and complexity.}
The last and largest response. Branching and kernelisation put problems into
$\mathrm{FPT}$; treewidth explains what makes a graph tractable and supplies the
dynamic programming that exploits it; \cref{ch:hardness} supplies the negative
theory for both this part and the previous one; and \cref{ch:randomized} closes
the book with the technique that makes several of these algorithms much
simpler than their deterministic counterparts.

\section{Where the material comes from}
\label{sec:intro-research}
%==============================================================================

A course is a selection, and it is fair to ask who made it and why these topics
rather than others. The answer here is unusually direct. Both authors of this
book are at Utrecht University, in the group that teaches the course, and the
selection is that group's research: a reader who follows the citations will
keep meeting the same department.

The treewidth chapter is the clearest case. That a graph of treewidth $k$ admits
a tree decomposition computable in linear time for fixed $k$ is
\citet{Bodlaender1996}, proved at Utrecht; the fact that a planar graph
on $n$ vertices has treewidth $\bigoh{\sqrt n}$, which
\cref{ch:approx-ii} uses to build a polynomial-time approximation scheme,
appeared first in a 1988 Utrecht technical report \citep{Bodlaender1988}; the
improvement of dominating set over a tree decomposition from $\bigoh{4^k}$ to
$\Ostar(3^k)$ is \citet{VanRooijBodlaenderRossmanith2009}, whose first author
is one of the authors of this book; and the result that
\cref{sec:tw-further-reading} mentions as the reason \lang{Hamiltonian Cycle}
is not as expensive over a tree decomposition as it looks ---
that the matchings connectivity matrix has rank $2^{k/2-1}$ over
$\mathrm{GF}(2)$ --- is \citet{CyganKratschNederlof2018}, whose last author is
at Utrecht and built much of this course. The kernelisation lower bounds that
\cref{ch:lower-bounds} states without proof are
\citet{BodlaenderDowneyFellowsHermelin2009}.

Part~II is the other case, and it is younger. That the art gallery problem is
$\ER$-complete --- that deciding how many guards a polygon needs is exactly as
hard as deciding a system of polynomial inequalities over the reals --- is
\citet{AbrahamsenAdamaszekMiltzow2022}, whose last author is the other author
of this book; and the machine-model characterisation of $\ER$ that
\cref{ch:etr} proves --- the one that makes membership arguments bearable ---
was established for a real RAM close to ours by
\citet{EricksonVanDerHoogMiltzow2024}. Part~II is a third of the course because
the field itself is about ten years old and is being built partly here.

This has a consequence worth stating for anyone considering a thesis. The
distance between the last section of a chapter and an open problem is, in
several places in this book, one conversation. \Cref{ch:geometric-er} lists
problems whose $\ER$-completeness is not known; \cref{ch:lower-bounds} lists
running times that nobody can match with a lower bound; and the notes at the
end of most chapters say where the current record stands, which is another way
of saying where it might move.

\section{How to read this book}
\label{sec:intro-how-to-read}
%==============================================================================

Three features of the book are worth knowing about before rather than after.

\paragraph{The exam pool is the learning goals.}
The course is examined with the
\href{https://openpoolexams.science.uu.nl/}{open pool exam method}: a pool of
questions is published in advance and at least $30\%$ of the questions on the
midterm and the final are taken from it word for word. The pool is reproduced
in \cref{app:pool}. Rather than keep two lists, this book keeps one: the
learning goals at the head of each chapter \emph{are} the pool questions, and a
goal marked \pooltag{} can appear on the exam exactly as written. So the list
at the start of a chapter is not decoration. It is the contract, and everything
needed to satisfy it is in the body of that chapter.

The pool is a floor and not a ceiling. The remaining questions test the same
material on instances you have not seen, which is what the exercises at the end
of each chapter are for; and a section whose heading says
\emph{(non-examinable)} is outside the contract in both directions --- it will
not be examined, and it is there because a note under the heading says why it
earns its place.

\paragraph{Some things are assumed.}
\Cref{app:preliminaries} lists exactly what: notation, graph vocabulary,
asymptotics, the machine model, and the classical complexity classes. It opens
with ten questions that take about fifteen minutes and tell you which of its
sections you need to read --- which for most readers of this book will be one
of them, the $\Ostar$ convention of \cref{sec:prelim-asymptotics}, because it
is specific to the exponential-time literature and is used from
\cref{ch:exact-i} onwards.

Running times quoted as current records will go out of date, and some of them
already have between one edition of the course and the next. Where a chapter
quotes one it says so; treat every such number as a snapshot.

\paragraph{The proofs are the content, and the intuition boxes are not proofs.}
This is a theory course, and an algorithm without its correctness proof and its
analysis is not an answer to anything here. But most arguments in this book have
one idea and a page of bookkeeping, and a reader who has just finished the
bookkeeping is often no wiser about the idea. The passages marked
\emph{Intuition} exist to say, in words and without rigour, what the preceding
argument was really doing and why it had to be done that way. They can be read
on their own, before or after the proof, and skipping them costs nothing on the
exam and quite a lot everywhere else.

Finally: this is a first edition of a book written quickly, and it will contain
mistakes. The preface says how it was made, which is relevant to what kind of
mistakes to expect, and how to report the ones you find. Reporting them is a
real contribution and it is the fastest way to make the next edition better
than this one.
