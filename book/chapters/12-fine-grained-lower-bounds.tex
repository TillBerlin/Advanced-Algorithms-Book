\chapter{Fine-Grained Lower Bounds}
\label{ch:lower-bounds}

\Cref{ch:exact-i,ch:exact-ii} produced a table of running times: $1.3217^n$ for
$3$-colouring, $\Ostar{}(2^n)$ for Hamiltonian cycle, $\Ostar{}(2^{n/2})$ for
subset sum. Every one of them is an upper bound, and every one of them raises
the same question --- is this the truth, or have we simply not been clever
enough? $\mathrm{P} \ne \mathrm{NP}$ does not answer it. That conjecture says
these problems need superpolynomial time; it says nothing whatever about
whether $3$-colouring needs $1.3^n$ or $1.0001^n$ or $2^{\sqrt n}$.

This chapter is about the conjectures that do answer it, and about what follows
from them. Two hypotheses --- the exponential time hypothesis and its strong
form --- imply sharp lower bounds for a long list of concrete problems,
exponential and polynomial alike. The technique is the one from
\textrm{NP}-hardness, with the accounting made exact: it is no longer enough
that the reduction be polynomial, it must not waste the resource we are
measuring.

\begin{goals}
  \poolgoal{state the exponential time hypothesis and the strong exponential
        time hypothesis, and explain how they differ from
        $\mathrm{P} \ne \mathrm{NP}$ and how far each is believed}
  \poolgoal{state the sparsification lemma and explain what it is needed for}
  \poolgoal{explain what a linear reduction is and why an ordinary polynomial

  \poolgoal{show that, assuming ETH, \lang{3-Coloring} has no $2^{o(n)}$

  \poolgoal{show that, assuming ETH, \lang{Planar 3-Coloring} has no

  \poolgoal{define \lang{Orthogonal Vectors} and prove that SETH implies it has
        no $\bigoh{N^{2-\delta}}$ algorithm}
  \poolgoal{define the discrete Fr\'echet distance, give its quadratic
        algorithm, and restate the problem as reachability in a grid of free
        cells}
  \poolgoal{give the coordinate and vector gadgets of the reduction from
        \lang{Orthogonal Vectors} to the discrete Fr\'echet distance, and prove

  \poolgoal{state the conditional quadratic lower bounds for edit distance and

  \poolgoal{give an example of a problem for which no such lower bound is

%==============================================================================
\section{What the hypotheses are for}
\label{sec:eth-motivation}
%==============================================================================

An \textrm{NP}-hardness proof is a coarse instrument. It says a problem has no
polynomial algorithm unless $\mathrm{P} = \mathrm{NP}$, and there it stops. For
almost every question an algorithm designer actually has, that is not enough:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \emph{Is $\Ostar{}(2^n)$ for Hamiltonian cycle the end of the road, or
        should we be looking for $1.5^n$?}
  \item \emph{The dynamic program of \cref{ch:treewidth} carries three states
        per bag vertex and, once its join step is done well, solves dominating
        set in $\Ostar{}(3^k)$ on a tree decomposition of width $k$. Is the $3$
        an artefact of the three states, or is it forced?}
  \item \emph{Edit distance, Fr\'echet distance and longest common subsequence
        all have textbook quadratic algorithms and no better one. Is that a gap
        in our knowledge or a fact about the problems?}
\end{itemize}

None of these is a question about polynomial versus exponential. They are
questions about the constant in the exponent, or about the exponent of a
polynomial, and answering them needs a hypothesis with a constant in it.

\begin{intuition}
It is worth being honest about what is going on here. We are not proving lower
bounds; we are proving that certain algorithms cannot exist \emph{unless} a
particular conjecture is false. The value of this depends entirely on whether
the conjecture is believable, and the case for these two is that they have
survived decades of attention and that a great many independent problems turn
out to rest on them. That is the same standing $\mathrm{P} \ne \mathrm{NP}$
has, one level down.
\end{intuition}

\section{ETH and SETH}
\label{sec:eth-seth}
%==============================================================================

Both hypotheses are about $k$-SAT, and both are stated in terms of one
quantity.

\begin{definition}\label{def:sk}
For $k \ge 3$ let
\[ s_k \;=\; \inf\bigl\{ \delta \;:\; \text{$k$-SAT can be solved in }
   \bigoh{2^{\delta n}} \text{ time on formulas with $n$ variables} \bigr\}. \]
\end{definition}

The trivial algorithm gives $s_k \le 1$ for every $k$, and $s_2 = 0$ because
$2$-SAT is in $\mathrm{P}$. Everything else is conjecture.

\begin{definition}[Exponential time hypothesis]\label{def:eth}
$\mathrm{ETH}$: $s_3 > 0$. Equivalently, there is no algorithm solving $3$-SAT
in $2^{o(n)}$ time.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Strong exponential time hypothesis]\label{def:seth}
$\mathrm{SETH}$: $\lim_{k \to \infty} s_k = 1$. Equivalently, for every
$\delta < 1$ there is a $k$ such that $k$-SAT cannot be solved in
$\bigoh{2^{\delta n}}$ time.
\end{definition}

Note the shape of \cref{def:seth}: it does not say that $3$-SAT needs $2^n$
time --- it does not, the best known algorithms run in about $1.307^n$ --- but
that the savings vanish as the clause width grows. In particular SETH implies
that CNF-SAT with unbounded clause width has no $\bigoh{2^{\delta n}}$
algorithm for any $\delta < 1$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:eth-seth-p-np}
$\mathrm{SETH}$ implies $\mathrm{ETH}$, and $\mathrm{ETH}$ implies
$\mathrm{P} \ne \mathrm{NP}$. Neither converse is known.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For the first implication we show the contrapositive: if ETH fails then so does
SETH. Suppose $s_3 = 0$, so that for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is an
algorithm solving $3$-SAT in $\Ostar(2^{\varepsilon n})$ time, and fix a $k$.
Given a $k$-CNF formula $F$ on $n$ variables, first apply the sparsification
lemma (\cref{thm:sparsification}, proved in the next section) with parameter
$\varepsilon$: it produces at most $2^{\varepsilon n}$ formulas, each a $k$-CNF
on the same $n$ variables with at most $Cn$ clauses, one of which is
satisfiable exactly when $F$ is. In each of them replace every clause of width
$k$ by $k-2$ clauses of width $3$ in the standard way, at the cost of $k-3$
fresh variables per clause. Because there are only $Cn$ clauses, the result is
a $3$-CNF on at most $c_k n$ variables, where $c_k = 1 + C(k-3)$ depends on $k$
but not on the formula. Solving each piece with the $3$-SAT algorithm run at
$\varepsilon/c_k$ costs $\Ostar(2^{\varepsilon n})$, and the whole computation
$\Ostar(2^{2\varepsilon n})$. As $\varepsilon$ was arbitrary, $s_k = 0$; as $k$
was arbitrary, $\lim_k s_k = 0 \ne 1$ and SETH fails.

The constant $c_k$ grows with $k$, and that is unavoidable: nothing here says
that the $s_k$ are bounded by a fixed multiple of $s_3$, and whether faster
$3$-SAT algorithms have any bearing on SETH at all is open.

For the second implication, if $\mathrm{P} = \mathrm{NP}$ then $3$-SAT has a
polynomial-time algorithm, which is in particular a $2^{o(n)}$ algorithm, so
ETH fails.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[How much are they believed?]\label{rem:believing-eth}
Not equally. ETH is widely believed. SETH is genuinely doubted by some
researchers: it is a statement about a limit, it would be refuted by any single
CNF-SAT algorithm running in $\bigoh{1.99^n}$, and such algorithms are known
for restricted classes of formulas. A refutation of SETH would be a major
result rather than a catastrophe --- it would falsify a long list of
conditional lower bounds without touching any algorithm. This is worth saying
plainly, because a conditional lower bound is only as interesting as its
hypothesis, and the two hypotheses in this chapter are not on the same footing.
\end{remark}

\section{Reductions that do not waste}
\label{sec:linear-reductions}
%==============================================================================

Here is the difficulty. \lang{3-Coloring} is \textrm{NP}-hard, so there is a
polynomial reduction from $3$-SAT to it. That alone gives \emph{nothing} about
$2^{o(n)}$ algorithms: if the reduction turns an $n$-variable formula into a
graph on $n^2$ vertices, then a $2^{o(n)}$ algorithm for $3$-colouring gives
only $2^{o(n^2)}$ for $3$-SAT, which ETH does not forbid.

So the reduction has to be measured, not merely bounded.

\begin{definition}[Linear reduction]\label{def:linear-reduction}
A reduction from problem $A$ to problem $B$ is \emph{linear} if it runs in
polynomial time and maps an instance whose parameter of interest is $n$ to an
instance whose parameter of interest is $\bigoh{n}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:linear-transfer}
If there is a linear reduction from $A$ to $B$ and $B$ has a $2^{o(n)}$
algorithm, then so does $A$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Run the reduction, producing an instance of size $cn$ for a constant $c$, and
solve it in $2^{o(cn)} = 2^{o(n)}$ time. The polynomial cost of the reduction
is absorbed.
\end{proof}

There is one obstacle left, and it is the reason the following lemma exists. A
$3$-SAT formula on $n$ variables may have as many as $\Theta(n^3)$ clauses, and
a reduction usually builds a gadget per clause, so the output has size
$\Theta(n^3)$ and is not linear in $n$. The fix is a remarkable theorem saying
that the clauses may as well be few.

\begin{theorem}[Sparsification lemma, \citet{ImpagliazzoPaturiZane2001}]
\label{thm:sparsification}
For every $\varepsilon > 0$ and every $k$ there is a constant
$C = C(k,\varepsilon)$ and an algorithm that, given a $k$-CNF formula $F$ on
$n$ variables, outputs in $\bigoh{2^{\varepsilon n}\poly(n)}$ time formulas
$F_1, \ldots, F_t$ with $t \le 2^{\varepsilon n}$, each a $k$-CNF on the same
$n$ variables and with at most $Cn$ clauses, such that
\[ F \text{ is satisfiable} \iff F_i \text{ is satisfiable for some } i. \]
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:eth-sparse}
$\mathrm{ETH}$ implies that $3$-SAT has no algorithm running in $2^{o(n+m)}$
time, where $m$ is the number of clauses.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Suppose such an algorithm exists and let $\varepsilon > 0$. Apply
\cref{thm:sparsification} and solve each of the at most $2^{\varepsilon n}$
sparse formulas, each of which has $n$ variables and at most $Cn$ clauses, in
$2^{o(n + Cn)} = 2^{o(n)}$ time. The total is
$2^{\varepsilon n} \cdot 2^{o(n)} \le 2^{2\varepsilon n}$ for large $n$. Since
$\varepsilon$ was arbitrary, $s_3 = 0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{intuition}
The sparsification lemma is doing something that sounds impossible: it removes
clauses without changing satisfiability. It does not, of course --- it splits
the formula into many formulas, and the point is that the number of pieces is
subexponential while each piece is sparse. Read it as a licence: \emph{when
proving lower bounds under ETH, you may assume $m = \bigoh{n}$}. Almost every
reduction in this chapter and in the literature uses it in exactly that way,
and almost none uses anything else about it.
\end{intuition}

\section{No subexponential algorithm for 3-colouring}
\label{sec:eth-3col}
%==============================================================================

The first lower bound. \Cref{ch:exact-i} gave a $\bigoh{1.3217^n}$ algorithm
for $3$-colouring, the last in a long chain of improvements; the following says
the chain cannot converge to $1$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:eth-3col}
Assuming $\mathrm{ETH}$, there is no algorithm deciding \lang{3-Coloring} on a
graph with $n$ vertices in $2^{o(n)}$ time. The same holds with $n$ replaced by
the number of edges.
\end{theorem}

The proof is the classical reduction from $3$-SAT, checked for waste. We give
it in full, because the only new content of a fine-grained lower bound is the
counting, and the counting is invisible unless the construction is on the page.

\begin{proof}
Let $F$ be a $3$-CNF formula with variables $x_1,\ldots,x_n$ and clauses
$C_1, \ldots, C_m$. By \cref{cor:eth-sparse} we may assume $m = \bigoh{n}$.
Build a graph $G$ as follows.

\emph{The palette.} Take a triangle on three vertices named $\mathsf{T}$,
$\mathsf{F}$ and $\mathsf{B}$. In any $3$-colouring they receive three
different colours, and we name the colours after them: the colour of
$\mathsf{T}$ is ``true'', that of $\mathsf{F}$ is ``false'', that of
$\mathsf{B}$ is ``base''.

\emph{Variables.} For each variable $x_i$ add two vertices $v_i$ and
$\bar{v}_i$ and make $\{v_i, \bar v_i, \mathsf{B}\}$ a triangle. Then $v_i$ and
$\bar v_i$ get different colours, and neither is the base colour, so one of
them is true and the other false. Reading $v_i$ as $x_i$ and $\bar v_i$ as
$\neg x_i$, a colouring of these vertices is exactly a truth assignment.

\emph{Clauses.} For a clause $C = \ell_1 \vee \ell_2 \vee \ell_3$ add the
six-vertex gadget of \cref{fig:eth-clause-gadget}: two triangles
$\{a_1,a_2,a_3\}$ and $\{b_1,b_2,b_3\}$ joined by the edge $\{a_3,b_1\}$, with
$a_1$ adjacent to the vertex of $\ell_1$, $a_2$ adjacent to the vertex of
$\ell_2$, $b_2$ adjacent to the vertex of $\ell_3$, and $b_3$ adjacent to both
$\mathsf{F}$ and $\mathsf{B}$.

Each triangle is a small or-gadget, and the first one is worth doing by hand.
Its three vertices get three different colours. If $a_3$ is true, then $a_1$
and $a_2$ take the false and the base colour in some order, and whichever of
them is false forces the literal vertex it is adjacent to be true. Conversely
if $\ell_1$ is true, colouring $a_1$ false and $a_2$ base leaves the true
colour free for $a_3$, and symmetrically for $\ell_2$. So $a_3$ can be true
exactly when $\ell_1 \vee \ell_2$ holds; and when both literals are false, $a_1$
and $a_2$ must take the true and the base colour, which forces $a_3$ to be
false.

In the second triangle $b_3$ is adjacent to both $\mathsf{F}$ and $\mathsf{B}$
and so is forced to be true, leaving the false and the base colour for $b_1$
and $b_2$. If $b_1$ is false and $b_2$ base, the only demand is that $a_3$ is
not false; if $b_1$ is base and $b_2$ false, the demands are that $a_3$ is not
base and that $\ell_3$ is not false. When $\ell_1$ or $\ell_2$ is true the
first option is available, because $a_3$ may then be true; when both are false,
$a_3$ is forced to be false and only the second option is left, which needs
$\ell_3$ true. The gadget therefore extends to a $3$-colouring, given the
colours of the three literal vertices, if and only if at least one of them has
the true colour.

\emph{Correctness.} If $F$ has a satisfying assignment, colour the literal
vertices accordingly and extend over each clause gadget, which is possible
because each clause has a true literal. Conversely a $3$-colouring of $G$
assigns each variable a truth value by the colour of $v_i$, and no clause can
be all-false, so the assignment satisfies $F$.

\emph{The counting.} $G$ has $3 + 2n + 6m$ vertices and $\bigoh{n+m}$ edges. As
$m = \bigoh{n}$, both are $\bigoh{n}$: the reduction is linear. So an algorithm
deciding $3$-colouring in $2^{o(|V|)}$ time would decide $3$-SAT in $2^{o(n)}$
time, contradicting ETH by \cref{lem:linear-transfer}. The statement for edges
follows because $|E| = \bigoh{|V|}$ here.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[What the bound does and does not say]\label{rem:eth-3col-scope}
\Cref{thm:eth-3col} rules out $2^{o(n)}$. It says nothing about the base: an
algorithm running in $1.0001^n$ time is not excluded by it, and no known
hypothesis excludes one. Fine-grained lower bounds of the form ``no
$\bigoh{(2-\varepsilon)^n}$'' do exist for some problems --- under SETH, not
ETH --- but $3$-colouring is not among them.

The bound does transfer, and cheaply. Any problem to which \lang{3-Coloring}
reduces linearly inherits it, and \cref{ex:eth-vc,ex:eth-is} ask you to do
this for vertex cover and independent set.
\end{remark}

\section{Two more bounds from ETH}
\label{sec:eth-more}
%==============================================================================

Before turning to SETH, two further consequences of ETH. The first closes a
gap the book has been carrying since \cref{ch:planar}; the second explains a
running time in \cref{ch:fpt-i}.

\subsection{Planar problems and the square root}

\Cref{ch:planar} solved maximum independent set on planar graphs in
$2^{\bigoh{\sqrt n}}$ time by recursively splitting on a separator, and
\cref{ch:treewidth} obtained the same bound through treewidth. Neither said
whether the square root is real. It is.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:eth-planar}
Assuming $\mathrm{ETH}$, there is no algorithm deciding \lang{Planar
3-Coloring} on a planar graph with $n$ vertices in $2^{o(\sqrt n)}$ time. The
same holds for \lang{Independent Set} and \lang{Vertex Cover} on planar
graphs.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Start from the graph $G$ produced by \cref{thm:eth-3col}, which has
$N = \bigoh{n}$ vertices and $\bigoh{n}$ edges and is $3$-colourable if and only
if $F$ is satisfiable.

Draw $G$ in the plane with straight-line edges in general position. Two edges
cross at most once, so the number of crossings is at most
$\binom{|E|}{2} = \bigoh{n^2}$, and such a drawing is found in polynomial
time.

Now remove the crossings. There is a planar \emph{crossover gadget} for
$3$-colouring --- a constant-size planar graph with four terminals, which can be
$3$-coloured exactly when the two opposite pairs of terminals carry equal
colours, so that it transmits two colours across each other without
interference. Replacing every crossing of the drawing by a copy of it yields a
planar graph $G'$ that is $3$-colourable if and only if $G$ is. This is the
construction that proves planar $3$-colourability \textrm{NP}-complete
\citep{GareyJohnsonStockmeyer1976}.

Each crossing costs a constant number of vertices, so
$|V(G')| = \bigoh{n} + \bigoh{n^2} = \bigoh{n^2}$. An algorithm running in
$2^{o(\sqrt{|V(G')|})}$ time therefore decides $3$-colourability of $G$ in
$2^{o(\sqrt{n^2})} = 2^{o(n)}$ time, and hence $3$-SAT in $2^{o(n)}$ time,
contradicting ETH.

For independent set and vertex cover, compose with the linear reductions of
\cref{ex:eth-is,ex:eth-vc}, which preserve planarity.
\end{proof}

\begin{intuition}
The square in $|V(G')| = \bigoh{n^2}$ and the square root in $2^{o(\sqrt n)}$
are the same square. Planarity costs a squaring to achieve, and buys a square
root back in the running time, and the two cancel exactly. That is not a
coincidence: it is the general shape of subexponential parameterized
algorithms, and the reason the exponent $\sqrt n$ appears again and again for
planar problems and essentially never for general ones.
\end{intuition}

\subsection{Clique and the price of the parameter}

\Cref{ch:fpt-i} noted that \lang{Clique} is solvable in $\bigoh{n^{k+1}}$ time
by trying every $k$-set, that it is $\mathrm{W}[1]$-complete, and that
$\mathrm{W}[1]$-hardness is evidence against an $f(k)\,n^{\bigoh{1}}$
algorithm. It is only evidence about the shape of the running time, though; it
says nothing about the exponent. ETH says something about the exponent.

\begin{theorem}[\citet{ChenHuangKanjXia2006}]\label{thm:eth-clique}
Assuming $\mathrm{ETH}$, there is no algorithm deciding whether a graph on $n$
vertices has a clique of size $k$ in time $f(k)\, n^{o(k)}$, for any computable
function $f$.
\end{theorem}

We do not prove this. The consequence is worth stating: the trivial
$\bigoh{n^{k+1}}$ algorithm is essentially optimal, so the gap between
$\mathrm{FPT}$ and $\mathrm{XP}$ that \cref{ch:fpt-i} drew is not merely a
difference in the shape of a formula --- for clique it is a difference nobody
can remove.

\section{Orthogonal vectors}
\label{sec:ov}
%==============================================================================

SETH is a statement about exponential time, and the lower bounds we now want
are about \emph{polynomial} time: we want to say that a problem with a
quadratic algorithm has no $\bigoh{n^{1.99}}$ one. The bridge between the two
is a single problem, which serves the fine-grained world the way $3$-SAT
serves \textrm{NP}-hardness.

\begin{probdef}{Orthogonal Vectors (OV)}
  \instance Two sets $A, B \subseteq \{0,1\}^d$ with $|A| = |B| = N$.
  \question Are there $a \in A$ and $b \in B$ with
  $\sum_{i=1}^{d} a_i b_i = 0$, that is, with no coordinate where both are
  $1$?
\end{probdef}

The trivial algorithm compares every pair, in $\bigoh{N^2 d}$ time. Nothing
substantially better is known when $d$ is, say, $\log^2 N$.

\begin{definition}\label{def:ov-conjecture}
The \emph{OV conjecture} states that for every $\delta > 0$ there is no
algorithm solving \lang{Orthogonal Vectors} in $\bigoh{N^{2-\delta}\poly(d)}$
time.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[\citet{Williams2005}]\label{thm:seth-ov}
$\mathrm{SETH}$ implies the OV conjecture.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $\delta > 0$ and suppose \lang{Orthogonal Vectors} is solvable in
$\bigoh{N^{2-\delta}\poly(d)}$ time. Let $F$ be a CNF formula with $n$
variables and $m$ clauses; we solve it faster than $2^n$.

Split the variables into two halves $X_1$ and $X_2$ of $n/2$ each. For every
one of the $2^{n/2}$ assignments $\alpha$ to $X_1$, build a vector
$a_\alpha \in \{0,1\}^m$ with
\[ a_\alpha[j] = \begin{cases}
     0 & \text{if $\alpha$ already satisfies clause $C_j$,} \\
     1 & \text{otherwise;}
   \end{cases} \]
so a coordinate is $1$ exactly when that clause still needs help. Do the same
for the $2^{n/2}$ assignments $\beta$ to $X_2$, giving vectors $b_\beta$. Let
$A$ and $B$ be the two resulting sets, of size $N = 2^{n/2}$ in dimension
$d = m$.

Now $a_\alpha$ and $b_\beta$ are orthogonal if and only if there is no clause
that both halves fail to satisfy --- that is, if and only if the combined
assignment $\alpha \cup \beta$ satisfies $F$. So $F$ is satisfiable exactly
when $A$ and $B$ contain an orthogonal pair.

Building the vectors costs $\bigoh{2^{n/2}m}$ and the assumed algorithm then
costs
\[ \bigoh{\bigl(2^{n/2}\bigr)^{2-\delta}\poly(m)}
   = \bigoh{2^{(1-\delta/2)n}\poly(m)}. \]
This is an algorithm for CNF-SAT with no bound on the clause width, running in
$\bigoh{2^{\delta' n}\poly(m)}$ for the fixed constant
$\delta' = 1-\delta/2 < 1$. In particular it solves $k$-SAT within that bound
for every $k$, so $s_k \le \delta'$ for all $k$ and
$\lim_k s_k \le \delta' < 1$, contradicting SETH.
\end{proof}

\begin{intuition}
The proof is one idea: \emph{meet in the middle, and let the vector coordinates
be the clauses}. Splitting the variables in half is the standard trick of
\cref{ch:exact-i}, and it converts one exponential search into a quadratic
search over two exponentially large sets. Everything after that is
bookkeeping. What makes OV the right target is that this bookkeeping is
\emph{all} the structure that survives: an OV instance remembers only which
clauses each half-assignment leaves unsatisfied, and it turns out that this is
enough to encode a startling number of geometric and string problems.
\end{intuition}

\section{The Fr\'echet distance}
\label{sec:frechet}
%==============================================================================

Now the geometry. The Fr\'echet distance measures the similarity of two curves,
and it is the standard tool wherever trajectories are compared: matching GPS
tracks to a road network, comparing handwriting, clustering the paths of
migrating birds.

\begin{definition}[Discrete Fr\'echet distance]\label{def:discrete-frechet}
Let $P = (p_1,\ldots,p_{n})$ and $Q = (q_1,\ldots,q_{m})$ be sequences of
points. A \emph{traversal} is a sequence of index pairs starting at $(1,1)$ and
ending at $(n,m)$, in which each step advances the first index, the second
index, or both, by one. Its \emph{cost} is the largest distance
$\lVert p_i - q_j \rVert$ over the pairs it visits, and the \emph{discrete
Fr\'echet distance} $d_{dF}(P,Q)$ is the minimum cost of a traversal.
\end{definition}

The usual image is a person walking along $P$ and a dog along $Q$, joined by a
leash: both may move forward at any speed and neither may go back, and the
Fr\'echet distance is the shortest leash that suffices. The continuous version
lets them stand anywhere on the curve rather than only at vertices.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:frechet-dp}
$d_{dF}(P,Q)$ can be computed in $\bigoh{nm}$ time.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Fill a table $D$, where $D[i][j]$ is the smallest cost of a traversal from
$(1,1)$ to $(i,j)$:
\[ D[i][j] = \max\Bigl\{ \lVert p_i - q_j\rVert, \;
   \min\bigl( D[i-1][j],\, D[i][j-1],\, D[i-1][j-1] \bigr) \Bigr\}, \]
with $D[1][1] = \lVert p_1 - q_1 \rVert$. The answer is $D[n][m]$, and every
entry costs constant time.
\end{proof}

That algorithm is from 1994 and has never been improved beyond logarithmic
factors. The rest of this section says why.

\subsection{The problem as reachability}

The reduction is easier to think about after one change of view, which is
worth making explicit because everything below happens in this picture.

\begin{definition}[Free cell]\label{def:free-cell}
Fix a threshold $\rho > 0$. The cell $(i,j)$ is \emph{free} if
$\lVert p_i - q_j\rVert \le \rho$ and \emph{blocked} otherwise.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:frechet-reachability}
$d_{dF}(P,Q) \le \rho$ if and only if there is a path from $(1,1)$ to $(n,m)$
in the grid $\{1,\ldots,n\} \times \{1,\ldots,m\}$ that uses only free cells
and whose every step is $(+1,0)$, $(0,+1)$ or $(+1,+1)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Such paths are exactly the traversals, and a traversal has cost at most $\rho$
exactly when every pair it visits is free.
\end{proof}

So deciding the Fr\'echet distance is a reachability question in a grid of
free cells, and building a hard instance means designing two point sequences
whose grid of free cells has a monotone path exactly when we want one.

\subsection{The coordinate gadget}

Everything below takes $\rho = 1$ and places all points on a line; distances
are absolute differences. Nothing changes if the points are then embedded in
the plane on the $x$-axis.

Encode a bit of a vector of $A$ and a bit of a vector of $B$ by
\[ \alpha(0) = 0, \quad \alpha(1) = 1, \qquad
   \beta(0) = 0, \quad \beta(1) = -1. \]

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:frechet-coordinate}
For $x,y \in \{0,1\}$ the cell $(\alpha(x), \beta(y))$ is free if and only if
not both $x = 1$ and $y = 1$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The four distances are $|0-0| = 0$, $|1-0| = 1$, $|0-(-1)| = 1$ and
$|1-(-1)| = 2$. Only the last exceeds $1$.
\end{proof}

That is the whole arithmetic content of the reduction: a single pair of points
whose distance detects the logical \textsc{and}. What remains is to force the
traversal to compare the $k$-th coordinate of $a$ with the $k$-th coordinate of
$b$, and nothing else --- and that turns out to be a parity argument.

\subsection{The vector gadget}

\begin{definition}[Vector gadget]\label{def:frechet-vector-gadget}
For $a \in \{0,1\}^d$ put
\[ \mathrm{VG}(a) \;=\; \bigl(\, 3,\; \alpha(a_1),\; 3,\; \alpha(a_2),\; 3,\;
   \ldots,\; 3,\; \alpha(a_d),\; 3 \,\bigr), \]
a sequence of $2d+1$ points that alternates the \emph{separator} $3$ with the
coordinate points; and for $b \in \{0,1\}^d$ define $\mathrm{VG}(b)$ the same
way with $\beta$ in place of $\alpha$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:frechet-vector-gadget}
For all $a,b \in \{0,1\}^d$,
\[ d_{dF}\bigl(\mathrm{VG}(a), \mathrm{VG}(b)\bigr) \le 1
   \iff a \cdot b = 0 . \]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Index both sequences by $1, \ldots, 2d+1$, so that odd indices carry the
separator $3$ and even index $2k$ carries $\alpha(a_k)$ on one side and
$\beta(b_k)$ on the other. There are four kinds of cell, and we compute all
four.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \emph{Odd, odd.} The distance is $|3-3| = 0$: free.
  \item \emph{Odd, even.} The distance is $|3 - \beta(b_l)|$, which is $3$ or
        $4$: blocked.
  \item \emph{Even, odd.} The distance is $|\alpha(a_k) - 3|$, which is $3$ or
        $2$: blocked.
  \item \emph{Even, even.} By \cref{lem:frechet-coordinate}, cell $(2k,2l)$ is
        free if and only if not both $a_k = 1$ and $b_l = 1$.
\end{itemize}
So a cell can be free only if its two indices have the same parity.

Now take any traversal of cost at most $1$, that is, by
\cref{lem:frechet-reachability}, any monotone path of free cells from $(1,1)$
to $(2d+1,2d+1)$. A step of the form $(+1,0)$ or $(0,+1)$ changes exactly one
of the two indices, hence flips exactly one parity, hence lands on a cell whose
indices have different parities --- a blocked cell. So every step of the path
is diagonal, and since the path runs from $(1,1)$ to $(2d+1,2d+1)$ it is
\emph{exactly} the diagonal
\[ (1,1),\ (2,2),\ (3,3),\ \ldots,\ (2d+1,2d+1). \]
This path exists if and only if all of its cells are free. The odd ones always
are; the even one at $(2k,2k)$ is free if and only if not both $a_k = 1$ and
$b_k = 1$. So the traversal exists if and only if there is no coordinate $k$
with $a_k = b_k = 1$, which is to say $a \cdot b = 0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{intuition}
The separator is the whole trick, and it is worth seeing what it does. Without
it, the grid for two vectors would be $d \times d$ with cell $(k,l)$ free

cell by stepping around it --- with $a = b = (0,1,0)$, for instance, the path
$(1,1),(2,1),(3,2),(3,3)$ is free although $a \cdot b = 1$. Interleaving the
separators makes every non-diagonal step land on a blocked cell, so there is
nowhere to dodge to. A gadget that computes the right thing on the diagonal is
easy; a gadget that cannot be circumvented is the work.
\end{intuition}

\subsection{From one pair to all pairs}

\Cref{lem:frechet-vector-gadget} decides one pair. An OV instance asks about
all $N^2$ pairs at once, and the last step of the reduction is to build two
curves whose free grid has a monotone path exactly when \emph{some} pair is
orthogonal.

This is the technical core of the reduction, and unlike the two gadgets above
it is not a paragraph. The difficulty is worth naming, because it is what
separates the Fr\'echet reduction from the string reductions of
\cref{sec:edit-distance}. A traversal must consume both curves \emph{entirely}
and \emph{monotonically}. So one cannot simply concatenate the $N$ gadgets on
each side and hope: the traversal would have to get through all $N$ of the
$b$-gadgets, not one, and it must pay for every point it passes. The
construction therefore has to provide, in addition to the gadgets, a way for
the traversal to move cheaply past the gadgets it is not testing, and to do so
without opening a route that succeeds when no pair is orthogonal.

\begin{lemma}[OR gadget, \citet{Bringmann2014}]\label{lem:frechet-or}
Given $A, B \subseteq \{0,1\}^d$ with $|A| = |B| = N$, one can construct in
$\bigoh{Nd}$ time two sequences $P$ and $Q$ of $\bigoh{Nd}$ points in the
plane, containing the vector gadgets $\mathrm{VG}(a)$ for $a \in A$ and
$\mathrm{VG}(b)$ for $b \in B$ as subsequences, such that
\[ d_{dF}(P,Q) \le 1 \iff \text{there are } a \in A,\ b \in B
   \text{ with } a \cdot b = 0 . \]
\end{lemma}

\begin{theorem}[\citet{Bringmann2014}]\label{thm:frechet}
Assuming $\mathrm{SETH}$, there is no algorithm computing the discrete or the
continuous Fr\'echet distance of two curves with $n$ vertices in the plane in
$\bigoh{n^{2-\delta}}$ time, for any $\delta > 0$. The same holds for computing
a $1.001$-approximation of it.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $\delta > 0$ and suppose the discrete Fr\'echet distance of two $n$-point
curves is computable in $\bigoh{n^{2-\delta}}$ time. Given an OV instance with
$|A| = |B| = N$ and dimension $d$, apply \cref{lem:frechet-or} to get curves on
$n = \bigoh{Nd}$ points, and compute their Fr\'echet distance. By the lemma
this decides the OV instance, and it costs
\[ \bigoh{(Nd)^{2-\delta}} = \bigoh{N^{2-\delta} \poly(d)}, \]
contradicting \cref{thm:seth-ov}, hence SETH. The statements for the continuous
distance and for the approximation are proved by refining the same
construction.
\end{proof}

\begin{intuition}
Notice what \cref{thm:frechet} costs and what it buys. It costs a hypothesis
that is stronger and less certain than $\mathrm{P} \ne \mathrm{NP}$. It buys a
statement about a problem with a perfectly good polynomial algorithm --- and
that is something no classical complexity theory can say at all. This is the
whole programme of fine-grained complexity: give up on unconditional bounds,
fix a small number of hypotheses, and organise the polynomial world by
reductions between concrete problems, in the way the \textrm{NP}-hard world is
organised by reductions to \lang{SAT}.
\end{intuition}

\section{Edit distance and longest common subsequence}
\label{sec:edit-distance}
%==============================================================================

The same hypothesis settles two of the most-used algorithms in computing.

\begin{probdef}{Edit Distance}
  \instance Two strings $x$ and $y$ of length $n$.
  \question The least number of insertions, deletions and substitutions of
  single characters turning $x$ into $y$.
\end{probdef}

\begin{theorem}[\citet{BackursIndyk2018}]\label{thm:edit-distance}
Assuming $\mathrm{SETH}$, there is no algorithm computing the edit distance of
two strings of length $n$ in $\bigoh{n^{2-\delta}}$ time, for any $\delta > 0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[\citet{AbboudBackursVassilevskaWilliams2015}]\label{thm:lcs}
The same holds for the length of a longest common subsequence of two strings of
length $n$, and for dynamic time warping.
\end{theorem}

Both are proved from \lang{Orthogonal Vectors}, on the same three-level plan as
\cref{sec:frechet}: a coordinate gadget, a vector gadget, and then an
\textsc{or} over the $N^2$ pairs. The interesting difference is in the third
level, and it is worth drawing out.

The Fr\'echet distance is a \emph{bottleneck} problem: the cost of a traversal
is a maximum, so the question is whether a route exists at all, and the
\textsc{or} has to be built as reachability. Edit distance and LCS are
\emph{additive}: the cost of an alignment is a sum. That gives the reduction a
different and gentler tool. One arranges the strings so that the optimal
alignment cost is
\[ C - 1 \quad\text{if some pair is orthogonal,} \qquad
   C \quad\text{otherwise,} \]
for a value $C$ fixed by the construction. The \textsc{or} is then a
\emph{minimum} over pairs rather than an existence of a path, and a minimum is
easier to arrange than a route: each pair contributes its own alignment cost,
and the best pair wins automatically. The price is that the construction has to
control the cost of \emph{every} alignment to within $1$, which is why these
proofs are full of carefully padded blocks.

\begin{intuition}
Bottleneck or additive is the first question to ask about a new problem when
looking for an OV reduction. If the objective is a maximum or a minimum over
the steps of a solution, you will be building reachability and the hard part
will be preventing shortcuts. If it is a sum, you will be building an
arithmetic identity and the hard part will be padding.
\end{intuition}

%==============================================================================
\section{When no such bound is expected}
\label{sec:no-lower-bound}
%==============================================================================

A lower bound is only informative against the background of problems that have
none, so it is worth looking at two places where the machinery does not apply
--- and at how little has to change for that to happen.

\subsection{One word away}

\lang{Longest Common Subsequence} asks for the longest string that appears in
both inputs as a subsequence, with gaps allowed. \lang{Longest Common
Substring} asks for the longest string that appears in both as a
\emph{contiguous} block. The two statements differ by one word.

By \cref{thm:lcs}, the first has no strongly subquadratic algorithm under
SETH. The second is solvable in $\bigoh{n}$ time on a constant-size alphabet:
build the suffix tree of $x \# y$ and find the deepest internal node having
descendants from both halves.

The difference is that a common substring is determined by two starting
positions and a length, so there is nothing to search over but positions,
whereas a common subsequence involves a monotone matching between the two
strings --- exactly the structure that the alignment gadgets exploit. When
looking for a conditional lower bound, that monotone matching is the feature to
look for; a problem without one is usually not OV-hard.

\subsection{The dimension is the hypothesis}

\lang{Orthogonal Vectors} itself is the second example, and the caveat is one
students should hear. \Cref{def:ov-conjecture} says there is no
$\bigoh{N^{2-\delta}\poly(d)}$ algorithm --- and it must, because for small $d$
better algorithms are known: when $d = \bigoh{\log N}$ there are algorithms
saving a superpolylogarithmic factor over $N^2$. The conjecture therefore bites
only in the regime $d = \omega(\log N)$, which is exactly the regime the
reduction of \cref{thm:seth-ov} produces, since there $d$ is the number of
clauses.

The moral applies to every reduction in this chapter. A conditional lower bound
inherits the fine print of its hypothesis, and ``no $\bigoh{n^{2-\delta}}$
algorithm'' never means ``no algorithm beats $n^2$'' --- it means no algorithm
beats it by a polynomial factor. Improvements by $\log$ factors, and even by
$2^{\sqrt{\log n}}$ factors, are not excluded and in several cases are known.

%==============================================================================
\section{SETH and dynamic programming over tree decompositions}
\label{sec:seth-treewidth}
%==============================================================================

The dominating set algorithm of \cref{ch:treewidth} carries three states per
bag vertex --- in the solution, dominated, undecided --- so its tables have
$3^k$ entries, and with the join step evaluated by fast subset convolution the
running time is $\Ostar{}(3^k)$ as well. Nothing in that chapter said whether a
cleverer table might carry fewer.

\begin{theorem}[\citet{LokshtanovMarxSaurabh2018}]\label{thm:seth-treewidth}
Assume $\mathrm{SETH}$ and let $\varepsilon > 0$. Then, given a tree
decomposition of width $k$, there is no algorithm solving
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \lang{Independent Set} or \lang{Vertex Cover} in
        $\bigoh{(2-\varepsilon)^k \poly(n)}$ time;
  \item \lang{Dominating Set} in $\bigoh{(3-\varepsilon)^k \poly(n)}$ time;
  \item \lang{$q$-Coloring} in $\bigoh{(q-\varepsilon)^k \poly(n)}$ time.
\end{itemize}
\end{theorem}

So the number of states really is the truth about each problem, and the pattern
is exact: two states for independent set, three for dominating set, $q$ for
$q$-colouring, and in every case the base of the exponent equals the number of
states. \Cref{ch:treewidth} could give the dynamic programs but not this;
without SETH there is no way to say that a table cannot be compressed.

%==============================================================================
\section{The other two hubs: 3SUM and APSP}
\label{sec:3sum-apsp}
%==============================================================================

\lang{Orthogonal Vectors} is one of three problems around which fine-grained
complexity is organised. The other two come with their own conjectures, neither
implied by nor implying SETH, and each has its own family of consequences. A
short tour, without proofs.

\begin{probdef}{3SUM}
  \instance A set $S$ of $n$ integers.
  \question Are there $x,y,z \in S$ with $x + y + z = 0$?
\end{probdef}

Sorting and scanning solves this in $\bigoh{n^2}$ time, and the best known
algorithms shave only subpolynomial factors. The \emph{3SUM conjecture} states
that no $\bigoh{n^{2-\delta}}$ algorithm exists. \citet{GajentaanOvermars1995}
showed that a long list of plane geometry problems are \emph{3SUM-hard} ---
among them: given $n$ points, are three of them collinear; given $n$ triangles,
do they cover a given triangle; given $n$ line segments, can a given polygon be
translated between them. Each of these has an obvious quadratic algorithm and
no better one, and the conjecture explains why.

\begin{probdef}{All-Pairs Shortest Paths (APSP)}
  \instance A directed graph on $n$ vertices with real edge weights.
  \question The distance between every ordered pair of vertices.
\end{probdef}

Floyd--Warshall solves this in $\bigoh{n^3}$ time, and again only
subpolynomial factors have been removed. The \emph{APSP conjecture} states that
no $\bigoh{n^{3-\delta}}$ algorithm exists.
\citet{VassilevskaWilliamsWilliams2018} proved something stronger and more
useful than a list of consequences: a whole family of problems is
\emph{equivalent} to APSP under subcubic reductions --- finding a negative
triangle, computing the radius or the median of a graph, computing the second
shortest path between two vertices, verifying a claimed distance matrix. Any
one of them has a truly subcubic algorithm if and only if all of them do.

\begin{intuition}
Three hubs, three conjectures, three families. The value of the picture is that
it converts a scattering of stubborn problems into a small number of open
questions: instead of asking separately why nobody can beat $n^2$ for edit
distance, for Fr\'echet distance, for collinearity and for $n^3$ for the graph
radius, one asks whether SETH, the 3SUM conjecture and the APSP conjecture are
true. That is exactly the service \textrm{NP}-completeness performed for the
exponential world, and it is why the subject exists.
\end{intuition}

%==============================================================================
\section{Lower bounds on kernels}
\label{sec:kernel-lower-bounds}
%==============================================================================

One last kind of lower bound, on a different resource and under a different
hypothesis. \Cref{thm:kernel-iff-fpt} said that a parameterized problem is in
$\mathrm{FPT}$ if and only if it has a kernel, and observed that the kernel
that argument produces may be astronomically large. The interesting question
is which problems have \emph{polynomial} kernels.

\begin{probdef}{$k$-Path}
  \instance A graph $G$ and an integer $k$.
  \parameter $k$.
  \question Does $G$ contain a simple path on $k$ vertices?
\end{probdef}

\lang{$k$-Path} is in $\mathrm{FPT}$, so by \cref{thm:kernel-iff-fpt} it has a
kernel. It has no polynomial one, unless something implausible happens in
classical complexity theory.

\begin{theorem}[\citet{BodlaenderDowneyFellowsHermelin2009}]
\label{thm:no-poly-kernel}
Unless $\mathrm{NP} \subseteq \mathrm{coNP}/\poly$, and hence unless the
polynomial hierarchy collapses to its third level, \lang{$k$-Path} has no
kernel of size polynomial in $k$.
\end{theorem}

The technique is called \emph{composition}. Suppose we could take $t$ instances
of $k$-path, all with the same parameter $k$, and combine them in polynomial
time into a single instance with parameter $\poly(k)$ that is a yes-instance
exactly when one of the $t$ is --- for $k$-path this is easy, since the
disjoint union does it. A polynomial kernel would then compress $t$ independent
instances into $\poly(k)$ bits, and for large $t$ that is fewer bits than the
instances contain. Making ``fewer bits than the instances contain'' into a
complexity-theoretic contradiction is the content of the theorem.

\begin{intuition}
Note that the hypothesis has changed. ETH and SETH are conjectures about
running times; $\mathrm{NP} \not\subseteq \mathrm{coNP}/\poly$ is a
conjecture about the polynomial hierarchy, of exactly the kind that
$\mathrm{P} \ne \mathrm{NP}$ is. That is not an accident: the resource being
bounded here is \emph{the size of an instance}, not time, and information is
the natural currency for it. When reading a conditional lower bound, always
read the hypothesis first --- it tells you what kind of statement is being
made.
\end{intuition}

\section{Notes and further reading}
\label{sec:lower-bounds-further-reading}
%==============================================================================

ETH and SETH are \citet{ImpagliazzoPaturi2001}; the sparsification lemma is
\citet{ImpagliazzoPaturiZane2001}. \Cref{thm:seth-ov} is due to
\citet{Williams2005}, where it appears as a step in an algorithm rather than as
a hardness result.

\Cref{thm:frechet} is \citet{Bringmann2014}. The coordinate and vector gadgets
of \cref{sec:frechet} are presented here in a stripped-down one-dimensional
form; the paper's construction is more elaborate because it must also handle
the continuous distance and the approximation version.
\Cref{thm:edit-distance} is \citet{BackursIndyk2018} and \cref{thm:lcs} is
\citet{AbboudBackursVassilevskaWilliams2015}.
\Cref{thm:eth-clique} is \citet{ChenHuangKanjXia2006} and
\cref{thm:seth-treewidth} is \citet{LokshtanovMarxSaurabh2018}. The
3SUM-hardness of plane geometry is \citet{GajentaanOvermars1995}; the subcubic
equivalences are \citet{VassilevskaWilliamsWilliams2018}.
\Cref{thm:no-poly-kernel} is \citet{BodlaenderDowneyFellowsHermelin2009}.

\citet[Chapter~14]{CyganEtAl2015} is the textbook account of ETH-based lower
bounds and \citet[Chapter~15]{CyganEtAl2015} of kernel lower bounds; both are
the natural next reading after this chapter.

\section{Exercises}
\label{sec:ex-lower-bounds}
%==============================================================================

\begin{exercise}\label{ex:eth-vc}
Show that ETH implies \lang{Vertex Cover} has no $2^{o(n)}$ algorithm, where
$n$ is the number of vertices. Which reduction do you use, and is it linear?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ex:eth-is}
The standard reduction from $3$-SAT to \lang{Independent Set} builds a triangle
per clause and joins contradictory literals. Check that it is linear, and state
the consequence.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Show that ETH implies that $3$-SAT has no $2^{o(m)}$ algorithm, where $m$ is
the number of clauses. Where is the sparsification lemma used?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
In the proof of \cref{thm:seth-ov} the two sets have equal size because the
variables were split evenly. What running time does the same argument give if
the split is $\gamma n$ and $(1-\gamma)n$, and why is $\gamma = 1/2$ the right
choice?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
In \lang{Dominating Pairs} we are given $A, B \subseteq \{0,1\}^d$ with
$|A| = |B| = N$ and must decide whether there are $a \in A$ and $b \in B$ with
$a_i \le b_i$ for every $i$. Show that, assuming SETH, it has no
$\bigoh{N^{2-\delta}\poly(d)}$ algorithm. Hint: one line.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Verify the claim made in the intuition after
\cref{lem:frechet-vector-gadget}: without the separators, the vectors
$a = b = (0,1,0)$ admit a monotone path of free cells although
$a \cdot b = 1$. Then find, for every $d \ge 3$, a non-orthogonal pair for
which the same happens.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
The separator in \cref{def:frechet-vector-gadget} is the point $3$. Determine
the set of all real numbers that could be used in its place, and check that
$2$ could not.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Suppose the coordinate gadget of \cref{lem:frechet-coordinate} were changed to
$\alpha(1) = 2$, everything else unchanged. Which of the four cells change
status, and what does the vector gadget then compute?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Give an $\bigoh{nm \log(nm)}$ algorithm for the discrete Fr\'echet distance by
binary search over the candidate values together with
\cref{lem:frechet-reachability}, and explain why this does not contradict
\cref{thm:frechet}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Suppose someone announces a $\bigoh{1.99^n}$ algorithm for CNF-SAT. Which
statements in this chapter survive, and which are lost?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\Cref{thm:seth-treewidth} says there is no $\bigoh{(2-\varepsilon)^k}$
algorithm for independent set on a tree decomposition of width $k$. Reconcile
this with the $\bigoh{1.3217^n}$ algorithm for $3$-colouring of
\cref{ch:exact-i}, in which the base of the exponent \emph{is} below the number
of colours.
\end{exercise}
