\chapter{Matching}
\label{ch:matching}

Matching is where the flow machinery finally runs out. In a bipartite graph a
maximum matching is just a maximum flow, and \cref{ch:max-flow} has already
done the work. In a general graph the same idea --- find an augmenting path,
flip it, repeat --- is still \emph{correct}, but the search for the path breaks
down, because an odd cycle can make a walk that looks augmenting fail to be a
path. Edmonds' solution, to contract the offending cycle and recurse, is one of
the most elegant algorithms in the subject, and historically one of the most
important: the paper that introduced it is also where the identification of
``efficient'' with ``polynomial time'' was first argued for in print.

\begin{goals}
  \poolgoal{22.1}{define a matching, and a maximal, maximum and perfect matching, and
        tell the three apart on an example}
  \poolgoal{22.2}{solve maximum matching on bipartite graphs by reduction to maximum
        flow}
  \poolgoal{22.3}{define $M$-alternating walks, $M$-augmenting paths, $M$-flowers,
        $M$-blossoms and stems}
  \poolgoal{22.4}{prove that a matching is maximum if and only if there is no
        $M$-augmenting path}
  \poolgoal{22.5}{describe the procedure that finds either an $M$-augmenting path or
        an $M$-flower, and explain why the even-cycle case cannot arise}
  \poolgoal{22.6}{prove that $G$ has an $M$-augmenting path if and only if $G/B$ has
        an $M/B$-augmenting path, for an $M$-blossom $B$}
  \poolgoal{22.7}{state Edmonds' algorithm and show that it runs in $\bigoh{n^2m}$
        time}
  \poolgoal{22.8}{state Schrijver's algorithm for perfect matchings in regular
        bipartite graphs, prove its invariant, and analyse its running time}
  \poolgoal{22.9}{prove that a bipartite graph of maximum degree $d$ has an edge
        colouring with $d$ colours, and use it to solve a classroom scheduling
        problem}
  \poolgoal{7}{give a polynomial time algorithm that decides whether a graph
        can be partitioned into simple cycles}
\end{goals}

\section{Matchings}
\label{sec:matching-basics}
%==============================================================================

\begin{definition}[Matching]
A \emph{matching} in an undirected graph $G = (V,E)$ is a set $M \subseteq E$
of edges such that no vertex is an endpoint of more than one edge of $M$. A
vertex incident to an edge of $M$ is \emph{matched}, and \emph{unmatched} or
\emph{free} otherwise.
\end{definition}

Three special kinds, and the difference between the first two is the source of
more confusion than any other definition in this book.

\begin{definition}
A matching $M$ is
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{maximal} if $M \cup \{e\}$ is not a matching for any
        $e \in E \setminus M$ --- nothing can be added;
  \item \emph{maximum} if $|M|$ is as large as possible --- nothing is better;
  \item \emph{perfect} if $|M| = n/2$, that is, every vertex is matched.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

Every maximum matching is maximal and every perfect matching is maximum, but
neither converse holds. A maximal matching is a local optimum and a greedy
algorithm finds one in linear time: repeatedly take any edge both of whose
endpoints are still free. A maximum matching is a global optimum and needs this
entire chapter.

\subsection{What we can and cannot do}

For the record, the landscape:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item a \emph{maximal} matching: greedy, linear time;
  \item a \emph{maximum} matching in a bipartite graph: maximum flow
        (\cref{sec:flow-transformations}), and it is the easy case;
  \item a maximum matching in a general graph: polynomial, and the subject of
        \cref{sec:matching-edmonds};
  \item a \emph{perfect} matching: the special case $|M| = n/2$ of the above;
        in a \emph{regular bipartite} graph one exists always
        (\cref{thm:mcf-regular-bipartite}) and can be found faster
        (\cref{sec:matching-schrijver});
  \item a minimum cost perfect or maximum weight matching: also polynomial, by
        more involved arguments; the bipartite case is minimum cost flow
        (\cref{sec:mcf-matching}), and the general case is beyond this book.
\end{itemize}

One striking asymmetry is worth knowing. Finding a perfect matching is
polynomial; \emph{counting} the perfect matchings of a graph is
$\#\mathrm{P}$-complete, and remains so on bipartite graphs. Searching and
counting can differ enormously.

\section{Applications}
\label{sec:matching-applications}
%==============================================================================

Matching is one of those problems that people keep rediscovering.

\paragraph{General graphs.} A chess club with $n$ players must pair them for a
round each week. Some pairs have already played, and the ratings differ; we want
a pairing that avoids repeats and puts players of similar strength together.
Pairing players against players is a matching in a general --- not bipartite ---
graph, which is exactly why the club cannot use the flow model.

\paragraph{Bipartite: assignment.} $n$ employees and $n$ jobs, with a
proficiency $u(i,j)$ for employee $i$ in job $j$; maximise total proficiency.
Or: an international firm with vacancies abroad, where for each
(employee, job) pair we know whether the employee \emph{can} do the job and what
it costs to relocate them and their family. The first is maximum weight
bipartite matching, the second minimum cost perfect matching, and both go
through \cref{ch:min-cost-flow}.

\paragraph{Tracking.} A camera sees a set of objects at time $t$ and another set
at time $t+1$. Which object at $t+1$ is which object at $t$? Score each possible
correspondence by how plausible the implied movement is, and take the best
matching. The same problem, with the same solution, arises in tracking trains
through sections of track that are not remotely monitored.

\section{Augmenting paths}
\label{sec:matching-augmenting}
%==============================================================================

Everything rests on one definition and one theorem.

\begin{definition}[Alternating and augmenting]\label{def:matching-augmenting}
Let $M$ be a matching. An \emph{$M$-alternating walk} is a walk --- possibly
not simple --- whose edges are alternately outside and inside $M$. An
\emph{$M$-augmenting path} is a simple $M$-alternating walk of odd length whose
two endpoints are both unmatched.
\end{definition}

An augmenting path starts and ends with a non-matching edge, so it has one more
non-matching edge than matching edges. Flipping it --- taking its non-matching
edges into $M$ and removing its matching edges --- therefore yields a matching
with one more edge. That much is immediate. What is not immediate is that this
is the \emph{only} obstruction.

\begin{theorem}[Berge]\label{thm:matching-berge}
A matching $M$ in a graph $G$ is maximum if and only if $G$ has no
$M$-augmenting path.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
($\Rightarrow$) If an $M$-augmenting path exists, flipping it gives a larger
matching, so $M$ was not maximum.

($\Leftarrow$) Suppose $M$ is not maximum, and let $N$ be a larger matching.
Consider the symmetric difference
\[ N \,\triangle\, M \;=\; (N \cup M) \setminus (N \cap M). \]
Every vertex is incident to at most one edge of $N$ and at most one of $M$, so
in $N \triangle M$ every vertex has degree $0$, $1$ or $2$. A graph with
maximum degree $2$ is a disjoint union of paths and cycles. Moreover every one
of those paths and cycles alternates between $N$-edges and $M$-edges, since two
consecutive edges from the same matching would share a vertex.

An alternating cycle has even length and thus equally many edges of each. Since
$|N| > |M|$, some component must contain more edges of $N$ than of $M$, and by
the previous sentence it is not a cycle. So it is an alternating path with more
$N$-edges than $M$-edges, which forces it to begin and end with an $N$-edge;
both of its endpoints are therefore unmatched by $M$. That is an
$M$-augmenting path.
\end{proof}

\Cref{thm:matching-berge} is stated for arbitrary graphs, and that is the point.
It gives the algorithm immediately: start anywhere, find an augmenting path,
flip, repeat; stop when none exists, and \emph{that} is a maximum matching.
There are at most $n/2$ iterations. Everything that follows is about the one
remaining question --- how do you find an augmenting path?

\subsection{The bipartite case}
\label{sec:matching-bipartite}

In a bipartite graph, flow answers it. Orient every edge from $U$ to $W$, give
it capacity $1$, add a source with unit-capacity arcs into every vertex of $U$
and a sink with unit-capacity arcs out of every vertex of $W$, and compute a
maximum flow with an algorithm that returns an integral one. The augmenting
paths of Ford--Fulkerson in this network are precisely the $M$-augmenting paths
of the matching: an augmenting path enters $U$ at a free vertex, crosses to $W$
along an unused edge, comes back along a used one, and so on, ending at a free
vertex of $W$.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\figplaceholder{45mm}{One flow-augmentation step on a bipartite graph, drawn as
the slides do: the two sides as columns, matched edges thick, the augmenting
path highlighted from a free vertex on the left to a free vertex on the right,
alternating between unmatched and matched edges. Label the two free endpoints
and mark the matched vertices in between.}
\caption{In a bipartite graph, a flow-augmenting path is an $M$-augmenting
path.}
\label{fig:matching-bipartite-augment}
\end{figure}

So why does this not settle the general case? Because the reduction to flow is
what fails, not the theory. Berge's theorem holds regardless. The trouble is
that the natural search --- walk out from a free vertex, alternating --- can
return to a vertex it has already used, and a walk that repeats a vertex is not
a path. In a bipartite graph that cannot happen in a harmful way, because every
cycle is even. In general it can, and the culprit has a name.

\section{Edmonds' algorithm}
\label{sec:matching-edmonds}
%==============================================================================

\begin{definition}[Flower, blossom, stem]\label{def:matching-flower}
An \emph{$M$-flower} is an $M$-alternating walk of odd length that starts at an
unmatched vertex and ends by closing an odd cycle. The odd cycle is the
\emph{$M$-blossom}; the vertices of the walk before the cycle form the
\emph{stem}.
\end{definition}

A blossom has odd length $2k+1$, and exactly $k$ of its edges are in $M$: one
vertex of it --- the one where the stem arrives, or the free vertex itself if
the stem is empty --- is left unmatched within the cycle.

\centering

stem of alternating edges running right, and an odd cycle at the end. Draw
matching edges thick and non-matching edges thin, mark the free vertex, label
the stem and the blossom, and mark the single vertex of the blossom that is not
matched inside it. Keep the alternation visibly correct --- the stem must arrive
at the blossom along a matching edge.}
\caption{An $M$-flower. The blossom is the odd cycle; the stem is what leads to
it.}
\label{fig:matching-flower}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Finding a walk}
\label{sec:matching-find-walk}

First, a search that never gets stuck: build a digraph in which one step is two
alternating edges.

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
\caption{Find an $M$-alternating walk between two free vertices}
\label{alg:matching-walk}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
  \State $X \gets$ the set of unmatched vertices
  \State $Y \gets$ the set of vertices adjacent to some vertex of $X$
  \State build the digraph $D = (V,A)$ with
         $A = \{(u,v) : \exists x \text{ with } \{u,x\} \in E \setminus M
         \text{ and } \{x,v\} \in M\}$
  \State find a shortest path $P$ from $X$ to $Y$ in $D$
  \If{no such path exists} \Return ``$M$ is maximum'' \EndIf
  \State expand $P$ into the underlying alternating edges of $G$ and append the
         final edge into $X$, giving a walk $P'$
  \State \Return $P'$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Each arc of $D$ represents traversing one non-matching edge followed by one
matching edge, so a path in $D$ unfolds into an alternating walk in $G$; adding
one last non-matching edge to a free vertex makes both ends free. If $Y$ is
unreachable from $X$ then no alternating walk between free vertices exists at
all, and by \cref{thm:matching-berge} the matching is maximum.

\subsection{Either a path or a flower}
\label{sec:matching-path-or-flower}

The walk $P'$ that comes back need not be simple. Two cases.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:matching-path-or-flower}
Let $P'$ be a shortest alternating walk between two free vertices, as produced
by \cref{alg:matching-walk}. Then either $P'$ is a simple path --- and then it
is an $M$-augmenting path --- or its initial segment up to the first repeated
vertex closes an \emph{odd} cycle, and $P'$ contains an $M$-flower.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If $P'$ is simple it is an alternating path of odd length between two free
vertices, which is the definition of augmenting.

Otherwise, follow $P'$ from its start until some vertex is met for the second
time; this closes a cycle. Suppose that cycle had \emph{even} length. Then the
edge by which the stem arrives is a non-matching edge, and the cycle can simply
be cut out of the walk: removing it leaves a shorter alternating walk between
the same two free vertices, contradicting the choice of $P'$ as shortest. So the
cycle is odd, the stem arrives along a matching edge, and what we have is an
$M$-flower.
\end{proof}

\centering
\figplaceholder{55mm}{Two panels. Left: the good case --- the walk is simple and

cycle, drawn as a flower. Underneath, a third small panel showing the even-cycle

\caption{A shortest alternating walk between free vertices is either an

\end{figure}

\subsection{Shrinking a blossom}
\label{sec:matching-shrinking}

\begin{definition}
For a set $B \subseteq V$, write $G/B$ for the graph obtained by contracting $B$
to a single vertex, and $M/B$ for $M$ with every edge having both endpoints in
$B$ deleted.
\end{definition}

If $B$ is an $M$-blossom then $M/B$ is a matching of $G/B$: the $k$ edges of $M$
inside the cycle disappear, and the contracted vertex is incident to at most the
one $M$-edge that entered the blossom from the stem.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:matching-blossom}
Let $B$ be an $M$-blossom in $G$. Then $G$ has an $M$-augmenting path if and
only if $G/B$ has an $M/B$-augmenting path.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
($\Leftarrow$) Let $P$ be an $M/B$-augmenting path in $G/B$. If $P$ avoids the
contracted vertex it is already an $M$-augmenting path of $G$. Otherwise it
meets the contracted vertex, and we lift it. Say $P$ enters the blossom along an
edge that in $G$ arrives at a vertex $b \in B$. The blossom is an odd cycle with
one vertex $r$ unmatched inside it, and from $b$ there are two ways round the
cycle to $r$ --- one of even length and one of odd. Exactly one of them
alternates correctly with the edges at $b$ and at $r$, and taking that one turns
$P$ into an alternating path in $G$ with the same free endpoints. There are two
sub-cases according to whether $P$ passes through the blossom and out along the
stem, or ends at it, and both are resolved the same way.

($\Rightarrow$) The converse needs a preliminary move. First arrange that the
blossom contains a free vertex: if the stem is non-empty, flip it, giving a
matching $M'$ with $|M'| = |M|$ in which the blossom's unmatched vertex is free
in $G$. This is harmless, because for two matchings of the same size, $G$ has an
$M$-augmenting path if and only if it has an $M'$-augmenting path --- both
statements say, by \cref{thm:matching-berge}, that the matchings are not
maximum.

Now suppose $G$ has an $M'$-augmenting path $P$. If $P$ does not meet $B$ it
survives contraction. Otherwise, reversing $P$ if necessary, assume it does not
start inside $B$, and follow it until it first reaches $B$. The blossom contains
no $M'$-augmenting path of its own --- only an augmenting \emph{walk}, which is
exactly what a blossom is --- and since the contracted vertex is free in
$G/B$, the initial segment of $P$ up to $B$ becomes an $M'/B$-augmenting path in
$G/B$.
\end{proof}

\centering
\figplaceholder{60mm}{IMPORTANT --- this is the picture the argument cannot do

\caption{Lifting an augmenting path back through a contracted blossom.}
\label{fig:matching-blossom-lift}
\end{figure}

\begin{intuition}
Why does contraction not lose information? Because a blossom is exactly the
structure that can be entered and left \emph{on either side} without breaking
the alternation --- the odd cycle has one way round of each parity, so whatever
the rest of the path needs, the blossom can supply. Contracting it says: ``do
not worry about how to get across this thing, it can always be crossed.'' The
even cycle case of \cref{lem:matching-path-or-flower} is the mirror image ---
an even cycle offers no such choice, which is why it is never needed and why
bipartite graphs never produce blossoms at all.
\end{intuition}

\subsection{The algorithm}
\label{sec:matching-algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
\caption{Edmonds' algorithm}
\label{alg:matching-edmonds}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
  \State $M \gets$ any matching, for instance a greedy maximal one
  \While{\textsc{FindAugmenting}$(G, M)$ returns a path $P$}
     \State $M \gets M \,\triangle\, P$
  \EndWhile
  \State \Return $M$
  \Statex
  \Function{FindAugmenting}{$G$, $M$}
     \State run \cref{alg:matching-walk}
     \If{it reports that no alternating walk exists} \Return ``none'' \EndIf
     \If{the walk $P'$ is a simple path} \Return $P'$ \EndIf
     \State let $B$ be the blossom of the flower found in $P'$
     \State $P \gets \textsc{FindAugmenting}(G/B,\ M/B)$
     \If{$P$ is ``none''} \Return ``none'' \EndIf
     \State \Return the lift of $P$ through $B$
  \EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Correctness is \cref{thm:matching-berge} for the outer loop and
\cref{thm:matching-blossom} for the recursion --- the latter is what licenses
returning ``none'' when the contracted instance has no augmenting path.

\begin{theorem}[\citet{Edmonds1965}]\label{thm:matching-edmonds-time}
A maximum matching can be found in $\bigoh{n^2m}$ time.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Each augmentation increases $|M|$ by one, so the outer loop runs at most $n/2$
times. Within one call to \textsc{FindAugmenting}, each contraction removes at
least two vertices, so the recursion has depth $\bigoh{n}$, and the work per
level --- building $D$, one graph search, one contraction, one lift --- is
$\bigoh{m}$. That gives $\bigoh{nm}$ per augmentation and $\bigoh{n^2m}$ in
total.
\end{proof}

Better bookkeeping and better data structures bring this to $\bigoh{n^3}$, and
\citet{MicaliVazirani1980} achieve $\bigoh{\sqrt{n}\,m}$ by much more
complicated means. A similar but more intricate algorithm solves minimum cost
perfect matching in polynomial time.

\section{Regular bipartite graphs: Schrijver's algorithm}
\label{sec:matching-schrijver}
%==============================================================================

We already know from \cref{thm:mcf-regular-bipartite} that every $d$-regular
bipartite graph has a perfect matching. That proof was an existence argument
resting on integrality, and it gives an algorithm only by way of a maximum-flow
computation. \citet{Schrijver1999} does better, and the algorithm is startlingly
short.

Give every edge a weight $w(e)$, all initialised to $1$, and write $G_w$ for the
subgraph of edges of positive weight.

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
\caption{Schrijver's algorithm}
\label{alg:matching-schrijver}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
  \State $w(e) \gets 1$ for every edge $e$
  \While{$G_w$ contains a cycle $C$}
     \State split $C$ into its two matchings $M$ and $N$, labelled so that
            $w(M) \ge w(N)$
     \State $w(e) \gets w(e) + 1$ for every $e \in M$
     \State $w(e) \gets w(e) - 1$ for every $e \in N$
  \EndWhile
  \State \Return $G_w$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The graph is bipartite, so every cycle has even length and its edges do split
alternately into two matchings. Why the output is a perfect matching is the
following invariant.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:matching-schrijver-invariant}
Throughout the algorithm, for every vertex $v$ the weights of the edges incident
to $v$ sum to $d$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Initially every vertex has $d$ incident edges of weight $1$. A cycle visits each
of its vertices exactly once, using two incident edges, one from $M$ and one
from $N$; the update adds $1$ to the first and subtracts $1$ from the second, so
the sum at that vertex is unchanged. Vertices off the cycle are untouched.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:matching-schrijver}
\Cref{alg:matching-schrijver} returns a perfect matching, in
$\bigoh{nd^2} = \bigoh{dm}$ time.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Correctness.} On termination $G_w$ is acyclic. Every vertex has positive
total incident weight $d$ by \cref{lem:matching-schrijver-invariant}, so every
vertex has at least one incident edge in $G_w$; a forest in which every vertex
has degree at least one and which has no vertex of degree greater than one would
be a perfect matching, so it suffices to rule out a vertex of degree $\ge 2$ in
$G_w$. Suppose $v$ had two incident edges of positive weight. Following
positive-weight edges from $v$, the invariant guarantees that every vertex
reached again has another positive-weight edge to continue along, so the walk
cannot stop, and in a finite graph it must repeat a vertex --- a cycle,
contradicting termination. Hence every vertex of $G_w$ has degree exactly one,
and, weights being integers summing to $d$ at each vertex, that single edge has
weight $d$. So $G_w$ is a perfect matching.

\emph{Termination and running time.} Consider the potential
$\Phi = \sum_{e} w(e)^2$. Splitting a cycle $C$ into $M$ and $N$ with
$w(M) \ge w(N)$ and updating changes $\Phi$ by
\[ \sum_{e \in M}\bigl((w(e)+1)^2 - w(e)^2\bigr)
 + \sum_{e \in N}\bigl((w(e)-1)^2 - w(e)^2\bigr)
 \;=\; |C| + 2\bigl(w(M) - w(N)\bigr) \;\ge\; |C|, \]
using $|M| + |N| = |C|$. So every iteration increases $\Phi$ by at least $|C|$.
Initially $\Phi = m$, and it never exceeds $nd^2$, since no weight exceeds $d$
and there are at most $nd/2$ edges. A cycle in $G_w$ can be found in
$\bigoh{|C|}$ amortised time by maintaining a path of edges of weight strictly
between $0$ and $d$ and extending it until it closes, with $\bigoh{n+m}$
overhead. Charging each cycle's cost against the increase in $\Phi$ gives
$\bigoh{nd^2 + n + m} = \bigoh{nd^2}$, which is $\bigoh{dm}$ since
$m = nd/2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{intuition}
Read the invariant as a fractional perfect matching: dividing every weight by
$d$ gives non-negative values summing to $1$ at every vertex, which is exactly
the fractional flow of \cref{thm:mcf-regular-bipartite}. The algorithm is
therefore doing something very concrete --- pushing a fractional perfect
matching towards an integral one, one cycle at a time, never disturbing the
vertex sums. The potential $\sum_e w(e)^2$ measures how far along that journey
it has come; it is maximised exactly when the weights are as unequal as
possible, which is to say integral.
\end{intuition}

\section{Application: edge colouring and classroom scheduling}
\label{sec:matching-edge-colouring}
%==============================================================================

A school has teachers and class groups, and it has already been decided which
teacher teaches which group and for how many hours. We must build a timetable
in which no teacher takes two groups at once and no group has two lessons at
once. How many hours are needed?

\paragraph{The model.} Build a bipartite graph with a vertex per teacher, a
vertex per group, and one edge per lesson that must be scheduled. A timetable
assigns each lesson an hour, and the two constraints say exactly that two edges
sharing a vertex get different hours. That is an \emph{edge colouring}, and the
question is the minimum number of colours.

A lower bound is immediate: a teacher who must teach $d$ lessons needs $d$
distinct hours, so at least $\Delta(G)$ colours are needed, where $\Delta$ is
the maximum degree. Remarkably, for bipartite graphs that bound is always
attained.

\begin{theorem}[König's edge colouring theorem]\label{thm:matching-edge-colouring}
Every bipartite graph $G$ with maximum degree $d$ has an edge colouring with $d$
colours.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
First make $G$ $d$-regular without decreasing $d$. Let the sides be $V_1$ and
$V_2$; add isolated vertices to the smaller side until $|V_1| = |V_2|$. Now,
while some vertex has degree less than $d$, pick $v \in V_1$ and $w \in V_2$
both of degree less than $d$ and add the edge $\{v,w\}$. Such a pair exists
whenever any vertex is deficient: the two sides have equal size, so
$\sum_{V_1} \deg = \sum_{V_2} \deg$, and if some vertex of $V_1$ has degree
below $d$ then the sum over $V_1$ is below $d|V_1|$, hence so is the sum over
$V_2$, so some vertex of $V_2$ is deficient too. The graph stays bipartite and
simple, and the process terminates because the number of edges increases.

Now repeat $d$ times: the graph is regular bipartite, so by
\cref{thm:mcf-regular-bipartite} it has a perfect matching $M$; take $M$ as the
next colour class and delete it. Deleting a perfect matching lowers every degree
by exactly one, so the graph remains regular and the argument repeats. After $d$
rounds every edge has a colour.

Finally discard the edges that were added, keeping the colours of the original
ones.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
A classroom timetable using the minimum number of hours can be computed in
$\bigoh{nd^3}$ time.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
$d$ rounds, each a perfect matching in a regular bipartite graph, at
$\bigoh{nd^2}$ each by \cref{thm:matching-schrijver}.
\end{proof}

\section{Partitioning a graph into cycles}
\label{sec:matching-modelling}
%==============================================================================

\begin{probdef}{Partition into Cycles}
  \instance An undirected graph $G = (V,E)$.
  \question Is there a collection of simple cycles $C_1, \ldots, C_r$ in $G$
  such that every vertex belongs to exactly one of them?
\end{probdef}

A collection of vertex-disjoint cycles covering every vertex is the same thing
as a spanning subgraph in which every vertex has degree exactly $2$ --- a
\emph{$2$-factor}. Indeed a graph all of whose degrees are $2$ is a disjoint
union of cycles, and in a simple graph those cycles have length at least $3$ and
so are simple cycles.

So the question is whether $G$ has a $2$-factor, and that is a matching problem
in disguise.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:matching-two-factor}
\lang{Partition into Cycles} can be solved in polynomial time.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If any vertex has degree less than $2$ the answer is no, so assume
$\deg(v) \ge 2$ throughout. Build an auxiliary graph $H$ as follows. For each
vertex $v$ of $G$:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item add $\deg(v)$ \emph{external} vertices, one for each edge incident to
        $v$;
  \item add $\deg(v) - 2$ \emph{internal} vertices;
  \item join every internal vertex of $v$ to every external vertex of $v$.
\end{itemize}
Finally, for each edge $\{u,v\}$ of $G$, join the external vertex of $u$
belonging to that edge to the external vertex of $v$ belonging to it. We claim
$G$ has a $2$-factor if and only if $H$ has a perfect matching.

($\Rightarrow$) Given a $2$-factor $F$, match across for the two edges of $F$ at
each vertex $v$, and match the remaining $\deg(v)-2$ external vertices of $v$ to
its $\deg(v)-2$ internal vertices, which is possible because they form a
complete bipartite graph on two sets of equal size. Every vertex of $H$ is then
matched.

($\Leftarrow$) Given a perfect matching of $H$, call an edge of $G$
\emph{selected} if the corresponding external--external edge is in the matching.
Fix $v$. Its internal vertices are matched, and their only neighbours are
external vertices of $v$, so exactly $\deg(v)-2$ of $v$'s external vertices are
matched internally. The remaining two are matched across, so exactly two edges
at $v$ are selected. Hence the selected edges form a $2$-factor.

The construction takes polynomial time: $H$ has $\sum_v (2\deg(v) - 2) = 4m-2n$
vertices and $\sum_v \deg(v)(\deg(v)-2) + m = \bigoh{nm}$ edges. Running
\cref{alg:matching-edmonds} on $H$ therefore decides the question in polynomial
time.
\end{proof}

\begin{intuition}
The gadget is worth remembering, because it generalises immediately. Replacing
``$\deg(v)-2$ internal vertices'' by ``$\deg(v)-k$'' forces every vertex to have
degree exactly $k$, so the same construction decides whether $G$ has a
$k$-factor --- and with two sets of internal vertices, degrees between a lower
and an upper bound. The internal vertices are a counting device: they soak up
exactly the edges you do \emph{not} want used.

Note also which matching algorithm is needed. $H$ is not bipartite --- the
external--external edges see to that --- so the flow model of
\cref{sec:matching-bipartite} does not apply and \cref{thm:matching-edmonds-time}
is doing real work.
\end{intuition}

\section{Exercises}
\label{sec:ex-matching}
%==============================================================================

\begin{exercise}
Show that a maximal matching is always at least half the size of a maximum
matching, and give a graph where it is exactly half.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Prove that a graph has a perfect matching only if every one of its components
has an even number of vertices, and show that the converse fails.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Answer the three questions the lecture leaves open about
\cref{fig:matching-blossom-lift}: what happens if the lifted path would leave
the blossom one vertex further round; must the lifted path always pass through
the stem; and when does the case where the path \emph{ends} at the contracted
vertex actually occur?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Show that contracting a blossom can create parallel edges or self-loops in
$G/B$, and explain why neither harms \cref{alg:matching-edmonds}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Why does \cref{alg:matching-walk} search for a \emph{shortest} path in $D$?
Give an alternating walk that is not shortest and closes an even cycle, and show
what goes wrong if the algorithm returns it.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Run \cref{alg:matching-schrijver} by hand on the $3$-regular bipartite graph
$K_{3,3}$, starting from all weights $1$. How many iterations does it take?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Prove the claim used in \cref{thm:matching-schrijver} that no weight ever
exceeds $d$, and that weights never go negative.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Adapt \cref{thm:matching-two-factor} to decide whether a graph has a
$k$-factor, and then to decide whether it has a spanning subgraph in which every
vertex $v$ has degree between $\ell(v)$ and $u(v)$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\lang{Partition into Cycles} asks for cycles covering every vertex. What
changes if we ask instead for cycles covering every \emph{edge} exactly once?
Which chapter answers that?
\end{exercise}
