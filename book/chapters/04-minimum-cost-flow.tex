\chapter{Minimum Cost Flow}
\label{ch:min-cost-flow}

Attaching a price to each arc changes everything. Maximum flow asks how much
can be shipped; minimum cost flow asks how much it costs to ship a given
amount, and that second question is the one industry actually has. It is also a
far more expressive modelling language: assignment, transportation, scheduling,
inventory over time and a good deal of image processing are minimum cost flow
problems in disguise, and the disguises are worth learning to see through.

\begin{goals}
  \poolgoal{define the minimum cost flow problem and the cost of a flow}
  \poolgoal{recognise when unbounded capacities make the problem unbounded, and remove them otherwise}
  \poolgoal{convert between the single-source, multiple-source and supply/demand formulations}
  \poolgoal{transform an instance with negative arc costs into one with non-negative costs}
  \poolgoal{write both maximum flow and minimum cost flow as linear programs, and say what is gained and lost by doing so}
  \poolgoal{prove that every circulation decomposes into a non-negative combination of cycles, and every flow into paths and cycles}
  \poolgoal{define the residual network with costs}
  \poolgoal{state the cycle cancelling algorithm and prove the optimality criterion it rests on: a flow is minimum cost if and only if its residual network has no negative-cost cycle}
  \poolgoal{state the successive shortest paths algorithm and prove it correct}
  \poolgoal{formulate the problem of phasing out capital equipment --- and other applications you have not seen --- as a minimum cost flow problem, and justify the model}
\end{goals}

%==============================================================================
\section{The problem}
\label{sec:mcf-problem}
%==============================================================================

Maximum flow asked how much can be shipped. Minimum cost flow asks what the
shipping costs, and answering it needs one more number on every arc.

\begin{definition}[Cost of a flow]
Let $G = (V,A)$ be a directed graph with capacities $c : A \to \R_{\ge 0}$ and
costs $\cost : A \to \R$. The \emph{cost} of a flow $f$ is
\[ \cost(f) \;=\; \sum_{a \in A} f(a) \cdot \cost(a). \]
\end{definition}

The cost is paid \emph{per unit of flow} on each arc, so an arc of cost $3$
carrying $4$ units contributes $12$. Costs may be negative; capacities may not.

\begin{probdef}{Minimum Cost Flow}
  \instance A directed graph $G = (V,A)$, capacities $c$, costs $\cost$, a
  source $s$, a sink $t$, and a target flow value $r$.
  \goal Among all flows from $s$ to $t$ of value exactly $r$, find one of
  minimum cost.
\end{probdef}

Note the shape of the problem: the value is \emph{prescribed}, not maximised.
Fixing $r$ to the maximum flow value gives the min-cost max-flow problem, which
is the common special case, but the general problem is more useful precisely
because $r$ is a parameter --- shipping less can be much cheaper per unit, and
the modeller usually knows how much must move.

\section{Normal forms}
\label{sec:mcf-normal-forms}
%==============================================================================

Three assumptions make life easier, and all three are free.

\subsection{Unbounded capacities}
\label{sec:mcf-unbounded}

Unlike maximum flow, minimum cost flow is often stated with arcs of unbounded
capacity, and this is not merely a convenience: it can change the answer from a
number to nothing at all.

\begin{observation}\label{obs:mcf-unbounded}
If $G$ contains a cycle of negative total cost all of whose arcs have unbounded
capacity, then the problem has no optimum: given any flow, adding one more unit
of circulation around the cycle gives a feasible flow of the same value and
strictly smaller cost, and this can be repeated forever.
\end{observation}

Such a cycle is found by running Bellman--Ford on the subgraph of
unbounded-capacity arcs. If none exists, unboundedness is an illusion and can be
removed.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:mcf-bound-capacities}
If no negative-cost cycle consists entirely of unbounded arcs, then replacing
every unbounded capacity by
\[ r + \sum_{a : c(a) < \infty} c(a) \]
changes neither the optimal cost nor the set of optimal flows.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof idea]
No arc ever needs to carry more than this. By the decomposition of
\cref{thm:mcf-decomposition} below, an optimal flow can be taken to consist of
$r$ units along $s$--$t$ paths plus cycles; the paths put at most $r$ on any
arc, and any cycle carrying flow either has non-negative cost --- in which case
dropping it does not hurt --- or uses at least one bounded arc, which limits it.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Supplies and demands}
\label{sec:mcf-demands}

The single source and single sink are a presentational choice. In applications
one usually has a \emph{demand} $b(v)$ at every vertex, positive for a consumer
and negative for a producer, with
\[ \sum_{v \in V} b(v) = 0, \]
and one asks for a flow that meets every demand exactly. The two formulations
are interchangeable: add a super-source $s$ with an arc to each producer $v$ of
capacity $-b(v)$, a super-sink $t$ with an arc from each consumer $v$ of
capacity $b(v)$, all of cost zero, and set the target value
\[ r \;=\; \sum_{v \,:\, b(v) > 0} b(v). \]
A flow of value $r$ saturates every one of the new arcs --- they form a cut of
capacity exactly $r$ --- which is precisely the statement that every supply is
shipped and every demand met.

\subsection{Non-negative costs}
\label{sec:mcf-nonnegative-costs}

Negative arc costs are a nuisance --- they rule out Dijkstra, among other
things --- and they too can be removed, provided capacities are bounded.

The idea is \emph{arc reversal}. Let $a = (u,v)$ have capacity $c$ and cost
$-x$ with $x > 0$. Saturate it: decide in advance that $c$ units will flow along
$a$, collecting a cost of $-cx$, and record the freedom to \emph{undo} that
decision by reversing the arc. Concretely, in the supply/demand formulation:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item delete $a$ and add the reversed arc $(v,u)$ with capacity $c$ and cost
        $+x$;
  \item add an arc $(s,v)$ of capacity $c$ and cost $0$, and an arc $(u,t)$ of
        capacity $c$ and cost $0$ --- the $c$ units are now assumed to arrive
        at $v$ from outside, and $u$ must find $c$ units to have sent;
  \item add $c$ to the target flow value $r$, and subtract $cx$ from the cost
        of the computed solution.
\end{itemize}
Flow on the reversed arc means ``one of the $c$ units I assumed would travel
along $a$ does not'', and it costs $+x$ because that unit forgoes the saving.

%==============================================================================
\section{Flows and linear programming}
\label{sec:mcf-lp}
%==============================================================================

Both problems in this part of the book are linear programs, and it is worth
seeing that plainly before going further.

For maximum flow, take one variable $f(a)$ per arc; the constraints are flow
conservation at every internal vertex and $0 \le f(a) \le c(a)$ on every arc,
all linear; the objective is to maximise the net flow out of $s$. For minimum
cost flow, keep the same variables and constraints, add the linear constraint
that the value equals $r$, and minimise $\sum_a f(a)\cost(a)$.

Linear programming is solvable in polynomial time, so this already settles the
complexity of both problems. Three remarks on why we do not stop here.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Specialised algorithms are simpler and usually much faster.}
        Nothing in this chapter needs an LP solver, and the combinatorial
        algorithms exploit structure a general solver cannot see.
  \item \textbf{Integrality is not free from an LP solver.} Our algorithms
        return integral flows on integral data automatically
        (\cref{cor:mcf-integrality}); a general LP solver returns a vertex of
        the polytope, which happens to be integral here, but that is a theorem
        about the constraint matrix rather than something the solver promises.
  \item \textbf{The connection runs deeper than convenience.} Preflow-push is a
        \emph{dual} algorithm: the height function is a dual solution, and the
        algorithm maintains dual feasibility --- that is what the height
        invariant is --- while working towards primal feasibility. That is
        exactly the description in \cref{sec:flow-preflow-push}, now with a
        name.
\end{itemize}

In practice the useful hybrid is a mixed-integer solver: most treat flow
constraints specially, which makes them very effective on problems that are
almost flow problems but carry a handful of side constraints that no flow model
can express.

\section{Decomposition into paths and cycles}
\label{sec:mcf-decomposition}
%==============================================================================

The proofs below all rest on one structural fact: a flow is nothing but a bundle
of paths and cycles. We need it precisely.

\begin{definition}[Non-negative linear combination]
For a cycle $C$ write $C(a) = 1$ if $a \in C$ and $0$ otherwise, and similarly
for a path $P$. A flow $f$ is a \emph{non-negative linear combination} of paths
$P_1,\ldots,P_k$ and cycles $C_1,\ldots,C_\ell$ if there are constants
$p_1,\ldots,p_k \ge 0$ and $q_1,\ldots,q_\ell \ge 0$ with
\[ f(a) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{k} p_i \, P_i(a) \;+\; \sum_{i=1}^{\ell} q_i \, C_i(a)
   \qquad \text{for every arc } a. \]
If all the constants are integers the combination is \emph{integral}.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:mcf-decomposition}
Every circulation $f$ is a non-negative linear combination of cycles of $G$. If
$f$ is integral, the combination can be taken integral, and at most $m$ cycles
are needed.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Induction on the number of arcs carrying positive flow. If there are none, the
empty combination works. Otherwise pick an arc with $f(a) > 0$ and follow
positive-flow arcs forward from its head. Conservation guarantees that every
vertex we reach has an outgoing arc with positive flow, so the walk continues
until it revisits a vertex, at which point we have found a cycle $C$ all of
whose arcs carry positive flow. Let $q = \min_{a \in C} f(a) > 0$ and set
$f' = f - q\,C$. Then $f'$ is again a circulation --- subtracting a cycle
preserves conservation --- it is non-negative, and it has strictly fewer arcs
with positive flow, since the minimising arc now carries none. Apply induction
and add $q\,C$ back. If $f$ is integral then so is $q$, and hence so is
everything that follows.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:mcf-decomposition-flow}
Every flow $f$ of value $F$ is a non-negative linear combination of $s$--$t$
paths and cycles, with the path coefficients summing to $F$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Add the arc $(t,s)$ carrying $F$ units, which turns $f$ into a circulation.
Apply \cref{thm:mcf-decomposition}. Each cycle in the resulting combination
either uses the new arc --- delete that arc from it and it becomes an $s$--$t$
path --- or does not, and stays a cycle. The coefficients of the cycles through
$(t,s)$ sum to $F$, since that is what the arc carries.
\end{proof}

\section{The residual network with costs}
\label{sec:mcf-residual}
%==============================================================================

The residual network carried us through the whole of \cref{ch:max-flow}, and
it does the same here --- once it has been told what an arc costs. The only
new idea is that undoing a unit of flow refunds what that unit paid.

\begin{definition}[Residual network with costs]\label{def:mcf-residual}
Let $f$ be a flow. The residual network $G_f$ has capacities as in
\cref{def:flow-residual}, and costs assigned as follows: a forward residual arc
$a$ keeps $\cost(a)$, and a backward residual arc $a^{-1}$, present whenever
$f(a) > 0$, gets
\[ \cost(a^{-1}) \;=\; -\cost(a). \]
An arc that already exists in the reverse direction in $G$ keeps its own cost;
the two are different arcs and must be kept apart.
\end{definition}

The sign is the whole point. Sending a unit backwards along $a$ cancels a unit
that was sent forwards, so it refunds what that unit cost. It follows that
pushing $\delta$ units around any cycle $C$ of $G_f$ changes the cost of the
flow by exactly $\delta \cdot \cost(C)$, where $\cost(C)$ is the sum of the
residual costs along $C$ --- and that is the quantity the next section is
about.

\section{Cycle cancelling}
\label{sec:mcf-cycle-cancelling}
%==============================================================================

The algorithm is exactly what the previous paragraph suggests.

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
\caption{Cycle cancelling}
\label{alg:mcf-cycle-cancelling}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
  \State construct any feasible flow $f$ of value $r$ (by a maximum flow
         computation, ignoring costs)
  \While{$G_f$ contains a cycle $C$ of negative cost}
     \State $\delta \gets$ the minimum residual capacity along $C$
     \State add $\delta$ units of flow around $C$
  \EndWhile
  \State \Return $f$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Every iteration keeps the value unchanged --- a cycle neither produces nor
consumes --- and strictly decreases the cost. What needs proof is that getting
stuck means being optimal.

\begin{lemma}[Difference of two flows]\label{lem:mcf-difference}
Let $f$ and $f'$ be flows in $G$. Define $f' - f$ on the residual network $G_f$
by
\[ (f'-f)(a) = \max\{0,\; f'(a)-f(a)\}, \qquad
   (f'-f)(a^{-1}) = \max\{0,\; -(f'(a)-f(a))\} \]
for every arc $a$ of $G$. Then $f'-f$ is a flow in $G_f$, and if $f$ and $f'$
have the same value then $f'-f$ is a circulation in $G_f$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
In words: subtract coordinatewise, and when the difference is negative, send
the negative part along the reverse arc instead --- which is exactly the arc
$G_f$ provides for it.

\emph{Capacities.} If $f'(a) > f(a)$ then $(f'-f)(a) = f'(a)-f(a) \le
c(a)-f(a)$, which is the residual capacity of $a$. If $f'(a) < f(a)$ then
$(f'-f)(a^{-1}) = f(a)-f'(a) \le f(a)$, which is the residual capacity of
$a^{-1}$.

\emph{Conservation.} At any vertex $v$, the net flow of $f'-f$ into $v$ is the
net inflow of $f'$ minus the net inflow of $f$ --- the reverse-arc convention
was designed so that this holds arc by arc. Both are zero at every internal
vertex, so the difference is zero there too.

\emph{Circulation.} At $s$ and $t$ the nets are $-|f'|$ and $-|f|$, and $|f'|$
and $|f|$ respectively; if the values agree these cancel, and conservation holds
everywhere.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Optimality criterion]\label{thm:mcf-optimality}
A flow $f$ has minimum cost among all flows of its value if and only if $G_f$
contains no negative-cost cycle.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
($\Rightarrow$) If $G_f$ has a negative-cost cycle, pushing flow around it
produces a flow of the same value and strictly smaller cost, so $f$ was not
optimal.

($\Leftarrow$) Suppose $G_f$ has no negative-cost cycle, and let $f'$ be a
minimum cost flow of the same value. By \cref{lem:mcf-difference}, $f'-f$ is a
circulation in $G_f$, and by \cref{thm:mcf-decomposition} it is a non-negative
combination of cycles of $G_f$. Every one of those cycles has non-negative
cost by assumption, so
\[ \cost(f') - \cost(f) \;=\; \cost(f'-f) \;\ge\; 0, \]
where the first equality is the observation that residual costs are signed to
make exactly this bookkeeping work. Hence $\cost(f) \le \cost(f')$, and $f$ is
optimal too.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Integrality]\label{cor:mcf-integrality}
If all capacities and the target value $r$ are integers, there is an integral
minimum cost flow, and \cref{alg:mcf-cycle-cancelling} finds one.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The initial flow can be taken integral by \cref{cor:flow-integrality}. On an
integral flow every residual capacity is an integer, so each $\delta$ is a
positive integer and integrality is preserved. The algorithm terminates because
the cost strictly decreases by at least $\delta \ge 1$ times the cycle cost each
round and is bounded below.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Two practical notes. First, cycle cancelling works unchanged in the presence of
lower bounds on arcs: find a feasible flow respecting them by the method of
\cref{sec:flow-lower-bounds}, and then cancel cycles in the residual network of
\cref{def:mcf-residual} adapted as in that section. Second, and less happily,
nothing above bounds the number of iterations by a polynomial. The algorithm
is correct but not, as stated, efficient.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Cancelling the right cycle}

The fix is to be choosy. If at each step one cancels a cycle of minimum
\emph{mean} cost --- total cost divided by number of arcs --- the algorithm
becomes polynomial.

A minimum mean cycle can be found in $\bigoh{nm}$ time by a dynamic program
over walks of each length, which is left as an exercise. The number of
iterations needed is $\bigoh{nm^2\log^2 n}$, which gives an
$\bigoh{n^2m^3\log^2 n}$ algorithm overall. That bound is generous; sharper
analyses and sharper algorithms exist, and \citet{GoldbergTarjan1988} is the
place to start.

\section{Successive shortest paths}
\label{sec:mcf-successive-shortest-paths}
%==============================================================================

Cycle cancelling starts from a feasible flow of the right value and repairs its
cost. The other natural approach starts from the zero flow, which has the right
cost --- nothing is cheaper than nothing --- and repairs its value, keeping
optimality at every intermediate value along the way.

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
\caption{Successive shortest paths}
\label{alg:mcf-ssp}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
  \State $f \gets 0$
  \While{$|f| < r$}
     \State find a shortest $s$--$t$ path $P$ in $G_f$, using the residual
            costs as arc lengths
     \State $q \gets$ the minimum residual capacity along $P$
     \State send $\min\{q,\; r - |f|\}$ additional units along $P$
  \EndWhile
  \State \Return $f$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:mcf-ssp}
If $G$ contains no negative-cost cycle, \cref{alg:mcf-ssp} returns a minimum
cost flow of value $r$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We prove the invariant: \emph{at every stage, $f$ has minimum cost among all
flows of value $|f|$.} By \cref{thm:mcf-optimality} it is equivalent, and more
convenient, to say that $G_f$ has no negative-cost cycle.

\emph{Initially.} $f = 0$ and $G_f = G$, which has no negative-cost cycle by
hypothesis. So the zero flow is optimal among flows of value $0$.

\emph{Inductive step.} Suppose $f$ is optimal for its value, and let $f'$ be
obtained by sending flow along a shortest path $P$ in $G_f$. Let $f''$ be any
minimum cost flow of the same value as $f'$. By \cref{lem:mcf-difference},
$f'' - f$ is a flow in $G_f$, so by \cref{cor:mcf-decomposition-flow} it is a
non-negative combination of cycles of $G_f$ and $s$--$t$ paths in $G_f$. Now:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item every cycle in the combination has cost at least $0$, since $f$ is
        optimal for its value and \cref{thm:mcf-optimality} applies;
  \item every path in the combination has cost at least $\cost(P)$, since $P$
        was chosen to be a shortest path in $G_f$.
\end{itemize}
The path coefficients sum to $|f'| - |f|$, the same amount that was sent along
$P$. Hence
\[ \cost(f'' - f) \;\ge\; (|f'|-|f|)\cdot \cost(P) \;=\; \cost(f'-f), \]
and therefore $\cost(f'') \ge \cost(f')$. So $f'$ is optimal for its value too.
\end{proof}

Two warnings, both worth taking seriously.

\begin{remark}
The hypothesis is not decorative. If $G$ has a negative-cost cycle the algorithm
is simply wrong: take $r = 0$, and it returns the zero flow at cost $0$ while
the optimum circulates around the cycle at negative cost. Remove negative cycles
first --- \cref{sec:mcf-nonnegative-costs} does it by making all costs
non-negative.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
The running time can be exponential, for the same reason as Ford--Fulkerson: the
number of iterations depends on how much flow each path carries, which depends
on capacities rather than on the size of the input. Note also that the residual
costs are negative even when the original costs are not, so the shortest paths
must be found with Bellman--Ford rather than Dijkstra --- unless one maintains
vertex potentials, which is the standard refinement and restores Dijkstra.
\end{remark}

%==============================================================================
\section{Applications}
\label{sec:mcf-applications}
%==============================================================================

Minimum cost flow shows up wherever a fixed amount of something has to be moved
and the movement has a price: transport and logistics, repositioning empty
containers, minimum cost bipartite matching (\cref{sec:mcf-matching}). One less
obvious example is medical imaging --- reconstructing the left ventricle from
X-ray projections. The input is a two-dimensional array of bits with known row
sums, known column sums, and a probability attached to each bit; the output
wanted is the most likely image consistent with those sums. That is a minimum
cost flow problem, with the sums as capacities and the log-probabilities as
costs, and it is closely related to the matrix rounding problem of
\cref{sec:flow-matrix-rounding}.

Two models are worth doing in full.

\subsection{Loading a hopping aeroplane}
\label{sec:mcf-hopping-plane}

An aeroplane flies the route $1 \to 2 \to \cdots \to n$, stopping at every city.
Between cities $i < j$ there are $b_{ij}$ passengers wanting to travel, each
paying a fare $f_{ij}$. The plane holds $p$ passengers at a time. Which
passengers should be carried?

The temptation is to think of this as a knapsack problem, which would make it
NP-hard. It is not, because the capacity constraint is per \emph{leg} and the
legs form a path --- and paths are flow networks.

\paragraph{The model.}
Build a network with
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item a \emph{city node} $i$ for each city, and the \emph{leg arcs}
        $(i, i+1)$ of capacity $p$ and cost $0$: the flow on a leg arc is the
        number of passengers on board for that leg, and the capacity is the
        aircraft;
  \item a \emph{demand node} $ij$ for each pair $i<j$ with $b_{ij} > 0$, of
        supply $b_{ij}$;
  \item a \emph{boarding arc} $(ij, i)$ of infinite capacity and cost
        $-f_{ij}$: routing a unit this way means carrying that passenger, and
        it earns the fare;
  \item a \emph{refusal arc} $(ij, j)$ of infinite capacity and cost $0$:
        routing a unit this way means turning that passenger away;
  \item at each city node $i$, a demand of $\sum_{j} b_{ji}$, the number of
        passengers whose journey ends there.
\end{itemize}
Every unit of the supply at $ij$ must reach the demand at $j$, and it has
exactly two routes: board at $i$, ride the legs $i, i+1, \ldots, j$ and consume
capacity on each, collecting $-f_{ij}$; or go straight to $j$ at no cost and no
benefit. Minimising cost therefore maximises total fare subject to no leg
carrying more than $p$ passengers, which is the problem. Convert to a single
source and sink by \cref{sec:mcf-demands}.

\subsection{Phasing out capital equipment}
\label{sec:mcf-ships}

This is the model the exam asks for, and it is worth doing slowly, because the
difficulty is entirely in setting it up.

\begin{probdef}{Fleet Phase-Out}
  \instance A shipping company owns $N$ identical cargo ships and plans over
  $p$ years. In year $i$ it needs $d(i)$ ships available, where $d$ is
  non-increasing. A ship sold in year $i$ yields $s_i$; a ship owned in year
  $i$ earns a profit $r_i$; if the company owns fewer than $d(i)$ ships in year
  $i$ it must hire the shortfall at $h_i$ per ship, where $h_i > r_i$.
  \goal Decide when to sell each ship so as to maximise cash assets at the end
  of year $p$.
\end{probdef}

\paragraph{What is the flow?}
Answer this first, as always. \emph{One unit of flow is one ship, travelling
forwards through time until it is sold.} Everything else follows.

\paragraph{The chain.}
Take nodes $1, 2, \ldots, p+1$, one per year plus one for the end, a source
$\sigma$ supplying $N$ units into node $1$, and a sink $\tau$. The flow on the
link from node $i$ to node $i+1$ is the number of ships the company still owns
during year $i$; call it $x_i$. Ships leave the chain by \emph{sale arcs}
$(i, \tau)$ of capacity $N$ and cost $-s_i$, and whatever remains leaves at the
end. Since nothing ever enters the chain except the initial $N$ units, $x_i$ is
automatically non-increasing --- the company can sell but not buy, which is what
``phasing out'' means.

\paragraph{The costs, and the one trick.}
Owning $x$ ships in year $i$ costs
\[ \gamma_i(x) \;=\; -r_i x \;+\; h_i \max\{0,\; d(i)-x\}, \]
profit counted as negative cost. This is not linear, so it cannot be the cost of
a single arc. Rewrite it:
\[ \gamma_i(x) \;=\; \underbrace{h_i\, d(i)}_{\text{constant}}
   \;-\; \bigl(r_i + h_i\bigr)\min\{x,\, d(i)\} \;-\; r_i \max\{0,\, x - d(i)\}. \]
Read off the slopes: the first $d(i)$ ships are worth $r_i + h_i$ each --- they
earn their profit \emph{and} save a hire --- and every ship beyond $d(i)$ is
worth only $r_i$. Since $h_i > 0$, the marginal value \emph{decreases} with $x$,
so $\gamma_i$ is convex, and a convex piecewise-linear cost is exactly what
parallel arcs express:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item a \emph{cheap} arc from $i$ to $i+1$ of capacity $d(i)$ and cost
        $-(r_i + h_i)$;
  \item a \emph{dear} arc from $i$ to $i+1$ of capacity $N$ and cost $-r_i$.
\end{itemize}
Add the constant $\sum_i h_i d(i)$ to the objective at the end.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:mcf-ships-correct}
A minimum cost flow of value $N$ in this network, plus the constant
$\sum_i h_i d(i)$, has cost equal to minus the maximum achievable cash position,
and its sale arcs give an optimal sale schedule.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Convexity is what makes the two parallel arcs behave. Because
$-(r_i+h_i) < -r_i$, any flow that puts a unit on the dear arc while the cheap
arc has spare capacity can be improved by moving that unit, so in an optimal
flow the cheap arc fills first. Hence the flow on the pair of arcs, $x_i$,
incurs exactly $-(r_i+h_i)\min\{x_i,d(i)\} - r_i\max\{0,x_i-d(i)\}$, which with
the constant is $\gamma_i(x_i)$.

Conversely every sale schedule gives such a flow. So the two optimisation
problems have the same feasible values and the same objective, and by
\cref{cor:mcf-integrality} an integral optimum exists --- which matters, since
half a ship is not a ship.
\end{proof}

\begin{intuition}
Notice what did the work. The constraint ``own at least $d(i)$ or pay to hire''
looks like a lower bound, and one \emph{could} model it with the lower-bound
machinery of \cref{sec:flow-lower-bounds}. Rewriting it as a convex cost is
better: it needs no lower bounds, no second maximum-flow computation, and it
makes the economics visible --- the first $d(i)$ ships are worth more than the
rest, and exactly how much more. Whenever a penalty in a model is proportional
to a shortfall, try to express it as a convex cost before reaching for lower
bounds.
\end{intuition}

\begin{remark}
Two modelling conventions have to be fixed before the model is well defined, and
an exam answer should state them: whether a ship sold in year $i$ still earns
$r_i$ that year, and what happens to ships still owned after year $p$. We take
it that a ship sold in year $i$ is sold at the start of the year and earns
nothing, and that any ships left at the end are sold at price $s_p$. Other
conventions change the arcs slightly and nothing else.
\end{remark}

\section{Bipartite matching, again}
\label{sec:mcf-matching}
%==============================================================================

Costs turn a problem we have already solved into a more useful one. Maximum
bipartite matching becomes the assignment problem: not just \emph{can}
everybody be matched, but at what cost.

\begin{definition}
A \emph{matching} in an undirected graph is a set of edges no two of which share
an endpoint. A graph is \emph{bipartite} if its vertices split into two parts
$U$ and $W$ with every edge having one endpoint in each.
\end{definition}

\Cref{sec:flow-transformations} already turned maximum bipartite matching into a
maximum flow problem: orient the edges from $U$ to $W$, give everything capacity
$1$, add $s \to u$ and $w \to t$. Integrality
(\cref{cor:flow-integrality}) is what makes the answer a matching rather than a
fractional gesture, so the algorithm has to be one that returns an integral
flow.

Costs extend the trick considerably.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Minimum cost perfect matching} in a bipartite graph: give each
        edge its weight as a cost, and ask for a minimum cost flow of value
        $|U|$.
  \item \textbf{$b$-matchings}: given $b : V \to \N$, find an edge set in which
        each vertex $v$ is an endpoint of exactly $b(v)$ edges. Set the capacity
        of $(s,u)$ to $b(u)$ and of $(w,t)$ to $b(w)$. Lower and upper bounds
        per vertex work the same way, via \cref{sec:flow-lower-bounds}.
\end{itemize}

The flow formulation also proves things, with no algorithm in sight.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:mcf-regular-bipartite}
Every $d$-regular bipartite graph with $d \ge 1$ has a perfect matching.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let the parts be $U$ and $W$; counting edges from each side gives
$d|U| = d|W|$, so $|U| = |W| = n/2$. Build the flow model above and consider the
\emph{fractional} flow that puts $1$ on every arc out of $s$, $1$ on every arc
into $t$, and $1/d$ on every arc corresponding to an edge. Capacities hold since
$1/d \le 1$, and conservation holds at each $u \in U$ because $u$ has exactly
$d$ incident edges each carrying $1/d$, totalling $1$; likewise at each $w$. Its
value is $n/2$, which is the maximum possible, since the arcs out of $s$ form a
cut of capacity $n/2$.

So the maximum flow value is $n/2$. By \cref{cor:flow-integrality} there is an
\emph{integral} flow of value $n/2$, and an integral flow of value $n/2$ in this
network is precisely a perfect matching.
\end{proof}

\begin{intuition}
This is the same move as \cref{ch:stable-marriage}: an existence theorem proved
by exhibiting an object and appealing to a general principle, rather than by
construction. Here the object is a fractional flow that is obviously feasible
and obviously optimal, and the general principle is integrality, which upgrades
it to a combinatorial solution at no cost. Matching in general --- non-bipartite
--- graphs does not reduce to flow, and needs a genuinely new idea; that is
\cref{ch:matching}.
\end{intuition}

practitioner's one --- and, again, the source of several exercises here.

\section{Exercises}
\label{sec:ex-mcf}
%==============================================================================

\begin{exercise}
Show how to find a minimum mean cost cycle in $\bigoh{nm}$ time. Hint: let
$D_k(v)$ be the minimum cost of a walk of exactly $k$ arcs from a fixed vertex
to $v$, and consider $\max_k \frac{D_n(v)-D_k(v)}{n-k}$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Give an instance on which cycle cancelling, with an adversarial choice of
cycle, takes a number of iterations exponential in the input size.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Prove that in \cref{lem:mcf-bound-capacities} the replacement capacity is large
enough, filling in the details of the decomposition argument.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Vertex potentials: given $\pi : V \to \R$, define the reduced cost of an arc
$(u,v)$ to be $\cost(u,v) + \pi(u) - \pi(v)$. Show that reduced costs leave the
cost of every cycle unchanged, and that if $\pi(v)$ is the shortest-path
distance from $s$ in $G_f$ then all reduced costs in $G_f$ are non-negative.
Conclude that successive shortest paths can use Dijkstra after its first
iteration.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
In the hopping aeroplane model, suppose the fares are such that some
$f_{ij} < 0$ --- the airline is paid to \emph{not} carry certain passengers.
Does the model still work? What changes?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Rework \cref{sec:mcf-ships} under the convention that a ship sold in year $i$
still earns $r_i$ for that year. Which arcs change?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
In \cref{sec:mcf-ships} we assumed $h_i > r_i$. Where is that used? What goes
wrong with the model --- and with the problem --- if $h_i < r_i$ for some year?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Model the following as a minimum cost flow problem: a factory must meet a known
demand $d(i)$ in each of $p$ months, can produce up to $u$ units per month at
cost $c_i$ per unit, and can store units between months at a cost of $w$ per
unit per month. Storage is unlimited.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Prove that a bipartite graph with all degrees equal to $d$ has its edge set
partitioned into $d$ perfect matchings. Hint: apply
\cref{thm:mcf-regular-bipartite} repeatedly.
\end{exercise}
