\chapter{Randomized Algorithms}
\label{ch:randomized}

Every algorithm so far has been told exactly what to do. This chapter lets them
flip coins, and the effect is out of all proportion to the licence granted. A
random guess makes the $k$-path problem fixed-parameter tractable; a random
starting point turns a local search into the best simple algorithm known for
$k$-SAT; a random vertex solves feedback vertex set in $\Ostar(4^k)$ with an
algorithm that fits on half a page. In each case the deterministic version is
either much harder to design or unknown.

The price is that the answers are sometimes wrong --- but only in one
direction, and only with a probability we control.

\begin{goals}
  \goalitem{define a one-sided error algorithm, and amplify its success
        probability by repetition}
  \goalitem{use Stirling's approximation in the form $n! \approx (n/e)^n$}
  \poolgoal{define the \lang{Directed $k$-Path} problem and acyclic graphs, and
        show that \lang{Directed $k$-Path} is solvable in polynomial time on
        acyclic graphs}
  \poolgoal{give a randomized constant one-sided error algorithm for
        \lang{Directed $k$-Path} with running time $\Ostar(k^k)$}
  \goalitem{improve it to $\Ostar((2e)^k)$ using colourful paths and dynamic
        programming over colour sets}
  \goalitem{give Sch\"oning's algorithm for $k$-SAT and analyse its running
        time}
  \goalitem{give the $\Ostar(4^k)$ randomized algorithm for \lang{Feedback
        Vertex Set}, and prove the lemma about multigraphs of minimum degree
        three that it rests on}
  \poolgoal{define \lang{2-List Coloring} and show that it can be solved in
        polynomial time}
  \poolgoal{using the previous item, describe a randomized constant one-sided
        error algorithm for \lang{3-Coloring} with running time
        $\bigoh{1.5^n}$}
\end{goals}

\section{One-sided error}
\label{sec:one-sided-error}
%==============================================================================

Every algorithm in this chapter has the same contract, and it is worth stating
once.

\begin{definition}[Constant one-sided error]\label{def:one-sided-error}
A randomized algorithm for a decision problem has \emph{constant one-sided
error} if
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item on a no-instance it always answers \textsc{no} --- there are no false
        positives;
  \item on a yes-instance it answers \textsc{yes} with probability at least
        some constant $c > 0$.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

Such algorithms are called \emph{Monte Carlo}. The asymmetry is what makes them
usable: a \textsc{yes} is a fact, because the algorithm found something and can
show it to you, while a \textsc{no} is only evidence. And the evidence
strengthens as fast as you like.

\begin{lemma}[Amplification]\label{lem:amplification}
Running an algorithm with one-sided success probability $p$ independently
$t$ times and answering \textsc{yes} if any run does gives success probability
at least $1 - (1-p)^t$. In particular $t = 1/p$ repetitions give success
probability at least $1 - 1/e > 0.63$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The runs are independent, so all $t$ fail with probability $(1-p)^{t}$. For the
second claim use $1 + x \le e^x$ with $x = -p$:
\[ (1-p)^{1/p} \;\le\; \bigl(e^{-p}\bigr)^{1/p} \;=\; e^{-1}. \qedhere \]
\end{proof}

The inequality $1+x \le e^{x}$ is used four times in this chapter; it is worth
committing to memory. So is the following, in the crude form on the right.

\begin{proposition}[Stirling's approximation]\label{prop:stirling}
$\sqrt{2\pi n}\,(n/e)^n \le n! \le e\sqrt{n}\,(n/e)^n$, and therefore
$n! = \Ostar\bigl((n/e)^n\bigr)$.
\end{proposition}

Under $\Ostar$ the polynomial factors vanish and only $(n/e)^n$ survives, which
is all we shall need.

\section{Colour coding}
\label{sec:colour-coding}
%==============================================================================

\begin{probdef}{Directed $k$-Path}
  \instance A directed graph $G$ and an integer $k$.
  \parameter $k$.
  \question Does $G$ contain a simple directed path on at least $k$ vertices?
\end{probdef}

The word \emph{simple} is the whole problem. Without it --- a \emph{walk} on $k$
vertices, repetitions allowed --- the question is a two-line dynamic program. It
is remembering which vertices have already been used that costs, and remembering
a subset of $V$ costs $2^n$.

\subsection{The acyclic case is easy}

\begin{definition}\label{def:acyclic}
A directed graph is \emph{acyclic} --- a DAG --- if it contains no directed
cycle. Equivalently, its vertices admit a \emph{topological order}
$v_1, \ldots, v_n$ in which every arc goes forwards, from $v_i$ to $v_j$ with
$i < j$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:kpath-dag}
\lang{Directed $k$-Path} is solvable in $\bigoh{n+m}$ time on acyclic graphs.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
In a DAG every walk is automatically a simple path: an arc always increases the
position in the topological order, so no vertex can recur. The constraint that
made the problem hard has evaporated.

Compute a topological order, and let $L[v]$ be the largest number of vertices on
a path ending at $v$. Processing the vertices in topological order,
\[ L[v] \;=\; 1 + \max\bigl\{\, L[u] \;:\; (u,v) \text{ an arc} \,\bigr\}, \]
with the maximum over the empty set read as $0$. Answer \textsc{yes} if some
$L[v] \ge k$. Every arc is examined once.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Forcing the graph to be acyclic}

The idea is to guess, and to guess in a way that makes the general graph
acyclic.

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
\caption{Colour coding for \lang{Directed $k$-Path}, first version}
\label{alg:colour-coding-1}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
  \For{$k^k$ rounds}
    \State choose $c(v) \in \{1,\ldots,k\}$ independently and uniformly at
           random, for every $v \in V$
    \State let $G'$ keep only the arcs $(u,v)$ of $G$ with $c(v) = c(u) + 1$
    \If{$G'$ has a path on $k$ vertices} \Return \textsc{yes} \EndIf
  \EndFor
  \State \Return \textsc{no}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:colour-coding-1}
\Cref{alg:colour-coding-1} has constant one-sided error and runs in
$\Ostar(k^k)$ time.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{$G'$ is acyclic, and line~4 is polynomial.} Along an arc of $G'$ the
label strictly increases, so no directed cycle survives; apply
\cref{prop:kpath-dag}. Moreover a path on $k$ vertices in $G'$ has labels
$c, c+1, \ldots, c+k-1$, which are $k$ distinct values in $\{1,\ldots,k\}$, so
the labels are exactly $1,\ldots,k$ in order.

\emph{No false positives.} A path of $G'$ is a path of $G$, since $G'$ only
deletes arcs, and its $k$ vertices carry distinct labels and so are distinct.

\emph{Success probability.} Suppose $G$ has a $k$-path $p_1, \ldots, p_k$. If
the random labelling happens to give $c(p_i) = i$ for every $i$, then all $k-1$
arcs of the path survive in $G'$ and line~4 finds a $k$-path. This happens with
probability at least
\[ \Pr\bigl[\, \forall i:\, c(p_i) = i \,\bigr] \;=\;
   \Bigl(\frac1k\Bigr)^{k}. \]
By \cref{lem:amplification} with $p = k^{-k}$, the $k^k$ repetitions bring the
failure probability below $1/e$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Colourful paths}

Insisting that the path's labels be $1, 2, \ldots, k$ \emph{in order} is more
than we need. All we want is that the $k$ vertices be distinguishable, and for
that it is enough that their labels be distinct in any order. That is a much
likelier event.

\begin{definition}\label{def:colourful}
Given $c : V \to \{1,\ldots,k\}$, a $k$-path $p_1,\ldots,p_k$ is
\emph{colourful} if $c(p_i) \ne c(p_j)$ for $i \ne j$ --- equivalently, if the
path uses every colour exactly once.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:colourful-prob}
For a fixed $k$-path, a uniformly random colouring makes it colourful with
probability $k!/k^k \ge e^{-k}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
There are $k^k$ colourings of the path's vertices and $k!$ of them are
injective. By \cref{prop:stirling}, $k! \ge \sqrt{2\pi k}(k/e)^k \ge (k/e)^k$,
so $k!/k^k \ge e^{-k}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:colourful-dp}
Given a colouring $c$, a colourful $k$-path can be found in $\Ostar(2^k)$ time.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Dynamic programming over \emph{colour sets} rather than vertex sets --- there
are only $2^k$ of the former. For $v \in V$ and $S \subseteq \{1,\ldots,k\}$,
let $T[v,S]$ be true when there is a path ending at $v$ whose vertices use
exactly the colours of $S$, each once. Then $T[v,\{c(v)\}]$ is true for every
$v$, and
\[ T[v,S] \;=\; \bigvee_{(u,v) \text{ an arc}}
   T\bigl[u,\, S \setminus \{c(v)\}\bigr]
   \qquad \text{when } c(v) \in S, \]
and false otherwise. The answer is whether $T[v,\{1,\ldots,k\}]$ holds for some
$v$. The table has $n2^k$ entries and each costs $\bigoh{\deg v}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[\citet{AlonYusterZwick1995}]\label{thm:colour-coding-2}
\lang{Directed $k$-Path} has a constant one-sided error algorithm running in
$\Ostar\bigl((2e)^k\bigr)$ time.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Repeat $e^k$ times: colour randomly and run \cref{lem:colourful-dp}. A colourful
path is in particular simple, so there are no false positives. By
\cref{lem:colourful-prob} each round succeeds with probability at least
$e^{-k}$, so by \cref{lem:amplification} the failure probability is below
$1/e$. The cost is $e^k \cdot \Ostar(2^k) = \Ostar((2e)^k)$, and
$2e < 5.44$.
\end{proof}

\begin{intuition}
Compare the two algorithms. Both guess a colouring; the difference is what they
demand of it. The first wants the path's colours to come out in a prescribed
order, which happens with probability $k^{-k}$ and buys a polynomial-time
subroutine. The second wants only distinctness, which happens with probability
$e^{-k}$ --- exponentially likelier --- and pays for it with a $2^k$ subroutine.
The product is what matters, and the trade is worth making: $k^k$ against
$(2e)^k$ is $10^{20}$ against $10^7$ at $k = 10$.
\end{intuition}

\section{Sch\"oning's algorithm}
\label{sec:schoening}
%==============================================================================

Randomness helps outside the parameterized world too. Recall from
\cref{ch:exact-i} the local search subroutine for satisfiability.

\begin{lemma}[\Cref{ch:exact-i}]\label{lem:hamming-ball}
Given a $k$-CNF formula $F$ and a truth assignment $a$, one can decide in
$\Ostar(k^d)$ time whether $F$ has a satisfying assignment at Hamming distance
at most $d$ from $a$.
\end{lemma}

The deterministic algorithm of \cref{ch:exact-i} applied this at the two
assignments \textsc{all-true} and \textsc{all-false}, taking $d = n/2$, and got
$\Ostar(3^{n/2}) = \bigoh{1.7321^n}$ for $3$-SAT. Randomness does better simply
by choosing the centre at random and taking a much smaller radius.

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
\caption{Sch\"oning's algorithm for $k$-SAT}
\label{alg:schoening}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
  \State $d \gets n/(k+1)$
  \For{enough rounds}
    \State choose $a \in \{0,1\}^n$ uniformly at random
    \If{\cref{lem:hamming-ball} finds a satisfying assignment within distance
        $d$ of $a$} \Return \textsc{yes} \EndIf
  \EndFor
  \State \Return \textsc{no}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{theorem}[\citet{Schoening1999}]\label{thm:schoening}
\Cref{alg:schoening} decides $k$-SAT with constant one-sided error in
\[ \Ostar\Bigl( \Bigl(\frac{2k}{k+1}\Bigr)^{\!n} \Bigr) \text{ time.} \]
For $k = 3$ this is $\bigoh{1.5^n}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
There are no false positives, because \cref{lem:hamming-ball} returns an actual
satisfying assignment.

Fix a satisfying assignment $y$. A uniformly random $a$ lands within Hamming
distance $d$ of $y$ with probability
\[ p \;=\; 2^{-n}\sum_{i \le d}\binom{n}{i}, \]
so $1/p$ repetitions suffice by \cref{lem:amplification}, and the total cost is
$\Ostar(k^{d}/p)$. Writing $d = \alpha n$ and using the standard estimate
$\sum_{i\le \alpha n}\binom{n}{i} = \Ostar(2^{H(\alpha)n})$, where
$H(\alpha) = -\alpha\log_2\alpha - (1-\alpha)\log_2(1-\alpha)$ is the binary
entropy, the running time is
\[ \Ostar\Bigl( \bigl( 2^{\,1 - H(\alpha)} \, k^{\alpha} \bigr)^{n} \Bigr), \]
and this expression is minimised at $\alpha = 1/(k+1)$, where it equals
$\bigl(2k/(k+1)\bigr)^n$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}\label{ex:schoening-numbers}
For $k = 3$ the bound is $(6/4)^n = 1.5^n$, against $1.7321^n$ for the
deterministic version of \cref{ch:exact-i} and $1.8393^n$ for plain branching.
For $k = 100$ it is $(200/101)^n = 1.9802^n$ --- still below $2^n$, which is
the point: the saving degrades as $k$ grows but never disappears.
\Cref{ch:lower-bounds} explains why it must degrade.
\end{example}

\section{Feedback vertex set}
\label{sec:fvs}
%==============================================================================

The last algorithm of the chapter is the one where the randomness looks most
like magic: it picks a vertex essentially at random and deletes it.

\begin{probdef}{Feedback Vertex Set}
  \instance A multigraph $G = (V,E)$ and an integer $k$.
  \parameter $k$.
  \question Is there a set $X$ of at most $k$ vertices such that $G - X$ is a
  forest? Equivalently, such that every cycle of $G$ contains a vertex of $X$?
\end{probdef}

We work with multigraphs, where two parallel edges form a cycle and a loop
forms a cycle by itself. Degrees count parallel edges separately and a loop
counts twice.

Three reduction rules put the graph in shape.

\begin{observation}\label{obs:fvs-reductions}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item A vertex of degree $0$ or $1$ lies on no cycle and may be deleted.
  \item A vertex $v$ of degree $2$ may be \emph{suppressed}: delete $v$ and add
        an edge between its two neighbours. Every cycle through $v$ becomes a
        cycle through the new edge and conversely.
  \item If there is a loop at $v$, then $v$ is in every solution: put it in the
        solution, delete it, and decrease $k$ by one.
\end{enumerate}
\end{observation}

After applying these exhaustively, every vertex has degree at least three. That
is exactly the hypothesis of the following lemma, which is the heart of the
matter.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:fvs-half-edges}
Let $G$ be a multigraph of minimum degree at least $3$ and let $X$ be a feedback
vertex set of $G$. Then at least half the edges of $G$ have an endpoint in $X$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write $F = V \setminus X$, which induces a forest. Let $I$ be the set of edges
with at least one endpoint in $X$, and $E_F$ the set of edges with both
endpoints in $F$. These partition $E$, so it suffices to show
$|I| \ge |E_F|$.

For $v \in F$ let $d_F(v)$ be the number of edges from $v$ into $F$. Since
$\deg(v) \ge 3$, the vertex $v$ sends at least $3 - d_F(v)$ edges into $X$, and
every such edge lies in $I$ and is counted at only one end. Hence
\[ |I| \;\ge\; \sum_{v \in F} \max\bigl(0,\, 3 - d_F(v)\bigr)
       \;\ge\; 2\,|F_{\le 1}| + |F_2|, \]
where $F_{\le 1}$, $F_2$ and $F_{\ge 3}$ are the vertices of $F$ with
$d_F(v) \le 1$, $=2$ and $\ge 3$ respectively.

Now use that $F$ is a forest. In any forest the vertices of degree at least
three are strictly fewer than those of degree at most one: root each tree and
induct, replacing a lowest branch vertex and everything below it by a single
leaf. So $|F_{\ge 3}| < |F_{\le 1}|$ and
\[ |I| \;\ge\; 2|F_{\le 1}| + |F_2|
       \;>\; |F_{\le 1}| + |F_2| + |F_{\ge 3}| \;=\; |F| \;>\; |E_F|, \]
the last step because a forest has fewer edges than vertices.
\end{proof}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\figplaceholder{5cm}{The vertex set split into $X$ on the left and the forest
$F$ on the right; the edges of $F$ drawn thin, the edges from $F$ to $X$ drawn
thick, with a leaf of $F$ shown sending two thick edges into $X$ because its
degree in $G$ is at least three.}
\caption{\Cref{lem:fvs-half-edges}. Minimum degree three forces every leaf of
the forest to send two edges into $X$, and a forest has more leaves than branch
vertices.}
\label{fig:fvs-lemma}
\end{figure}

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
\caption{Randomized \lang{Feedback Vertex Set}}
\label{alg:fvs}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
  \Function{FVS}{$G$, $k$}
    \State apply \cref{obs:fvs-reductions} exhaustively, adjusting $k$
    \If{$G$ has no edges} \Return \textsc{yes} \EndIf
    \If{$k = 0$} \Return \textsc{no} \EndIf
    \State pick an edge of $G$ uniformly at random, then one of its two
           endpoints uniformly at random; call it $v$
    \State \Return \Call{FVS}{$G - v$, $k-1$}
  \EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:fvs}
\Cref{alg:fvs} has no false positives, and on a yes-instance it answers
\textsc{yes} with probability at least $4^{-k}$. Repeating it $4^k$ times gives
a constant one-sided error algorithm running in $\Ostar(4^k)$ time.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The reduction rules are sound, and the algorithm only ever answers \textsc{yes}
when the graph really has become a forest, so there are no false positives.

For the success probability, induct on $k$. For $k = 0$ the claim is trivial.
For $k > 0$, let $X$ be a feedback vertex set of size $k$. After the reduction
rules the minimum degree is at least $3$, so by \cref{lem:fvs-half-edges} at
least half the edges have an endpoint in $X$; the random edge is one of them
with probability at least $1/2$, and the random endpoint is in $X$ with
probability at least $1/2$ given that. So $\Pr[v \in X] \ge 1/4$. Conditioned on
that event, $X \setminus \{v\}$ is a feedback vertex set of $G - v$ of size
$k-1$, and the induction hypothesis gives success probability at least
$4^{-(k-1)}$ from there. Multiplying,
\[ \Pr[\textsc{yes}] \;\ge\; \tfrac14 \cdot 4^{-(k-1)} \;=\; 4^{-k}. \]
Finally apply \cref{lem:amplification} with $p = 4^{-k}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{intuition}
Look at what the lemma is really doing. Picking a vertex at random would be
hopeless --- the solution might be four vertices out of a million. Picking a
random \emph{edge} and then one of its ends is a biased sample: it favours
high-degree vertices, and the lemma says the solution carries at least half the
edge mass. Sampling proportionally to the right quantity is the whole trick, and
it is the reason the reduction rules exist: they establish minimum degree
three, without which the lemma is false.
\end{intuition}

\section{Three-colouring in \texorpdfstring{$\bigoh{1.5^n}$}{O(1.5 to the n)}}
\label{sec:randomized-3col}
%==============================================================================

The last algorithm is the pool exercise, and it is a two-step argument: solve an
easier colouring problem exactly, then guess your way to it.

\begin{probdef}{2-List Coloring}
  \instance A graph $G = (V,E)$ and, for each vertex $v$, a list
  $L(v)$ of at most two permitted colours.
  \question Is there a proper colouring $c$ with $c(v) \in L(v)$ for every $v$?
\end{probdef}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:2-list-colouring}
\lang{2-List Coloring} is solvable in polynomial time.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
It is $2$-SAT in disguise. A vertex with an empty list makes the instance a
no-instance. A vertex with a list of one colour is forced. For a vertex $v$ with
$L(v) = \{\alpha,\beta\}$ introduce a Boolean variable $x_v$, read as ``$v$ gets
colour $\alpha$'' --- so $\neg x_v$ means $v$ gets $\beta$, and every assignment
of the variables is a list colouring, proper or not.

For each edge $\{u,v\}$ and each colour $\gamma \in L(u) \cap L(v)$, add a
clause forbidding both ends from taking $\gamma$: with $\ell_u^\gamma$ denoting
the literal that says ``$u$ gets $\gamma$'', add
$(\neg \ell_u^{\gamma} \vee \neg \ell_v^{\gamma})$. Each clause has two
literals, and the whole formula has $\bigoh{m}$ clauses. A satisfying assignment
is exactly a proper list colouring, and $2$-SAT is solvable in linear time.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:randomized-3col}
\lang{3-Coloring} has a constant one-sided error algorithm running in
$\bigoh{1.5^n}$ time.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Repeat $(3/2)^n$ times: for each vertex $v$ independently, choose $L(v)$
uniformly at random among the three two-element subsets of $\{1,2,3\}$; then
decide the resulting \lang{2-List Coloring} instance by
\cref{prop:2-list-colouring}. Answer \textsc{yes} if any round succeeds.

\emph{No false positives.} A proper list colouring is in particular a proper
$3$-colouring.

\emph{Success probability.} Suppose $G$ is $3$-colourable and fix a proper
colouring $c$. The round succeeds whenever $c$ is a legal list colouring, that
is whenever $c(v) \in L(v)$ for every $v$. For a single vertex this has
probability $2/3$ --- two of the three lists contain any given colour --- and
the choices are independent, so
\[ \Pr\bigl[\,\forall v:\, c(v) \in L(v)\,\bigr] \;=\;
   \Bigl(\frac23\Bigr)^{\! n}. \]
By \cref{lem:amplification} with $p = (2/3)^n$, the $(3/2)^n$ repetitions leave
failure probability below $1/e$. Each round is polynomial, so the total is
$\Ostar((3/2)^n) = \bigoh{1.5^n}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{intuition}
The step worth admiring is the choice of what to guess. Guessing the colouring
outright costs $3^n$. Guessing, for each vertex, which colour it does
\emph{not} get costs $(3/2)^n$, because it leaves a problem that is still
solvable --- two colours per vertex is $2$-SAT, three is
\textrm{NP}-complete. The art of a randomized algorithm is usually to find the
largest amount of information you can afford to discard while keeping the
remaining problem tractable, and $3$-colouring sits exactly one colour above
that line.
\end{intuition}

Colour coding is \citet{AlonYusterZwick1995}, who also showed how to
derandomise it using families of perfect hash functions, at the cost of a larger
polynomial factor. \Cref{thm:schoening} is \citet{Schoening1999}; the algorithm

\cref{lem:hamming-ball} and achieves the same bound.
\citet[Chapter~5]{CyganEtAl2015} covers colour coding, random separation and the
feedback vertex set algorithm; \citet[Chapter~5]{FominKratsch2010} covers the
exponential-time side.

\section{Exercises}
\label{sec:ex-randomized}
%==============================================================================

\begin{exercise}
An algorithm has one-sided error with success probability $p = 0.01$. How many
repetitions are needed to push the failure probability below $10^{-9}$?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Why is there no useful analogue of \cref{lem:amplification} for an algorithm
that may err in \emph{both} directions with probability $1/2$ each? What is the
right notion of amplification there?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\Cref{alg:colour-coding-1} keeps the arcs with $c(v) = c(u)+1$. What happens if
we keep the arcs with $c(v) > c(u)$ instead? Is the graph still acyclic, and
what does that do to the analysis?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Adapt \cref{lem:colourful-dp} to find a colourful path of exactly $k$ vertices
\emph{between two given vertices} $s$ and $t$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
