\chapter{Maximum Flow and Minimum Cut}
\label{ch:max-flow}

Flows are the workhorse of combinatorial optimisation. The reason is not that
routing material through a network is a common problem --- it is, but that is
not the point. The reason is that a surprising number of problems that mention
no network at all become easy the moment they are phrased as flow problems, and
that the phrasing is usually short. This chapter develops the theory, the
standard algorithms, and --- at greatest length, because it is what the
exercises and the exam actually ask for --- the modelling.

\section*{Learning goals}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Learning goals}

After this chapter you should be able to:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item define flow networks, feasible flows, $s$--$t$ cuts and the value of a
        flow;
  \item construct the residual graph and prove that augmenting along a path
        yields a feasible flow of larger value;
  \item prove the max-flow min-cut theorem and derive the integrality theorem
        from it;
  \item state and compare Ford--Fulkerson, Edmonds--Karp and Dinic's
        algorithm, and analyse their running times;
  \item define preflows, excesses and height functions, state the height
        invariant, and explain why it forces the final preflow to be a maximum
        flow;
  \item state the push and lift operations and verify that each preserves the
        invariant;
  \item bound the number of lifts, of saturating pushes and of non-saturating
        pushes, and conclude that generic preflow-push runs in $\bigoh{n^2m}$
        time;
  \item state Goldberg's highest-label rule and prove the $\bigoh{n^3}$ bound;
  \item solve the maximum flow problem with lower bounds on arcs, by reducing
        first to an admissible circulation and then to two ordinary maximum
        flow computations;
  \item model the consistent matrix rounding problem as a circulation with
        lower bounds, and explain why integrality is the crux;
  \item reduce flow problems with vertex capacities, several sources or sinks,
        or lower bounds on arcs to plain maximum flow, and argue that the
        reduction is correct and does not blow up the instance;
  \item derive Menger's theorem in both its edge and vertex form;
  \item model a disjoint-paths problem as a flow problem and analyse the
        resulting running time in the parameters the problem is stated in.
\end{itemize}

\section{Flow networks}
\label{sec:flow-networks}
%==============================================================================

\begin{definition}[Flow network]
A \emph{flow network} is a directed graph $G = (V,E)$ together with a capacity
function $c : E \to \R_{\ge 0}$ and two distinguished vertices, the
\emph{source} $s$ and the \emph{sink} $t$, with $s \ne t$.
\end{definition}

We write $n = |V|$ and $m = |E|$, and we assume throughout that every vertex
lies on some $s$--$t$ path; vertices that do not can be deleted without
changing anything. We allow both $(u,v)$ and $(v,u)$ to be arcs.

\begin{definition}[Flow]
A \emph{flow} in $G$ is a function $f : E \to \R_{\ge 0}$ satisfying
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{capacity constraints}: $0 \le f(e) \le c(e)$ for every
        $e \in E$; and
  \item \emph{flow conservation}: for every $v \in V \setminus \{s,t\}$,
        \[ \sum_{e \text{ into } v} f(e) \;=\; \sum_{e \text{ out of } v} f(e). \]
\end{itemize}
The \emph{value} of $f$ is the net amount leaving the source,
\[ |f| \;=\; \sum_{e \text{ out of } s} f(e) \;-\; \sum_{e \text{ into } s} f(e). \]
\end{definition}

A flow of maximum value is called a \emph{maximum flow}. Note that a maximum
flow exists: the set of feasible flows is a non-empty compact subset of
$\R^m$ and $|\cdot|$ is continuous on it. That is a soft argument and it tells
us nothing about how to find one; the whole content of this chapter is that
there is a combinatorial route to the same conclusion which also computes the
answer.

The other half of the story is cuts.

\begin{definition}[$s$--$t$ cut]
An \emph{$s$--$t$ cut} is a partition $V = S \,\dot\cup\, T$ with $s \in S$ and
$t \in T$. Its \emph{capacity} is
\[ c(S,T) \;=\; \sum_{\substack{e = (u,v) \in E \\ u \in S,\, v \in T}} c(e), \]
the total capacity of the arcs crossing from $S$ to $T$. Arcs running from $T$
back to $S$ do not contribute.
\end{definition}

The asymmetry in that definition --- forward arcs count, backward arcs do not
--- is not a convention we could have chosen differently. It is what makes the
next lemma true.

\begin{lemma}[Flow across a cut]\label{lem:flow-across-cut}
Let $f$ be a flow and $(S,T)$ an $s$--$t$ cut. Then
\[ |f| \;=\; \sum_{\substack{e=(u,v)\\ u \in S, v \in T}} f(e)
        \;-\; \sum_{\substack{e=(v,u)\\ v \in T, u \in S}} f(e). \]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For a vertex $v$ write $\mathrm{net}(v)$ for the flow out of $v$ minus the flow
into $v$. By conservation $\mathrm{net}(v) = 0$ for every
$v \notin \{s,t\}$, and $\mathrm{net}(s) = |f|$ by definition. Hence
\[ |f| \;=\; \sum_{v \in S} \mathrm{net}(v). \]
Now expand the right-hand side arc by arc. An arc with both endpoints in $S$
contributes $+f(e)$ at its tail and $-f(e)$ at its head, so it cancels. An arc
from $S$ to $T$ contributes $+f(e)$ and nothing else; an arc from $T$ to $S$
contributes $-f(e)$ and nothing else. Arcs with both endpoints in $T$ do not
appear. That is exactly the claimed expression.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Weak duality]\label{cor:flow-weak-duality}
For every flow $f$ and every $s$--$t$ cut $(S,T)$ we have $|f| \le c(S,T)$.
Consequently the maximum flow value is at most the minimum cut capacity.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
In \cref{lem:flow-across-cut} the first sum is at most $c(S,T)$ by the capacity
constraints and the second is non-negative.
\end{proof}

\begin{intuition}
Weak duality is the half of the theory that costs nothing, and it is already
useful: any cut you can point at is a certificate that no flow beats its
capacity. The work in this chapter goes into the other half --- that the bound
is always tight. Once we know that, a maximum flow and a minimum cut certify
each other, and we can stop computing as soon as we see a matching pair.
\end{intuition}

\section{Residual graphs and augmenting paths}
\label{sec:flow-residual}
%==============================================================================

The naive way to look for a better flow is to push more along some $s$--$t$
path with spare capacity. That works until it does not: a flow can be
non-maximum and yet admit no such path, because getting more out of the network
requires \emph{undoing} part of what has already been routed. The residual
graph is the bookkeeping device that makes ``undo'' look like ``push''.

\begin{definition}[Residual graph]\label{def:flow-residual}
Let $f$ be a flow in $G$. The \emph{residual graph} $G_f$ has vertex set $V$
and, for every arc $e = (u,v) \in E$,
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item a \emph{forward} arc $(u,v)$ of residual capacity $c(e) - f(e)$,
        present when $f(e) < c(e)$; and
  \item a \emph{backward} arc $(v,u)$ of residual capacity $f(e)$, present
        when $f(e) > 0$.
\end{itemize}
An \emph{augmenting path} is a directed $s$--$t$ path in $G_f$. Its
\emph{bottleneck} is the smallest residual capacity along it.
\end{definition}

Note that $G_f$ has at most $2m$ arcs, so it is no bigger than $G$ up to a
constant factor, and it can be built in $\bigoh{m}$ time.

\begin{lemma}[Augmentation]\label{lem:flow-augment}
Let $f$ be a flow, let $P$ be an augmenting path in $G_f$ with bottleneck
$\delta > 0$, and define $f'$ by sending $\delta$ along $P$: for each arc of
$P$, add $\delta$ to $f(e)$ if the arc is forward, and subtract $\delta$ from
$f(e)$ if it is backward. Then $f'$ is a flow and $|f'| = |f| + \delta$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Capacity.} A forward arc of $P$ has $c(e) - f(e) \ge \delta$, so
$f'(e) = f(e) + \delta \le c(e)$; a backward arc has $f(e) \ge \delta$, so
$f'(e) = f(e) - \delta \ge 0$. Arcs not on $P$ are unchanged.

\emph{Conservation.} Let $v \notin \{s,t\}$. If $v$ is not on $P$ nothing
changes. Otherwise $P$ passes through $v$ once --- it is a path --- entering by
one residual arc and leaving by another. Check the four cases. If both are
forward arcs, the flow into $v$ and the flow out of $v$ each rise by $\delta$.
If the entering arc is forward and the leaving arc is backward, the flow into
$v$ rises by $\delta$ along one arc and falls by $\delta$ along another, so the
inflow is unchanged and so is the outflow. The remaining two cases are
symmetric. In every case $\mathrm{net}(v)$ is unchanged, hence still $0$.

\emph{Value.} The same case analysis at $s$ shows that the net flow out of $s$
rises by exactly $\delta$, since $P$ leaves $s$ once and, being a path, never
returns.
\end{proof}

\section{The max-flow min-cut theorem}
\label{sec:flow-maxflow-mincut}
%==============================================================================

\begin{theorem}[Max-flow min-cut]\label{thm:flow-mfmc}
Let $f$ be a flow in $G$. The following are equivalent.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item $f$ is a maximum flow.
  \item $G_f$ contains no augmenting path.
  \item $|f| = c(S,T)$ for some $s$--$t$ cut $(S,T)$.
\end{enumerate}
In particular the maximum value of a flow equals the minimum capacity of an
$s$--$t$ cut.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{(i) $\Rightarrow$ (ii).} If $G_f$ had an augmenting path, its bottleneck
would be positive --- residual arcs have positive residual capacity by
definition --- and \cref{lem:flow-augment} would produce a strictly better
flow.

\emph{(ii) $\Rightarrow$ (iii).} Let $S$ be the set of vertices reachable from
$s$ by a directed path in $G_f$, and let $T = V \setminus S$. Then $s \in S$,
and $t \in T$ precisely because there is no augmenting path, so $(S,T)$ is an
$s$--$t$ cut. Consider an arc $e = (u,v) \in E$ with $u \in S$ and $v \in T$.
If $f(e) < c(e)$ then $G_f$ would contain the forward arc $(u,v)$ and $v$ would
be reachable, a contradiction; hence $f(e) = c(e)$. Now consider an arc
$e = (v,u) \in E$ with $v \in T$ and $u \in S$. If $f(e) > 0$ then $G_f$ would
contain the backward arc $(u,v)$ and again $v$ would be reachable; hence
$f(e) = 0$. Substituting both into \cref{lem:flow-across-cut},
\[ |f| \;=\; \sum_{\substack{e=(u,v)\\ u\in S, v\in T}} c(e) \;-\; 0
       \;=\; c(S,T). \]

\emph{(iii) $\Rightarrow$ (i).} By \cref{cor:flow-weak-duality} no flow exceeds
$c(S,T)$, and $f$ attains it.
\end{proof}

\begin{intuition}
The proof of (ii) $\Rightarrow$ (iii) is the whole theorem, and it is worth
remembering in one sentence: \emph{when you are stuck, the set of vertices you
can still reach is a minimum cut}. Nothing is guessed. The cut is read off the
failed search, which is also why every implementation gets a minimum cut for
free by running one last breadth-first search after the flow is maximum.
\end{intuition}

\begin{corollary}[Integrality]\label{cor:flow-integrality}
If all capacities are integers, then there is a maximum flow $f$ with $f(e)$ an
integer for every arc $e$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Start from the zero flow, which is integral, and repeatedly augment. If $f$ is
integral then every residual capacity is an integer, so the bottleneck of any
augmenting path is a positive integer and the augmented flow is again integral.
Each augmentation raises the value by at least $1$, and the value is bounded
above by any cut capacity, so the process stops after finitely many steps at a
flow with no augmenting path, which is maximum by \cref{thm:flow-mfmc}.
\end{proof}

Integrality is what makes flows useful for combinatorial problems. When we
model a disjoint-paths question as a flow problem in \cref{sec:flow-grid-escape},
what we actually need is not that a fractional optimum exists but that an
\emph{integral} one does --- half a path is not a path.

\section{Augmenting path algorithms}
\label{sec:flow-algorithms}
%==============================================================================

The proof of \cref{cor:flow-integrality} is already an algorithm.

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
\caption{Ford--Fulkerson}
\label{alg:flow-ff}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
  \State $f \gets 0$
  \While{$G_f$ contains an $s$--$t$ path $P$}
     \State $\delta \gets$ the bottleneck of $P$
     \State augment $f$ by $\delta$ along $P$
  \EndWhile
  \State \Return $f$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:flow-ff}
On integer capacities, \cref{alg:flow-ff} returns a maximum flow after at most
$|f^\ast|$ augmentations, in total time $\bigoh{m \cdot |f^\ast|}$, where
$f^\ast$ is a maximum flow.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Each augmentation raises the value by at least $1$ and costs $\bigoh{m}$: one
graph search to find $P$ and one pass along $P$ to update. Correctness on
termination is \cref{thm:flow-mfmc}.
\end{proof}

That bound is \emph{pseudopolynomial}: it depends on the capacity values, not
just on their encoding length. It is genuinely bad. There is a five-vertex
network with capacities $\{1, C\}$ on which a careless choice of augmenting
paths takes $2C$ augmentations, alternating along a single middle arc, and $C$
may be astronomically large while the input is short.

\begin{remark}
With irrational capacities Ford--Fulkerson can fail outright: there are
networks and choices of augmenting path on which it runs forever, and worse,
converges to a value that is not the maximum. So the choice of path is not a
performance detail. It is what makes the algorithm an algorithm.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Edmonds--Karp: always take a shortest path (non-examinable)}
\nonexam{Edmonds--Karp is assumed background from the bachelor algorithms
course rather than taught here, and the lecture does not recap it. The analysis
below is included because the two lemmas it turns on --- distances never
decrease, and each arc is critical rarely --- are the standard way to argue
that an augmenting-path rule terminates quickly, and the same shape of argument
recurs.}

\begin{theorem}[\citet{EdmondsKarp1972}]\label{thm:flow-ek}
If every augmenting path is chosen to have the fewest arcs --- that is, found
by breadth-first search in $G_f$ --- then the number of augmentations is at
most $nm/2$, and the total running time is $\bigoh{nm^2}$, independent of the
capacities.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Write $d_f(v)$ for the number of arcs on a shortest $s$--$v$ path in $G_f$.

\emph{Step 1: distances never decrease.} Augmenting along a shortest path
cannot create a shortcut. The arcs added to the residual graph by the
augmentation are the reverses of arcs on the path, and reversing an arc of a
shortest path only ever leads from a vertex at distance $d+1$ back to one at
distance $d$; using it can never shorten a route from $s$.

\emph{Step 2: each arc is critical rarely.} Call an arc \emph{critical} on an
augmentation if it is the bottleneck; it then disappears from the residual
graph. Suppose $(u,v)$ is critical, so $d_f(v) = d_f(u) + 1$ at that moment.
For $(u,v)$ to reappear, some later augmenting path must use $(v,u)$, and at
that moment $d_{f'}(u) = d_{f'}(v) + 1$. Since distances never decrease,
$d_{f'}(u) = d_{f'}(v) + 1 \ge d_f(v) + 1 = d_f(u) + 2$. So between two
critical events for the same arc, $d(u)$ grows by at least $2$; as
$d(u) \le n$, each of the $2m$ residual arcs is critical at most $n/2$ times.

Every augmentation makes at least one arc critical, so there are at most
$nm$ augmentations --- $nm/2$ counting the two directions of each arc as one
--- and each costs $\bigoh{m}$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Dinic: augment in phases (non-examinable)}
\nonexam{Not covered in the lecture. It earns its place here for one reason:
the unit-capacity bound at the end of this subsection is what gives the
$\bigoh{n^3}$ running time for the grid escape problem in
\cref{sec:flow-grid-escape}, which \emph{is} examinable.}

Edmonds--Karp throws away work: it recomputes the breadth-first search from
scratch for every single augmenting path, even though many paths of the same
length usually exist at once. Dinic's algorithm groups them.

\begin{definition}[Level graph, blocking flow]
The \emph{level graph} $L_f$ keeps only those arcs $(u,v)$ of $G_f$ with
$d_f(v) = d_f(u)+1$. A flow in $L_f$ is \emph{blocking} if every $s$--$t$ path
in $L_f$ contains at least one saturated arc.
\end{definition}

Dinic's algorithm repeats: build $L_f$, find a blocking flow in it, add it to
$f$. Each such \emph{phase} strictly increases $d_f(t)$, so there are at most
$n$ phases; a blocking flow can be found in $\bigoh{nm}$ time by a
depth-first search that retreats along dead ends and never revisits them. The
total is $\bigoh{n^2m}$.

Two special cases matter for us, because they are the ones that arise from
modelling rather than from genuine capacities:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item with all capacities $1$, Dinic runs in $\bigoh{m^{3/2}}$ time;
  \item on unit-capacity networks in which every vertex has either one
        entering or one leaving arc --- exactly what vertex splitting produces
        in \cref{sec:flow-modelling} --- it runs in $\bigoh{m\sqrt{n}}$ time.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Where this leaves us}

Every algorithm in this section maintains a flow and improves it, and every one
of them pays $\bigoh{m}$ per unit of progress because an augmenting path is a
global object. \Cref{sec:flow-preflow-push} takes the opposite approach and does
better on dense graphs. It is also the algorithm this course examines, so read
it with more attention than this section: of the material above, only the
Ford--Fulkerson method and the max-flow min-cut theorem are examinable.

\section{Preflow-push}
\label{sec:flow-preflow-push}
%==============================================================================

Every algorithm so far maintains a flow and improves it. That is a strong
invariant, and it is expensive: a single augmentation has to travel the whole
way from $s$ to $t$ before anything is gained, and each such journey costs
$\bigoh{m}$. Preflow-push --- also called push-relabel --- gives up the
invariant. It maintains something weaker than a flow, moves material one arc at
a time with no global plan, and only at the very end does the object it is
holding become a flow. When it does, it is automatically maximum.

\begin{definition}[Preflow, excess]\label{def:flow-preflow}
A \emph{preflow} is a function $f : E \to \R_{\ge 0}$ satisfying the capacity
constraints $0 \le f(e) \le c(e)$ together with the weaker
\emph{non-deficiency} condition: for every $v \in V \setminus \{s\}$, the
\emph{excess}
\[ e_f(v) \;=\; \sum_{e \text{ into } v} f(e) \;-\; \sum_{e \text{ out of } v} f(e) \]
is non-negative. A vertex $v \notin \{s,t\}$ with $e_f(v) > 0$ is
\emph{overflowing}. The residual network $G_f$ is defined exactly as for flows
(\cref{def:flow-residual}).
\end{definition}

So material may pile up at a vertex, but never go missing. A preflow with no
overflowing vertex is precisely a flow: conservation is exactly the statement
that every excess is zero. The source and the sink are exempt --- they produce
and consume.

\subsection{Heights, and why they are the whole idea}

The algorithm needs to know which way is downhill, and it needs to know it
locally, since it never looks at the whole graph at once. It maintains a
\emph{height} $h(v) \in \N$ for every vertex, fixed at
\[ h(s) = n, \qquad h(t) = 0, \]
with all other vertices starting at $0$, and it maintains one invariant.

\begin{definition}[Height invariant]\label{def:flow-height-invariant}
A height function $h$ is \emph{valid} for a preflow $f$ if
\[ h(u) \;\le\; h(v) + 1 \qquad \text{for every arc } (u,v) \in G_f. \]
\end{definition}

In words: \emph{in the residual network you may step down by at most one}. You
may step up as far as you like, and you may step sideways; what you may never
do is fall off a cliff. This one condition does all the work, because of the
following.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:flow-no-augmenting}
If $h$ is valid for $f$, then $G_f$ contains no $s$--$t$ path.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Suppose $P$ were such a path, say with $k$ arcs. It is a path, so
$k \le n-1$. Walking along it, the height drops by at most $1$ per arc, so
$h(s) \le h(t) + k \le 0 + n - 1$. But $h(s) = n$, a contradiction.
\end{proof}

\begin{intuition}
This is the trick, and it is worth stating baldly: \emph{the invariant makes the
answer true in advance.} Throughout the run there is no augmenting path, and by
\cref{thm:flow-mfmc} that is exactly the optimality condition --- except that it
is not a flow yet, so the theorem does not apply. All the algorithm has to do,
then, is get rid of the excesses without ever breaking the invariant. The moment
it succeeds it is holding a flow with no augmenting path, which is a maximum
flow. Contrast this with Ford--Fulkerson, which maintains feasibility throughout
and fights for optimality at the end. Preflow-push maintains optimality
throughout and fights for feasibility.
\end{intuition}

\subsection{The two operations}

\paragraph{Initialisation.}
Saturate every arc out of the source: $f(s,v) = c(s,v)$ for all
$(s,v) \in E$, and $f(e) = 0$ elsewhere. Set $h(s) = n$ and $h(v) = 0$
otherwise. This is a preflow --- every neighbour of $s$ has non-negative excess
and nobody else is touched --- and $h$ is valid: arcs not involving $s$ run
between vertices of height $0$, and the arcs of $G_f$ that do involve $s$ all
point \emph{towards} $s$, hence upwards, and going up is always allowed.

\paragraph{Push.}
Applicable to an arc $(u,v) \in G_f$ when $u$ is overflowing and
$h(u) = h(v) + 1$. Send
\[ \delta \;=\; \min\{\, e_f(u),\; c_f(u,v) \,\} \]
along the arc, increasing $e_f(v)$ and decreasing $e_f(u)$ by $\delta$. The
push is \emph{saturating} if $\delta = c_f(u,v)$, and \emph{non-saturating}
otherwise --- in the latter case $u$ stops overflowing.

Heights do not change, so the invariant can only be endangered by a new
residual arc, and the only new arc is $(v,u)$, which goes \emph{up} by one.
Allowed.

\paragraph{Lift.}
Applicable to an overflowing $u \notin \{s,t\}$ when no residual arc out of $u$
goes down, that is, $h(u) \le h(v)$ for every $(u,v) \in G_f$. Raise $u$ to one
above its lowest residual neighbour:
\[ h(u) \;\gets\; 1 + \min\{\, h(v) : (u,v) \in G_f \,\}. \]
The residual network does not change, and by construction every arc out of $u$
still descends by at most one. Arcs \emph{into} $u$ only became easier to
satisfy, since $h(u)$ went up. Allowed.

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
\caption{Generic preflow-push}
\label{alg:flow-preflow-push}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
  \State saturate every arc out of $s$; set $h(s) \gets n$ and
         $h(v) \gets 0$ for $v \ne s$
  \While{some push or lift operation is applicable}
     \State select an applicable operation and perform it
  \EndWhile
  \State \Return $f$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:flow-preflow-push-correct}
If \cref{alg:flow-preflow-push} terminates, it returns a maximum flow.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Both operations preserve the preflow property and the validity of $h$, as
checked above. On termination no operation applies. We claim no vertex is then
overflowing. Suppose $u \notin \{s,t\}$ has $e_f(u) > 0$. By validity, every
residual arc out of $u$ satisfies $h(u) \le h(v)+1$. If some such arc has
$h(u) = h(v)+1$, a push applies; otherwise $h(u) \le h(v)$ for all of them and a
lift applies. Either way an operation was available, a contradiction. Note that
$u$ has at least one outgoing residual arc: its excess arrived somehow, so some
arc into $u$ carries flow, and its reversal is residual.

So all excesses are zero, meaning $f$ is a flow; and by
\cref{lem:flow-no-augmenting} the residual network has no $s$--$t$ path, so by
\cref{thm:flow-mfmc} it is maximum.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Running time}

Three counting arguments, in increasing order of cleverness.

\begin{lemma}[Heights stay bounded]\label{lem:flow-height-bound}
At every moment, $h(u) \le 2n-1$ for every $u$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Heights only change by lifts, and a lift applies only to an overflowing vertex,
so it suffices to bound $h(u)$ when $e_f(u) > 0$. We claim there is then a
simple path from $u$ to $s$ in $G_f$. Indeed, the excess at $u$ arrived from
$s$: decomposing the preflow, some sequence of arcs carrying flow leads from
$s$ to $u$, and the reverse of each is a residual arc, giving a path from $u$
to $s$ in $G_f$. Such a path has at most $n-1$ arcs, and along each the height
drops by at most one, so
\[ h(u) \;\le\; h(s) + (n-1) \;=\; 2n-1. \qedhere \]
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:flow-lift-count}
Each vertex is lifted fewer than $2n$ times, so there are fewer than $2n^2$
lifts in total.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Heights never decrease, every lift raises one by at least $1$, and heights start
at $0$ and never exceed $2n-1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:flow-saturating-count}
There are at most $2nm$ saturating pushes.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Fix an arc $(u,v)$. A saturating push across it removes it from $G_f$. For it to
come back, some push must go across $(v,u)$, which requires $h(v) = h(u)+1$; at
the time of the original push we had $h(u) = h(v)+1$. So between two saturating
pushes across $(u,v)$, the height of $v$ must rise by $2$, and by
\cref{lem:flow-height-bound} that can happen fewer than $n$ times. Summing over
the at most $2m$ arcs of the residual network gives the bound.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:flow-nonsaturating-count}
There are $\bigoh{n^2m}$ non-saturating pushes.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Use the potential
\[ \Phi \;=\; \sum_{v \text{ overflowing}} h(v), \]
which is $0$ initially and $0$ at termination, and is never negative.

A lift raises one height by some amount, so it increases $\Phi$ by that amount;
over the whole run, \cref{cor:flow-lift-count} and \cref{lem:flow-height-bound}
bound the total increase from lifts by $2n^2 \cdot 2n = \bigoh{n^3}$.

A saturating push across $(u,v)$ may cause $v$ to start overflowing, raising
$\Phi$ by $h(v) \le 2n-1$; by \cref{lem:flow-saturating-count} the total
increase from saturating pushes is at most $2nm \cdot 2n = \bigoh{n^2m}$.

A non-saturating push across $(u,v)$ makes $u$ stop overflowing, dropping
$h(u)$ from the sum, and may make $v$ start overflowing, adding $h(v)$. Since
$h(v) = h(u)-1$, the net change is at most $-1$: every non-saturating push
decreases $\Phi$ by at least one.

As $\Phi$ starts and ends at $0$, the number of non-saturating pushes is at most
the total increase, which is $\bigoh{n^3} + \bigoh{n^2m} = \bigoh{n^2m}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:flow-preflow-push-time}
With a set of applicable pushes and a set of overflowing vertices maintained
alongside the preflow, each push costs $\bigoh{1}$ and each lift costs
$\bigoh{n}$, and \cref{alg:flow-preflow-push} runs in $\bigoh{n^2m}$ time.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Lifts: $\bigoh{n^2}$ of them at $\bigoh{n}$ each is $\bigoh{n^3}$. Pushes:
$\bigoh{n^2m}$ of them at $\bigoh{1}$ each. Since $m \ge n-1$ in a network
where every vertex lies on an $s$--$t$ path, $\bigoh{n^3} \subseteq
\bigoh{n^2m}$ and the second term dominates.
\end{proof}

That already beats $\bigoh{nm^2}$ whenever the graph is dense. But the generic
algorithm says nothing about \emph{which} applicable operation to perform, and
that choice is worth a factor.

\subsection{Goldberg's algorithm: highest label first}

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
\caption{Goldberg's algorithm (highest label first)}
\label{alg:flow-goldberg}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
  \State saturate every arc out of $s$; set $h(s) \gets n$ and
         $h(v) \gets 0$ for $v \ne s$
  \While{some vertex is overflowing}
     \State let $u$ be an overflowing vertex of maximum height
     \While{$u$ is overflowing}
        \If{some push out of $u$ is applicable} perform it
        \Else{} lift $u$
        \EndIf
     \EndWhile
  \EndWhile
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{theorem}[\citet{GoldbergTarjan1988}]\label{thm:flow-goldberg}
\Cref{alg:flow-goldberg} performs $\bigoh{n^3}$ non-saturating pushes and runs
in $\bigoh{n^3}$ time.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The counts of lifts and saturating pushes are unchanged:
\cref{cor:flow-lift-count} and \cref{lem:flow-saturating-count} did not depend
on the selection rule. Only the non-saturating pushes improve.

Consider the interval between two consecutive lifts. During it no height
changes. Every non-saturating push takes the currently highest overflowing
vertex and empties it, sending its excess strictly downhill --- so the vertex
that receives the excess is lower, and the vertex just emptied cannot become
overflowing again during this interval, since that would require flow pushed
down from something higher, and nothing higher is overflowing. Each vertex can
therefore be the source of at most one non-saturating push per interval, giving
at most $n$ of them. With $\bigoh{n^2}$ lifts, and hence $\bigoh{n^2}$
intervals, we get $\bigoh{n^3}$ non-saturating pushes.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Variants and the state of the art}

The selection rule is the whole design space, and it has been mined thoroughly.

\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Selection rule} & \textbf{Worst-case running time} \\
\midrule
generic (any applicable operation) & $\bigoh{n^2m}$ \\

lift-to-front                      & $\bigoh{n^3}$ \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

The dynamic-tree bound is \citet{GoldbergTarjan1988}, using the link-cut trees
of Sleator and Tarjan to push along whole paths at once rather than single arcs.

Beyond preflow-push, \citet{Orlin2013} solves maximum flow in $\bigoh{nm}$
time, completing a line of work in which \citet{KingRaoTarjan1994} had achieved
$\bigoh{nm}$ for all but the sparsest graphs. And in 2022 the problem was
essentially closed: \citet{ChenKyngLiuPengProbstGutenbergSachdeva2022} give an
$m^{1+o(1)}$-time algorithm for polynomially bounded integer capacities, via
interior-point methods and dynamic data structures rather than combinatorics.
None of these is what anyone implements. Preflow-push is what anyone
implements, because its worst case is pessimistic and its constants are small.

\section{Flows with lower bounds}
\label{sec:flow-lower-bounds}
%==============================================================================

So far an arc could always carry nothing. Many applications insist that it
carry \emph{something}: a shift must be covered, a pipeline must not be shut
off, a rounded matrix entry must be at least the floor of the original. This is
a genuinely different problem, and the difference shows up immediately: the
zero flow is no longer feasible, so we cannot even start.

\begin{definition}[Flow with lower bounds]
Given additionally a lower bound $\ell : E \to \R_{\ge 0}$ with
$\ell(e) \le c(e)$, a \emph{feasible flow} is a function $f$ satisfying flow
conservation at every $v \notin \{s,t\}$ together with
\[ \ell(e) \;\le\; f(e) \;\le\; c(e) \qquad \text{for every } e \in E. \]
An \emph{admissible} flow is any feasible one; we want one of maximum value.
\end{definition}

The plan is to call an ordinary maximum-flow algorithm twice.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{Phase \arabic*.},leftmargin=*,wide=0pt]
  \item Find \emph{some} admissible flow, or report that none exists.
  \item Augment it to a maximum one.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Phase 1a: from flows to circulations}

\begin{definition}[Circulation]
An \emph{admissible circulation} in a network with bounds $\ell \le c$ is a
function $f$ with $\ell(e) \le f(e) \le c(e)$ for all $e$, satisfying
conservation at \emph{every} vertex --- there is no source and no sink.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:flow-to-circulation}
Let $G$ be a network with lower bounds, source $s$ and sink $t$. Form $G^\circ$
by adding the arc $(t,s)$ with lower bound $0$ and capacity
$\sum_{e \in E} c(e)$. Then admissible flows in $G$ correspond exactly to
admissible circulations in $G^\circ$, and the value of the flow equals the
amount the circulation sends along $(t,s)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Given an admissible flow $f$ of value $F$, set $f^\circ = f$ on $E$ and
$f^\circ(t,s) = F$. Conservation now holds at $s$ and $t$ as well: the surplus
leaving $s$ returns along the new arc, and the surplus arriving at $t$ leaves
along it. The capacity of the new arc is generous enough, since
$F \le \sum_e c(e)$. Conversely, deleting the new arc from a circulation leaves
a flow whose value is what that arc carried.
\end{proof}

The point of the move is that it removes the distinguished vertices, leaving a
single homogeneous condition to satisfy.

\subsection{Phase 1b: from circulations to ordinary maximum flow}

Now the lower bounds go away, at the cost of a new source and sink.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:flow-circulation-to-maxflow}
Let $H$ be a network with bounds $\ell \le c$ and no source or sink. Build
$H'$ as follows: add a new source $s'$ and a new sink $t'$, and replace each
arc $e = (u,v)$ by three arcs, all with lower bound zero:
\[ (u,v) \text{ of capacity } c(e)-\ell(e), \qquad
   (s',v) \text{ of capacity } \ell(e), \qquad
   (u,t') \text{ of capacity } \ell(e). \]
Then $H$ has an admissible circulation if and only if the maximum $s'$--$t'$
flow in $H'$ saturates every arc out of $s'$, and from such a flow $g$ an
admissible circulation is recovered by $f(e) = g(u,v) + \ell(e)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Think of it as draining and refilling: every arc is forced to carry $\ell(e)$,
so remove that obligation from the arc and account for it separately --- the
$\ell(e)$ units are injected directly at the head $v$ by $s'$, and the same
$\ell(e)$ units are drained from the tail $u$ into $t'$.

($\Leftarrow$) Suppose $g$ saturates every arc out of $s'$, and set
$f(e) = g(u,v) + \ell(e)$. The bounds hold because
$0 \le g(u,v) \le c(e)-\ell(e)$. For conservation at a vertex $v$ of $H$,
compute the difference between what $f$ brings in and what it takes out. Each
entering arc contributes its $g$-value plus its $\ell$, and each leaving arc
subtracts the same; the $g$-parts balance by conservation of $g$ at $v$ in
$H'$ once we account for the arcs to $s'$ and $t'$, and those contribute
exactly $\sum_{e \text{ into } v} \ell(e)$ and
$\sum_{e \text{ out of } v} \ell(e)$, which are saturated by assumption.
Adding up, conservation holds.

($\Rightarrow$) Conversely an admissible circulation $f$ in $H$ gives
$g(u,v) = f(e) - \ell(e)$ on the copied arcs and saturates all arcs at $s'$ and
$t'$, and one checks that this $g$ conserves flow at every vertex of $H$.
Since the arcs out of $s'$ form a cut, no flow can do better, so this $g$ is
maximum and the maximum saturates $s'$.
\end{proof}

So the test is again ``is the maximum flow as large as it could possibly be'':
compute a maximum $s'$--$t'$ flow, compare its value with
$\sum_{e} \ell(e)$, and if they agree, add the lower bounds back.

\subsection{Phase 2: from admissible to maximum}

Having an admissible flow $f$, we now want the best one. The augmenting-path
machinery works verbatim once the residual network is defined correctly: an arc
may be increased up to its capacity, and decreased only down to its lower
bound.

\begin{definition}[Residual network with lower bounds]
For an admissible flow $f$ and an arc $e = (u,v)$, the residual network has a
forward arc $(u,v)$ of capacity $c(e) - f(e)$ and a backward arc $(v,u)$ of
capacity $f(e) - \ell(e)$, each present when its capacity is positive.
\end{definition}

Running any maximum-flow algorithm on this residual network and adding the
result to $f$ gives a maximum admissible flow: the argument is exactly that of
\cref{thm:flow-mfmc}, with ``$f(e) = 0$'' replaced by ``$f(e) = \ell(e)$''
in the analysis of arcs from $T$ back to $S$.

\begin{remark}
Lower bounds only make sense in one direction. If both $(u,v)$ and $(v,u)$
carried positive lower bounds, they could be satisfied by sending material
around the two-cycle forever without affecting anything else, which is not what
the modeller meant. So we may assume that between any ordered pair of vertices
there is either a single arc, with or without a lower bound, or two opposite
arcs with no lower bounds at all.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:flow-lower-bounds}
A maximum flow subject to lower bounds can be computed with two calls to an
ordinary maximum-flow algorithm on networks with $\bigoh{n}$ vertices and
$\bigoh{m}$ arcs, plus $\bigoh{n+m}$ additional work.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\Cref{lem:flow-to-circulation} adds one arc, \cref{lem:flow-circulation-to-maxflow}
adds two vertices and at most triples the arcs, and both are constructed in
linear time. The first maximum-flow call decides feasibility and produces an
admissible flow; the second augments it to a maximum one.
\end{proof}

\section{Modelling with flows}
\label{sec:flow-modelling}
%==============================================================================

This is the section that the exam questions are about. The theory above is
fixed and finite; the modelling is open-ended, and it is a skill.

\begin{intuition}
A recipe that covers most cases. First, decide \emph{what one unit of flow
means} --- one path, one worker, one ship, one unit of material. Everything
else follows from that decision, and if the model comes out wrong it is almost
always because this question was answered vaguely. Second, express every
constraint of the problem as a capacity, and every ``must happen'' as
saturation of a cut. Third, check that integrality gives you back a solution of
the original problem: the flow decomposition of an integral flow is a
multiset of paths, and you need those paths to mean something.
\end{intuition}

\subsection{Vertex capacities}
\label{sec:flow-vertex-capacities}

Many problems limit how much may pass \emph{through a vertex}, not along an
arc. This is not a new problem.

\begin{lemma}[Vertex splitting]\label{lem:flow-vertex-splitting}
Let $G = (V,E)$ be a flow network with arc capacities $c : E \to \R_{\ge 0}$
and, in addition, vertex capacities $c_V : V \to \R_{\ge 0}$ limiting the total
flow through each vertex. Then there is a flow network $G'$ with arc capacities
only, having $2|V|$ vertices and $|E| + |V|$ arcs, and a value-preserving
bijection between feasible flows of $G$ and feasible flows of $G'$. It can be
built in $\bigoh{n+m}$ time.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Replace each vertex $v$ by two vertices $v_{\mathrm{in}}$ and
$v_{\mathrm{out}}$ joined by an arc $(v_{\mathrm{in}}, v_{\mathrm{out}})$ of
capacity $c_V(v)$. Redirect every arc $(u,v) \in E$ to
$(u_{\mathrm{out}}, v_{\mathrm{in}})$, keeping its capacity. Take
$s' = s_{\mathrm{out}}$ and $t' = t_{\mathrm{in}}$.

Given a feasible flow $f$ in $G$, define $f'$ on the copied arcs by $f$ and on
each split arc by the total flow through $v$. Conservation at
$v_{\mathrm{in}}$ and $v_{\mathrm{out}}$ holds because conservation held at
$v$, and the split arc's capacity constraint is exactly the vertex capacity.
Conversely, given a feasible $f'$ in $G'$, the flow it induces on $E$ satisfies
conservation at $v$ --- everything entering $v_{\mathrm{in}}$ must leave
$v_{\mathrm{out}}$ --- and the flow through $v$ equals $f'$ on the split arc,
hence is at most $c_V(v)$. Both maps preserve the value, since $s$ and $t$ are
untouched apart from the renaming. The counts and the running time are
immediate.
\end{proof}

The instance grows by a factor of two in the vertices and by an additive $|V|$
in the arcs, so ``a flow network of comparable size'' in the sense of the exam
question. Any polynomial-time flow algorithm therefore solves the
vertex-capacitated problem in polynomial time.

\subsection{Other standard transformations}
\label{sec:flow-transformations}

\paragraph{Several sources and sinks.}
Add a super-source $s^\ast$ with an arc to each source and a super-sink
$t^\ast$ with an arc from each sink. Give the new arcs capacity $\infty$, or a
finite bound if the problem caps how much each individual source may supply.
Flows in the new network of value $F$ correspond exactly to flows in the old
one that ship $F$ units in total.

\paragraph{Undirected graphs.}
Replace each undirected edge $\{u,v\}$ of capacity $c$ by the two arcs $(u,v)$
and $(v,u)$, each of capacity $c$. An integral flow in the result never needs
to use both directions at once: cancelling $\min\{f(u,v), f(v,u)\}$ from both
leaves a flow of the same value.

\paragraph{Lower bounds on arcs.}
Treated in full in \cref{sec:flow-lower-bounds}, since the reduction is long
enough to deserve its own section.

\paragraph{Bipartite matching.}
Orient every edge of a bipartite graph $(A \cup B, E)$ from $A$ to $B$, give it
capacity $1$, add $s \to a$ for $a \in A$ and $b \to t$ for $b \in B$, all of
capacity $1$. By integrality, maximum flows correspond to maximum matchings.
We return to this in \cref{ch:matching}, where the general, non-bipartite case
turns out to need a genuinely new idea.

\section{Applications}
\label{sec:flow-applications}
%==============================================================================

It is worth pausing to say where these problems actually come from, because the
list is longer and stranger than ``moving goods through a network''.

\emph{Maximum flow} itself does turn up literally --- transporting goods,
repositioning empty shipping containers --- but far more often it appears
through the modelling tricks of \cref{sec:flow-modelling}: bipartite matching,
disjoint paths, scheduling with deadlines, project selection, image
segmentation.

\emph{Minimum cut} has a life of its own, and it is usually the more valuable
half of the theorem. It measures how hard a network is to disconnect, which is
the standard notion of network reliability and the standard question in
military targeting, from which the theory originally comes: Ford and Fulkerson
were working at RAND on the capacity of the Soviet rail network, and the
minimum cut is what one attacks. Minimum cuts also find dense components in
graphs, and --- the application below --- they preprocess road networks.

\emph{Flows with lower bounds} look like a technicality until one meets a
problem where every arc has an obligation. Rounding is the canonical one.

\subsection{Consistent matrix rounding}
\label{sec:flow-matrix-rounding}

\begin{probdef}{Consistent Matrix Rounding}
  \instance A $p \times q$ matrix of non-negative reals $(b_{ij})$, with row
  sums $r_i = \sum_j b_{ij}$ and column sums $s_j = \sum_i b_{ij}$.
  \goal Round every $b_{ij}$, every $r_i$ and every $s_j$ up or down to an
  integer, in such a way that the rounded matrix still has the rounded row sums
  as its row sums and the rounded column sums as its column sums.
\end{probdef}

This is a real problem: statistical agencies publish tables that must be
rounded for disclosure control, and a table whose rows no longer add up is an
embarrassment. It is not obvious that a consistent rounding always exists ---
rounding each entry to the nearest integer certainly does not work.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:flow-matrix-rounding}
A consistent rounding always exists, and can be found by a single feasibility
computation for a circulation with lower bounds.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Build a network with a vertex for each row, a vertex for each column, a source
$s$ and a sink $t$, and arcs
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item $(s, \text{row}_i)$ with bounds
        $[\lfloor r_i \rfloor, \lceil r_i \rceil]$,
  \item $(\text{row}_i, \text{col}_j)$ with bounds
        $[\lfloor b_{ij} \rfloor, \lceil b_{ij} \rceil]$,
  \item $(\text{col}_j, t)$ with bounds
        $[\lfloor s_j \rfloor, \lceil s_j \rceil]$,
\end{itemize}
and close it into a circulation with the arc $(t,s)$ as in
\cref{lem:flow-to-circulation}.

An \emph{integral} circulation in this network is exactly a consistent
rounding: the value on $(\text{row}_i,\text{col}_j)$ is the rounded entry, it
lies between the floor and the ceiling of $b_{ij}$ by the bounds, and
conservation at $\text{row}_i$ says that the rounded entries of row $i$ sum to
the value on $(s,\text{row}_i)$, which is a rounding of $r_i$. Similarly for
columns.

Such a circulation exists because a fractional one does --- take
$f = b_{ij}$ on the middle arcs, $r_i$ and $s_j$ on the outer ones, which
satisfies every bound and conserves flow by the definition of the row and column
sums --- and because feasibility with integral bounds implies \emph{integral}
feasibility, by \cref{cor:flow-integrality} applied to the network of
\cref{lem:flow-circulation-to-maxflow}, whose capacities are all integers.
\end{proof}

\begin{intuition}
Note what is being used here, because it is the reason lower bounds earn their
place. We do not want a \emph{maximum} anything. We want \emph{any} feasible
integral solution, and the entire content of the theorem is that one exists ---
which is the integrality theorem plus the observation that the unrounded matrix
is itself a feasible fractional point. Flows are being used as an existence
proof, in the same spirit as \cref{ch:stable-marriage}.
\end{intuition}

\subsection{Natural cuts in road networks}
\label{sec:flow-natural-cuts}

Route planning on a continental road network --- hundreds of millions of
vertices --- cannot afford a Dijkstra run per query, and the standard response
is to preprocess the graph into a hierarchy. The difficulty is finding a good
partition into cells: one wants few edges between cells, and general-purpose
graph partitioners are slow and indifferent to geography.

\citet{DellingGoldbergRazenshteynWerneck2011} observe that road networks come
with their partitions already drawn. Traffic between two regions separated by a
river crosses at a handful of bridges; between two countries, at a handful of
border crossings; across a mountain range, at a handful of passes. These are
small cuts, and they are \emph{natural} --- they reflect the geography rather
than the algorithm. So one finds them the obvious way: run minimum-cut
computations from many seeds, contract the dense regions they isolate, and
partition the much smaller contracted graph. The result is both faster to
compute and better than what a generic partitioner produces.

The lesson is worth stating in general. Minimum cut is not only an answer to
``how do I break this network''; it is a way of \emph{finding structure that is
already there}.

%==============================================================================
\section{Menger's theorem and disjoint paths (non-examinable)}
\label{sec:flow-menger}
%==============================================================================

\nonexam{The lecture covers this material as two recipes --- how to find a
minimum set of arcs whose removal disconnects $t$ from $s$, and how to find a
minimum such set of vertices. Both appear below as special cases. What is added
here is the name and the theorem statement, which is worth having because the
vertex form is exactly the certificate that a grid escape instance is
unsolvable.}

Max-flow min-cut has a purely graph-theoretic shadow, proved decades earlier by
\citet{Menger1927}: connectivity is limited by separators, and by nothing else.

\begin{theorem}[Menger, edge version]\label{thm:flow-menger-edge}
Let $s \ne t$ be vertices of a directed graph $G$. The maximum number of
pairwise arc-disjoint $s$--$t$ paths equals the minimum number of arcs whose
deletion leaves no $s$--$t$ path.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Give every arc capacity $1$. An integral flow of value $k$ decomposes into $k$
arc-disjoint $s$--$t$ paths: repeatedly follow arcs carrying flow from $s$ to
$t$, remove that path's flow, and note that any cycles left over carry flow
that may simply be deleted. Conversely $k$ arc-disjoint paths give a flow of
value $k$. So the maximum number of arc-disjoint paths is the maximum flow
value. A cut of capacity $k$ is a set of $k$ arcs whose removal disconnects
$t$ from $s$, and conversely, so the minimum cut capacity is the minimum number
of arcs to delete. Now apply \cref{thm:flow-mfmc}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Menger, vertex version]\label{thm:flow-menger-vertex}
Let $s \ne t$ be non-adjacent vertices of a graph $G$. The maximum number of
internally vertex-disjoint $s$--$t$ paths equals the minimum size of a set
$X \subseteq V \setminus \{s,t\}$ whose deletion leaves no $s$--$t$ path.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Apply \cref{lem:flow-vertex-splitting} with $c_V(v) = 1$ for
$v \notin \{s,t\}$ and $c_V(s) = c_V(t) = \infty$, and all arc capacities
$\infty$. Then every minimum cut consists of split arcs only --- the others
have infinite capacity --- and a set of $k$ split arcs is precisely a set of
$k$ vertices whose removal separates $s$ from $t$. Integral flows of value $k$
decompose into $k$ paths, internally vertex-disjoint because each split arc
carries at most one unit. Now apply \cref{thm:flow-mfmc} again.
\end{proof}

The vertex version is the one we need next, and the pattern of its proof ---
unit vertex capacities, infinite arc capacities, so that cuts are forced to be
sets of vertices --- is worth memorising. It is the whole of the next section.

\section{Case study: escaping a grid}
\label{sec:flow-grid-escape}
%==============================================================================

\begin{probdef}{Grid Escape}
  \instance The $n \times n$ grid graph, whose vertices are $v_{i,j}$ for
  $1 \le i,j \le n$ with $v_{i,j}$ adjacent to $v_{i',j'}$ when
  $|i-i'| + |j-j'| = 1$, together with $m \le n^2$ \emph{starting points}
  $v_{x_1,y_1}, \ldots, v_{x_m,y_m}$.
  \question Are there $m$ vertex-disjoint paths leading from the $m$ starting
  points to $m$ \emph{different} boundary vertices --- those $v_{i,j}$ with
  $i \in \{1,n\}$ or $j \in \{1,n\}$?
\end{probdef}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\figplaceholder{55mm}{Two grids side by side, as in the exam question. Starting
points as large filled disks, other grid vertices as small circles. Left: an
instance together with a solution --- draw the escape paths as thick coloured
routes so that their disjointness is visible. Right: an instance with no
solution, ideally one where the obstruction is a small separating set of
vertices, so that the picture also illustrates why the answer is no.}
\caption{Grid escape. The left instance is solvable; the right one is not.}
\label{fig:flow-grid-escape}
\end{figure}

Read the problem statement carefully before modelling: \emph{$n$ is the side
length of the grid and $m$ is the number of starting points}, not the numbers
of vertices and arcs. The grid has $n^2$ vertices. A running time that is
polynomial in $n$ is therefore a much stronger requirement than one polynomial
in the number of vertices would be --- or rather it would be, if the grid were
not given explicitly; since it is, the two coincide, but the analysis has to be
carried out in the right variables.

\paragraph{The model.}
Two things must be enforced: the paths are vertex-disjoint, and they end at
distinct boundary vertices. Both are capacity constraints.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Orient every grid edge in both directions, giving each arc capacity
        $\infty$ (capacity $1$ also works and is what one implements).
  \item Give every grid vertex capacity $1$, and apply
        \cref{lem:flow-vertex-splitting}.
  \item Add a super-source $s$ with an arc of capacity $1$ to
        $(v_{x_k,y_k})_{\mathrm{in}}$ for each starting point $v_{x_k,y_k}$.
  \item Add a super-sink $t$ with an arc of capacity $1$ from
        $b_{\mathrm{out}}$ for every boundary vertex $b$.
  \item Compute a maximum $s$--$t$ flow, and answer \textsc{yes} if and only if
        its value is $m$.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:flow-grid-escape}
The construction is correct: the instance is solvable if and only if the
maximum flow value is $m$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
($\Rightarrow$) Given $m$ vertex-disjoint escape paths, send one unit along
each, prefixed by the arc from $s$ and suffixed by the arc into $t$. Vertex
capacities are respected because the paths are vertex-disjoint; the arcs into
$t$ are respected because the endpoints are distinct; the arcs out of $s$ are
respected because the starting points are distinct. The value is $m$.

($\Leftarrow$) Suppose the maximum flow value is $m$. All capacities are
integers, so by \cref{cor:flow-integrality} we may take the flow $f$ to be
integral, and then every capacity-$1$ arc carries $0$ or $1$ unit. Decompose
$f$ into $m$ paths from $s$ to $t$ as in the proof of
\cref{thm:flow-menger-edge}. Each such path leaves $s$ by a distinct arc, so it
starts at a distinct starting point; each enters $t$ by a distinct arc, so it
ends at a distinct boundary vertex; and each grid vertex is used by at most one
of them, since its split arc has capacity $1$. Deleting $s$, $t$ and the
splitting gives $m$ vertex-disjoint escape paths.

Note that the value can never exceed $m$, since the $m$ arcs out of $s$ form a
cut of capacity $m$. So the test is really ``is the maximum flow as large as it
could possibly be''.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Running time.}
The grid has $N = n^2$ vertices and at most $2n^2$ edges, hence at most $4n^2$
arcs after orienting. Splitting doubles the vertices and adds $N$ arcs, and the
source and sink contribute $m \le n^2$ and $\bigoh{n}$ arcs respectively. So
the network has
\[ |V'| = 2n^2 + 2 = \bigoh{n^2}, \qquad |E'| = \bigoh{n^2} \]
and is built in $\bigoh{n^2}$ time. The maximum flow value is at most $m$, so
Ford--Fulkerson with breadth-first search performs at most $m$ augmentations at
$\bigoh{|E'|} = \bigoh{n^2}$ each:
\[ \bigoh{m \, n^2} \;\subseteq\; \bigoh{n^4}. \]
This is polynomial in $n$, as required. Since all capacities are $1$ and every
split vertex has a single entering arc, Dinic's algorithm does better:
$\bigoh{|E'|\sqrt{|V'|}} = \bigoh{n^2 \cdot n} = \bigoh{n^3}$.

\begin{intuition}
The instance on the right of \cref{fig:flow-grid-escape} has no solution, and
by \cref{thm:flow-menger-vertex} there is a short reason why: a set of fewer
than $m$ vertices separating the starting points from the boundary. Finding
that set is what the algorithm does when it fails --- the minimum cut is read
off the last breadth-first search --- so the algorithm always outputs either an
escape or a proof that none exists. That is worth more than a yes/no answer,
and it costs nothing extra.
\end{intuition}

and the next, and in particular for modelling: its exercise sections are a

\citet[Chapter 7]{KleinbergTardos2005} and \citet[Chapter 24]{CLRS2022} both
cover maximum flow well; the lift-to-front implementation of preflow-push is in
Chapter 26 of the third edition of the latter.

\section{Exercises}
\label{sec:ex-max-flow}
%==============================================================================

\begin{exercise}
Show that if all capacities are integers then \emph{every} maximum flow need
not be integral, but that a maximum flow that is not integral can always be
converted into an integral one of the same value.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Give a flow network with a unique maximum flow but several minimum cuts, and
one with a unique minimum cut but several maximum flows.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Prove that the set of minimum $s$--$t$ cuts is closed under union and
intersection: if $(S_1,T_1)$ and $(S_2,T_2)$ are minimum cuts then so are
$(S_1 \cap S_2, \cdot)$ and $(S_1 \cup S_2, \cdot)$. Deduce that there is a
unique minimum cut whose source side is smallest.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Let $f$ be a maximum flow, and suppose the capacity of a single arc is
increased by $1$. Show how to obtain a maximum flow in the new network in
$\bigoh{m}$ time. What if a capacity is \emph{decreased} by $1$?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
In the grid escape problem, suppose the paths are only required to be
\emph{arc}-disjoint rather than vertex-disjoint. Does the construction of
\cref{sec:flow-grid-escape} still work? What changes, and what happens to the
running time?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Modify the grid escape construction so that it answers the harder question:
what is the largest number of starting points that can escape simultaneously?
Argue that your modification is correct.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
A network has capacities on arcs \emph{and} a bound $k$ on the number of arcs
that may carry non-zero flow. Show that finding a maximum flow subject to this
extra bound is NP-hard.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Show that the bound of \cref{lem:flow-height-bound} is essentially tight: give a
family of networks on which some vertex reaches height $\Omega(n)$ during a run
of preflow-push.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
In the generic preflow-push algorithm, show that the source is never lifted and
that its height stays $n$ throughout, and explain where the argument would break
if we initialised $h(s)$ to anything smaller.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Run preflow-push by hand on a network of your choice with five vertices, once
with the highest-label rule and once choosing the \emph{lowest} overflowing
vertex. Count the operations. Is the lowest-label rule obviously bad, or does it
merely lack a good analysis?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Give a network with lower bounds that admits no feasible flow at all, and
identify what goes wrong in the transformation of
\cref{lem:flow-circulation-to-maxflow} --- that is, which arcs out of $s'$
cannot be saturated, and what that tells you about the original instance.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
In the matrix rounding problem, suppose we additionally insist that the total
sum be rounded consistently as well. Does a solution still always exist? Prove
it or give a counterexample.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Show that a maximum flow can be computed by capacity scaling: work through the
capacities bit by bit, from the most significant downwards, maintaining a
maximum flow of the network truncated to the bits seen so far. Analyse the
running time.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Prove the following strengthening of \cref{cor:flow-integrality}: if all
capacities are integers, then for any maximum flow $f$ there is an integral
maximum flow $f'$ with $\lfloor f(e) \rfloor \le f'(e) \le \lceil f(e) \rceil$
for every arc $e$.
\end{exercise}
