\chapter{\texorpdfstring{Geometric $\ER$-Completeness}{Geometric exists-R-Completeness}}
\label{ch:geometric-er}

\Cref{ch:etr} put $\ER$ on the map and showed how to prove membership. This
chapter is about the other half: proving that a geometric problem is
$\ER$-\emph{hard}, and therefore that its difficulty really is the difficulty of
real solutions. Two tools do all the work --- a pair of stripped-down normal
forms of \ETR{}, and one geometric problem, \lang{Stretchability}, that already
sits between the algebra and the geometry --- and between them they reach two
very different theorems: that recognising unit disk graphs is $\ER$-complete,
and that the art gallery problem is.

The last two sections are background rather than machinery. Point--line duality
and order types are where this subject came from historically, and they are
where \lang{Stretchability} comes from; neither is examined, and nothing in the
main line of the chapter depends on them.

\begin{goals}
  \poolgoal{define \lang{ETR-AM} and show that it is $\ER$-complete}
  \goalitem{define \lang{ETR-INV}, and show how inversion constraints replace
        multiplication and how the variable range is made compact}
  \goalitem{define pseudoline arrangements and \lang{Stretchability}, and state
        what is known about its complexity}
  \poolgoal{define geometric intersection graphs, and unit disk graphs in
        particular}
  \poolgoal{show that deciding whether a given graph is a unit disk
        intersection graph is $\ER$-complete}
  \poolgoal{show that \lang{Optimal Curve Straightening} is
        $\ER$-complete}
  \goalitem{explain how a variable and an inversion constraint are encoded in
        the art gallery problem, and why the encoding must be compact}
\end{goals}

%==============================================================================
\section{Two normal forms}
\label{sec:etr-normal-forms}
%==============================================================================

Reducing \emph{from} full \ETR{} is unpleasant. Two stripped-down versions do
all the work: one that keeps addition and multiplication, and one that trades
multiplication for inversion and confines the variables to a compact interval.

\subsection{ETR-AM}
\label{sec:etr-am}

\ETR{} in full generality is unwieldy for hardness proofs, where one wants to
reduce \emph{from} it. The following stripped-down version is far easier to work
with, and is no easier to solve.

\begin{probdef}{ETR-AM}
  \instance A conjunction of constraints, each of one of the three forms
  \[ x = 1, \qquad x + y = z, \qquad x \cdot y = z. \]
  \question Is the conjunction satisfiable over $\R$?
\end{probdef}

The letters stand for \emph{addition} and \emph{multiplication}. Note that the
constraint $x = 1$ is needed: without it, setting every variable to zero
satisfies everything.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:etr-am}
\lang{ETR-AM} is $\ER$-complete.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Membership is immediate: \lang{ETR-AM} is a syntactic restriction of \ETR{}.

For hardness, let $\Phi$ be an arbitrary \ETR{} instance; we transform it in
polynomial time by a sequence of normal-form steps.

\emph{Remove negations and disjunctions.} Push negations down to the atomic
constraints, so that $\neg(p > 0)$ becomes $p \le 0$, and distribute
disjunctions until they occur only between polynomial (in)equalities. Strict and
non-strict inequalities are then removed with a fresh variable:
\[ p > 0 \iff \exists x : p \cdot x^2 = 1, \qquad
   p \ge 0 \iff \exists x : p = x^2, \]
the first because $p$ has an inverse exactly when it is non-zero and $x^2 > 0$
forces the sign, the second because the squares are exactly the non-negative
reals. A disjunction of equalities becomes a product:
\[ (p = 0) \vee (q = 0) \iff p \cdot q = 0. \]
What remains is a conjunction of polynomial equations.

\emph{Remove coefficients.} Build the constants from $1$ with auxiliary
variables: $b = 1+1$ gives $2$, then $c = b+b$ gives $4$, $d = c+c$ gives $8$,
and $e = d+b$ gives $10$. Repeated doubling produces any integer coefficient
with logarithmically many constraints.

\emph{Arithmetise.} For every sum and every product occurring in a polynomial,
introduce a fresh variable for its value and the corresponding constraint. A
final equation $c = 0$ becomes $c + 1 = 1$, which is expressible in the allowed
forms. For instance $x^2y + z = 0$ becomes
\[ \exists a,b,c \;:\; a = x \cdot x \;\wedge\; b = a \cdot y \;\wedge\;
   c = b + z \;\wedge\; c + 1 = 1. \]

Every step is polynomial and preserves satisfiability, so the result is an
equivalent \lang{ETR-AM} instance.
\end{proof}

\Cref{lem:etr-am} is the starting point for essentially every geometric
$\ER$-hardness proof in this chapter: one reduces from \lang{ETR-AM} and builds
a gadget for each of the three constraint types. The next subsection squeezes
it further.

\subsection{ETR-INV}
\label{sec:etr-inv}

\lang{ETR-AM} is enough for the classical geometric reductions. It is not
enough for the art gallery problem, and the reason is a real one: in a geometric
gadget a variable is encoded as the position of an object, and objects live in
\emph{compact} regions. A reduction from \lang{ETR-AM}, whose variables range
over all of $\R$, cannot respect that. The fix is a second normal form.

\begin{probdef}{ETR-INV}
  \instance A set of real variables ranging over the compact interval
  $[\tfrac12, 2]$, and a set of constraints, each of the form
  \[ x + y = z \qquad\text{or}\qquad x \cdot y = 1. \]
  \question Is the system satisfiable?
\end{probdef}

Two things have changed: multiplication has become \emph{inversion}, and the
domain has become compact. Both are genuine restrictions, and each needs an
argument.

\paragraph{Inversion replaces multiplication.}
Write \lang{UN-INV} for the same problem with the variables ranging over all of
$\R$ and the constraint $x = 1$ added.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:un-inv}
\lang{UN-INV} is $\ER$-complete.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Membership is immediate, since \lang{UN-INV} is a special case of \ETR{}. For
hardness we reduce from \lang{ETR-AM} in two steps: first replace multiplication
by squaring, then squaring by inversion.

For the first, note that $(x+y)^2 - x^2 - y^2 = 2xy$. So a constraint
$x \cdot y = z$ is replaced by
\[ A = x+y, \quad B = A^2, \quad C = x^2, \quad D = y^2, \quad
   E = C+D, \quad B = F+E, \quad F = z+z, \]
which uses only addition and squaring, and forces $z = x \cdot y$.

For the second, observe that
\[ \frac{1}{x} - \frac{1}{x+1} = \frac{1}{x(x+1)}, \qquad\text{hence}\qquad
   \frac{1}{\;\frac1x - \frac1{x+1}\;} - x \;=\; x^2 . \]
So a constraint $x^2 = y$ is replaced by
\[ A \cdot x = 1, \quad B = x+1, \quad C \cdot B = 1, \quad D + C = A, \quad
   E \cdot D = 1, \quad y = E + x, \]
which uses only addition and inversion. Both replacements are local and there
are linearly many of them.
\end{proof}

Note also that $x \cdot x = 1$ forces $x = \pm 1$, and since \lang{ETR-INV}
confines its variables to $[\tfrac12,2]$ it forces $x = 1$. That is why
\lang{ETR-INV} needs no separate constraint $x = 1$.

\paragraph{Making the domain compact.}
Bounding the variables to an \emph{open} interval is elementary.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:bounded-etr}
\lang{Bounded-ETR}, in which every variable is confined to the open interval
$(-1,1)$, is $\ER$-complete.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Membership is again immediate. For hardness, reduce from \lang{ETR-AM} using
the continuous bijection $f : (-1,1) \to \R$,
\[ f(x) \;=\; \frac{x}{(x-1)(x+1)}. \]
Replace every occurrence of every variable $x$ by $f(x)$ and clear
denominators. For example $x + y = z$ becomes
\[ x(y-1)(y+1)(z-1)(z+1) + y(x-1)(x+1)(z-1)(z+1)
   = z(x-1)(x+1)(y-1)(y+1). \]
A solution of the new system maps to one of the old under $f$, and conversely
under $f^{-1}$.
\end{proof}

A \emph{closed} interval is a different matter, and the elementary argument
cannot survive: there is no continuous bijection between a closed interval and
$\R$, because the two are not homeomorphic. What is needed instead is a bound on
how far from the origin a solution can be forced to lie.

\begin{theorem}[Ball theorem]\label{thm:ball-theorem}
Every semi-algebraic set in $\R^n$ of complexity at most $L \ge 4$ contains a
point at distance at most $2^{L^{8n}}$ from the origin.
\end{theorem}

Here the \emph{complexity} of a semialgebraic set $S$ is the length of the
shortest formula $\varphi$ with $S = \{x \in \R^n : \varphi(x)\}$. The version
used here is the one distilled by \citet{SchaeferStefankovic2017}; the original
results are from algebraic geometry and go back to 1984.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:compact-etr}
\lang{Compact-ETR}, in which every variable is confined to $[-1,1]$, is
$\ER$-complete.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $\exists x \in \R^n : \varphi(x)$ be an \lang{ETR-AM} instance of
description length $L \ge 4$, and let $S = \{x : \varphi(x)\}$. By
\cref{thm:ball-theorem}, if $S$ is non-empty it contains a point within
distance $M = 2^{L^{8n}}$ of the origin, hence a point of the cube
$[-M,M]^n$. So the instance is equivalent to $\exists x \in [-M,M]^n :
\varphi(x)$.

It remains to shrink the cube to $[-1,1]^n$, which the bijection
$f(x) = x/\varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon = 1/M$ does. We cannot write $M$ down
--- it is doubly exponential --- but we can \emph{construct} $\varepsilon$ by
repeated squaring: set $x_1 = \tfrac12$ and $x_{i+1} = x_i^2$, so that
$x_k = 2^{-2^k}$, and choose $k = \bigoh{n \log L}$ so that
$\varepsilon^{-1} \ge M$. That is a formula of size $\bigoh{k}$. Now replace
every variable $x$ by $x/\varepsilon$ throughout $\varphi$ and clear
denominators: $x \cdot y = z$ becomes $xy = z\varepsilon$, and so on.
\end{proof}

\begin{intuition}
The ball theorem is doing something delicate and it is worth naming. Restricting
an \ETR{} instance to a bounded box is only sound if we know that a solution, if
one exists, can be found inside the box --- and the whole point of $\ER$ is that
solutions can be enormous. The ball theorem is the quantitative promise that
they are not \emph{arbitrarily} enormous: doubly exponential is the worst that
can happen. Everything about compact encodings rests on it.
\end{intuition}

\section{Pseudoline arrangements and stretchability}
\label{sec:stretchability}
%==============================================================================

The normal forms are the algebraic end of the chapter. The geometric end is a
single problem, and both hardness proofs that follow start from it.

\begin{definition}[Pseudoline arrangement, stretchability]\label{def:stretchability}
A \emph{pseudoline} is a simple curve in the plane, unbounded in both
directions, that behaves topologically like a straight line. A
\emph{pseudoline arrangement} is a collection of pseudolines any two of which
meet in exactly one point, and cross there. Two arrangements are
\emph{combinatorially equivalent} if every pseudoline meets the others in the
same order in both. \lang{Stretchability} asks whether a given pseudoline
arrangement is combinatorially equivalent to an arrangement of straight lines.
\end{definition}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{er-pseudolines}
\caption{A pseudoline arrangement, and a combinatorially equivalent
straight-line arrangement. \lang{Stretchability} asks whether the crossing
order can be preserved by straight lines.}
\label{fig:er-pseudolines}
\end{figure}

\begin{theorem}[\citet{Mnev1985}, \citet{Shor1991}]\label{thm:stretchability}
\lang{Stretchability} is $\ER$-complete.
\end{theorem}

We use \cref{thm:stretchability} as a black box and do not prove it. What
should be believed about it is the shape of the statement rather than its
proof: an arrangement of pseudolines is a purely combinatorial object --- who
crosses whom, in what order --- and asking whether it can be drawn with
straight lines is asking whether a system of real inequalities has a solution,
in exactly the sense \ETR{} formalises. The hard direction reduces from
\lang{ETR-AM}, so the algebra of \cref{sec:etr-normal-forms} is what makes the
theorem true; \cref{sec:order-types} tells that story, and is not examined.

Two features of \lang{Stretchability} make it the right thing to reduce from.
It is combinatorial, so a reduction from it starts with a finite object that
can be manipulated by hand, not with a formula. And its solutions are
\emph{configurations}, so a construction that turns a configuration into a
geometric object --- disks, a curve, a polygon --- has something to be faithful
to. The next three sections do exactly that, three times.

\section{Geometric intersection graphs}
\label{sec:intersection-graphs}
%==============================================================================

The first family of geometric problems we prove hard consists of recognition
problems: given an abstract graph, can it be realised as the intersection
pattern of geometric objects of a prescribed shape?

\begin{definition}[Intersection graph]
Let $W$ be a finite set of objects in the plane. The \emph{intersection graph}
$G(W)$ has one vertex per object, with two vertices adjacent exactly when the
corresponding objects intersect. A \emph{unit disk graph} is the intersection
graph of a set of disks all of radius $1$.
\end{definition}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{er-disks}
\caption{Left: a set $W$ of disks. Right: its intersection graph $G(W)$.}
\label{fig:er-disks}
\end{figure}

Recognition is the natural algorithmic question: given a graph, is it the
intersection graph of objects of a given type? For most families of geometric
objects the answer is now known to be $\ER$-complete, and all the proofs go
through encoding a pseudoline arrangement. We give one, chosen because it is the
most elegant.

%==============================================================================
\section{Recognising unit disk graphs}
\label{sec:udg-hardness}
%==============================================================================

Here is the first hardness proof, chosen because it is the most elegant one in
the subject.

\begin{theorem}[\citet{McDiarmidMuller2013}]\label{thm:udg}
It is $\ER$-complete to decide whether a graph is a unit disk intersection
graph.
\end{theorem}

Membership is easy, and both routes of \cref{sec:er-membership} work. By the
machine model: guess the disk centres and verify every edge and non-edge by
computing distances. By the formula: introduce $x_v, y_v$ for each vertex and
add
\[ (x_v-x_u)^2 + (y_v-y_u)^2 \le 4 \quad \text{for } uv \in E, \qquad
   (x_v-x_u)^2 + (y_v-y_u)^2 > 4 \quad \text{for } uv \notin E, \]
taking the disks to have radius $1$, so that two of them meet exactly when their
centres are at distance at most $2$.

Hardness is the interesting half.

\begin{proof}[Proof of hardness, from \lang{Stretchability}]
Let $L$ be an arrangement of $n$ pseudolines. It helps to think of $L$ as an
arrangement of straight lines first: that mental picture makes the construction
obvious, and afterwards we check that it never used straightness.

\emph{The construction.} Place one point in each cell of the arrangement, and
call the resulting set $P$. For each line $\ell$ there are two disks $D^{+}$
and $D^{-}$ of equal radius such that $D^{+}$ contains every point of $P$ above
$\ell$ and $D^{-}$ every point below --- a disk of large radius is locally
almost a half-plane, which is why one radius $R$ can be chosen to work for every
line at once. This gives $2n$ disks of radius $R$. Now halve every radius,
keeping the centres, and add one further disk of radius $R/2$ centred at each
point of $P$. Let $G$ be the intersection graph of all these radius-$R/2$
disks, and write $v_i^{+}, v_i^{-}$ for the vertices of the two disks belonging
to line $\ell_i$.

\emph{Five observations.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=*]
  \item \emph{The upper disks form a clique, and so do the lower ones.} The
        arrangement may be squeezed without changing its combinatorics, and
        after squeezing all the upper disks meet.
  \item \emph{$G$ depends only on $L$, not on any drawing of it.} For each cell
        the arrangement tells us whether it lies above or below each pseudoline,
        and that is all the construction consults: a cell above $\ell_i$ gives a
        vertex adjacent to $v_i^{+}$ and not to $v_i^{-}$.
  \item \emph{$G$ has $\bigoh{n^2}$ vertices,} two per line and one per cell,
        and an arrangement of $n$ pseudolines has $\bigoh{n^2}$ cells. So the
        reduction is polynomial.
  \item \emph{If $L$ is stretchable, $G$ is a unit disk graph}, since the
        construction applied to the stretched arrangement produces exactly such
        a representation.
  \item \emph{Any unit disk representation of $G$ yields an arrangement
        equivalent to $L$.} This is the converse and the heart of the matter.
        Given a representation, let $k_i$ be the perpendicular bisector of the
        centres of the disks realising $v_i^{+}$ and $v_i^{-}$. We claim
        $k_1, \ldots, k_n$ is a line arrangement combinatorially equivalent to
        $L$. Let $c$ be a cell of $L$, let $m$ be the centre of the disk placed
        in it and $u$ the corresponding vertex. If $c$ is above $\ell_i$ then
        $u$ is adjacent to $v_i^{+}$ and not to $v_i^{-}$, so $m$ is closer to
        the centre of $D_i^{+}$ than to that of $D_i^{-}$, so $m$ lies above
        $k_i$. The case below is identical. So the cells of the two
        arrangements correspond, which is what equivalence means.
\end{enumerate}
Together, (ii) makes the reduction well defined, (iii) makes it polynomial, and
(iv) with (v) make it correct.
\end{proof}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[page=1,width=0.8\textwidth]{er-disk-reduction}
\caption{The two large disks belonging to one line: enlarging a disk makes it
locally more and more like a half-plane, so one radius serves every line.}
\label{fig:er-disk-reduction}
\end{figure}

\section{Optimal curve straightening}
\label{sec:curve-straightening}
%==============================================================================

A closed curve $\gamma$ in the plane, possibly self-intersecting, is described
combinatorially by the $4$-regular plane graph its self-intersections induce.
For visualisation, compression and geometric algorithms one wants a simpler
curve representing $\gamma$ with as few segments as possible, without changing
that graph.

\begin{probdef}{Optimal Curve Straightening}
  \instance A closed curve $\gamma$ in the plane and an integer $k$.
  \question Can $\gamma$ be represented by at most $k$ straight segments,
  keeping the same self-intersection pattern?
\end{probdef}

\begin{theorem}[\citet{Erickson2019}]\label{thm:curve-straightening}
\lang{Optimal Curve Straightening} is $\ER$-complete.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Membership]
Describing $k$ segments with an \ETR{} formula is easy; expressing that the
induced plane graph is unchanged is not, and the first proof that did so runs to
five pages. That difficulty was one of the motivations for the machine model.
With \cref{thm:er-machine-model} the argument is one sentence: take the
coordinates of the $k$ segments as the witness, and let the verifier compute the
intersection pattern and compare the two plane graphs.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Hardness, from \lang{Stretchability}]
Let $L$ be an arrangement of $n$ pseudolines; we build a curve $\gamma$
representable with $4(n+1)$ segments exactly when $L$ is stretchable.

Assume $n$ is odd, adding if necessary one further pseudoline crossing all
others far to the left, which changes nothing about stretchability. Take a large
rectangle containing every crossing, add one extra line above everything that
crosses nothing, and stitch the ends together with small curls. Set
$k = 4(n+1)$.

If $L$ is stretchable, straighten it, restrict the lines to the rectangle and
attach the curls: the result is a drawing of $\gamma$ with $4(n+1)$ segments.

Conversely, let $D$ be a drawing of $\gamma$ with $4(n+1)$ segments. Each curl
needs at least two vertices, and there are $2(n+1)$ curls, so all $4(n+1)$
vertices are consumed by curls and none is left over. Hence no vertex lies on
the part of the drawing corresponding to a pseudoline, so each pseudoline is
drawn as a single straight segment. Extending those segments to full lines gives
a line arrangement equivalent to $L$.
\end{proof}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[page=1,width=0.8\textwidth]{er-curve-straightening}
\caption{A closed curve in the plane and a representation of it with few
segments.}
\label{fig:er-curve-straightening}
\end{figure}

\section{The art gallery problem}
\label{sec:art-gallery}
%==============================================================================

Imagine a gallery with a complicated floor plan --- walls, corners, narrow
corridors. A guard sees in all directions but walls block the view. How many
guards are needed, and where do they go?

For $x, y$ in a simple polygon $P$, say $x$ \emph{sees} $y$ if the segment $xy$
lies in $P$. A set $G \subseteq P$ \emph{guards} $P$ if every point of $P$ is
seen by some $g \in G$. Despite the simple statement the problem runs deep, and
it produced results such as Chv\'atal's theorem, that $\lfloor n/3 \rfloor$
guards always suffice for a polygon with $n$ vertices.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{er-art-gallery}
\caption{A polygon with two guards, between them covering part of it.}
\label{fig:er-art-gallery}
\end{figure}

\citet{AbrahamsenAdamaszekMiltzow2017} showed that NP-membership is not merely
hard to prove but false-looking for a good reason: there is a polygon whose
optimal guard positions are \emph{irrational}. Until then the working intuition
had been that one could always wiggle a solution into rational position and use
that as a certificate.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{er-irrational-guards}
\caption{A polygon whose optimal guarding needs irrational coordinates. The
example is small and the verification is a short calculation.}
\label{fig:er-irrational-guards}
\end{figure}

\begin{theorem}[\citet{AbrahamsenAdamaszekMiltzow2022}]\label{thm:art-gallery}
The \lang{Art Gallery Problem} is $\ER$-complete.
\end{theorem}

Membership was \cref{prop:er-art-gallery}. The hardness proof is long and
technical, and we do not give it; \citet{Stade2025} is a more accessible route.
What we do give is the two ideas that made it possible, both of which have been
reused many times since.

\subsection{Encoding a variable}

A variable is represented by a guard, confined to a single segment. The
confinement is achieved by placing a few points that the guard must see: to see
all of them at once, it must lie in the intersection of their visibility
regions, and that intersection is arranged to be the segment.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{er-variable-guard-segment}
\caption{Any guard that must see all four marked points simultaneously is
forced onto the highlighted segment.}
\label{fig:er-variable-guard}
\end{figure}

This is where compactness becomes unavoidable, and it is worth being precise
about why. The guard is forced into
$\mathrm{Vis}(p_1) \cap \cdots \cap \mathrm{Vis}(p_k)$, and a visibility region
is by definition closed; the polygon is bounded; so the region available to the
guard is \emph{compact}. A reduction from \lang{ETR-AM}, whose variables range
over all of $\R$, therefore cannot be made to fit --- and even
\lang{Bounded-ETR}, with its open interval, does not fit either. Only
\lang{ETR-INV}, with its closed range $[\tfrac12,2]$, matches what the geometry
provides. That is what the whole apparatus of
\cref{lem:compact-etr,thm:ball-theorem} was for.

\subsection{Encoding inversion}

A variable gadget on its own proves nothing; the constraints of
\lang{ETR-INV} have to be enforced as well. The following configuration
enforces inversion, which is the only non-linear constraint the normal form
needs.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:art-gallery-inversion}
In the configuration of \cref{fig:er-art-inversion}, assume no other guard
interferes and that the two guards lie on their respective segments,
representing values $x$ and $y$. Then together they see the whole critical wall
if and only if $x \cdot y \ge 1$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Mark the critical wall with values too. Let $s$ be the value of the highest
point of the wall seen by the first guard, and $t$ the value of the lowest point
seen by the second. The two guards see the whole wall exactly when
$s \ge t$.

The first guard's segment is parallel to the critical wall and its pivot point
lies exactly midway between the two, so $s = x$.

For the second, consider two similar triangles: one of height $1$ and width $y$,
the other of height $t$ and width $1$. Similarity gives
$\tfrac{1}{y} = \tfrac{t}{1}$, that is $t = 1/y$. So $s \ge t$ says
$x \ge 1/y$, which is $x \cdot y \ge 1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[page=1,width=0.62\textwidth]{er-art-inversion}
\caption{Two guards on their segments see the critical wall on the right
together if and only if $x \cdot y \ge 1$.}
\label{fig:er-art-inversion}
\end{figure}

\begin{intuition}
Why inversion rather than multiplication? Because multiplication is a non-linear
interaction between \emph{three} objects, all of which must be controlled
simultaneously, whereas inversion involves only \emph{two}. In a geometric
gadget that difference is the difference between hard and very hard, and it is
the reason \lang{ETR-INV} exists.

A postscript worth knowing: neither published hardness proof for the art gallery
problem actually uses the gadget above. It is simpler than the ones that were
used, but it has two defects --- the two guard segments are not parallel, so
copying a value from a horizontal segment to a vertical one is awkward, and it
is hard to connect to the rest of the construction. Finding a gadget of this
simplicity that does fit into a full reduction is still open.
\end{intuition}

\section{Point--line duality (non-examinable)}
\label{sec:duality}
%==============================================================================

\nonexam{The lecture no longer covers point--line duality, and the three pool
items asking for it have been withdrawn. The material is kept because
it is how \cref{sec:order-types} connects order types to pseudoline
arrangements, and because it is the standard vocabulary of the area. Nothing
earlier in the chapter depends on it.}

Points and lines in the plane carry the same combinatorial information, and the
standard way to move between them is point--line duality. One convenient choice
is the map $\chi$ from $\R^2$ to the set $L$ of lines,
\[ (a,b) \;\longleftrightarrow\; \ell_{(a,b)} : y = ax - b,
   \qquad y = mx + c \;\longleftrightarrow\; (m,-c). \]
We write $p^{*}$ for $\chi(p)$ and $\ell^{*}$ for $\chi^{-1}(\ell)$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:er-duality}
Let $p, q \in \R^2$ and let $\ell$ be a line. Then
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{verticality}: $p$ and $q$ have the same $x$-coordinate if and only
        if $p^{*}$ and $q^{*}$ are parallel;
  \item \emph{incidence}: $p$ lies on $\ell$ if and only if $p^{*}$ passes
        through $\ell^{*}$;
  \item \emph{above/below}: $p$ lies above $\ell$ if and only if $\ell^{*}$ lies
        above $p^{*}$.
\end{itemize}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Verticality is immediate from the definition, since the $x$-coordinate of a
point becomes the slope of its dual line.

For the other two, write $p = (a,b)$ and $\ell : y = mx + n$. Then $p$ lies on
$\ell$ exactly when $b = ma + n$, which rearranges to $-n = am - b$; and that
says exactly that the point $\ell^{*} = (m,-n)$ lies on the line
$p^{*} : y = ax - b$.

Replacing the equality by an inequality gives the third statement: $p$ lies
above $\ell$ exactly when $b > ma+n$, that is $-n > am - b$, that is $\ell^{*}$
lies above $p^{*}$.
\end{proof}

Combinatorial information about a point set therefore translates into
combinatorial information about an arrangement of lines. It is not quite as
easy as one might hope, though, and the reason is worth stating. Duality
preserves \emph{above and below}, whereas order types are about \emph{left and
right}. Reversing the orientation of a line leaves above and below unchanged
but swaps left and right; rotating a point set changes many above--below
relations but preserves the order type. What survives needs care.

\subsection{Cyclic order}
\label{sec:cyclic-orders}

Duality preserves above and below. Order types are about left and right, so one
more notion is needed before the two can be connected.

\begin{definition}[Cyclic order]
Let $P$ be a point set and $p$ a point. Rotate a vertical line clockwise around
$p$; the order in which it meets the points of $P$ is the \emph{cyclic order} of
$P$ around $p$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:er-cyclic-order}
Let $p_1, \ldots, p_n \in P$ be in cyclic order around $p$. Then the line
$p^{*}$ meets the lines $p_1^{*}, \ldots, p_n^{*}$ from left to right in that
order. Conversely, if lines $\ell_1, \ldots, \ell_n$ meet a line $\ell$ from
left to right in that order, then the dual points
$\ell_1^{*}, \ldots, \ell_n^{*}$ occur in that cyclic order around
$\ell^{*}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
We prove the second statement. Let $p_1, \ldots, p_n$ be the intersection
points of $\ell_1, \ldots, \ell_n$ with $\ell$, so that $p_1$ is to the left of
$p_2$, and so on. By \cref{lem:er-duality} the dual line $p_i^{*}$ has slope
equal to the $x$-coordinate of $p_i$, so $p_1^{*}$ has the smallest slope and
$p_n^{*}$ the largest. Each dual point $\ell_i^{*}$ lies on the dual line
$p_i^{*}$, and the slopes increasing is exactly the cyclic order around
$\ell^{*}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{er-cyclic-order}
\caption{Left: the line $\ell$ meets $\ell_1, \ldots, \ell_n$ from left to
right. Right: in the dual the same information is the cyclic order of the
points $\ell_i^{*}$ around $\ell^{*}$.}
\label{fig:er-cyclic-order}
\end{figure}

\begin{intuition}
\Cref{lem:er-cyclic-order} is the bridge between the two halves of this
subject. A cyclic order around a point is a statement about a point set; a
left-to-right crossing order along a line is a statement about a line
arrangement; and the lemma says they are the same statement. That is why a
problem about \emph{point configurations} --- the order type problem of
\cref{sec:order-types} --- and a problem about \emph{pseudoline arrangements}
--- \lang{Stretchability} --- turn out to be the same problem, which is what
\cref{thm:stretchability} rests on.
\end{intuition}

\begin{exercise}
Let $s = ab$ be the segment from $a$ to $b$. Describe the dual of $s$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
When a point set is rotated very slowly, how does the dual line arrangement
change combinatorially? Hint: watch the leftmost and rightmost crossings.
\end{exercise}

\section{Order types (non-examinable)}
\label{sec:order-types}
%==============================================================================

\nonexam{The lecture no longer covers order types, and the two pool items
asking for them have been withdrawn. The material is kept because it is
the historical route into $\ER$ and because it is where
\cref{thm:stretchability} --- which the reductions earlier in this chapter do
use --- comes from. Nothing in this section is examined, and nothing earlier in
the chapter depends on it.}

Numerical instability was a problem computational geometers noticed early, and
it produced a wish for algorithms that solve geometric problems but are
combinatorial in nature. Remarkably, many geometric problems can be solved
knowing only the \emph{order type} of a point set.

\begin{definition}[Order type]\label{def:order-type}
The \emph{order type} of a point set specifies, for each ordered triple of
points, whether the third point lies on, to the left of, or to the right of the
line through the first two --- equivalently, whether the triple is collinear,
oriented clockwise, or oriented counterclockwise.
\end{definition}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{er-order-type}
\caption{The order type records, for every ordered triple, whether it turns
left, turns right, or is collinear.}
\label{fig:er-order-type}
\end{figure}

The convex hull of a point set, for instance, is completely determined by the
order type: once the order type has been extracted, the hull can be computed
without ever looking at a coordinate again. A second motivation is structural.
If we accept that the order type contains all the essential information about a
point set, then for each $n$ there are only finitely many order types, and hence
only finitely many point configurations up to order type --- so one may hope to
prove structural theorems about point sets the way one does about graphs.

Out of mathematical curiosity, \citet{Ringel1956} asked in 1956: are there two point sets
$P$ and $Q$ in the plane that cannot be moved continuously into one another
without changing the order type along the way? The answer is yes, but it is
hard to give a concrete example or a simple proof; the smallest known such
configuration has $13$ points and was found in 2012.

To answer Ringel's question, \citet{Mnev1985} and \citet{Shor1991} studied the
realisation space of order types in full generality --- and did so in the
equivalent language of stretchability, which is how \cref{thm:stretchability}
came to exist.


Their proof reduces from the \lang{Order Type Problem}; a coherent account in
the language of oriented matroids is \citet{BokowskiMockStreinu2001}. We do not
give it. What joins the two halves of the picture is
\cref{lem:er-duality,lem:er-cyclic-order}: a cyclic order around a point becomes
a left-to-right crossing order in the dual, and the crossing order is exactly
what a pseudoline arrangement records. So a question about which point sets
exist is a question about which pseudoline arrangements can be straightened.

The problem of deciding whether a given abstract order type is realised by an
actual point set --- and its variant the \lang{Partial Order Type Problem}, in
which only some triples are prescribed --- is $\ER$-complete by the same
circle of reductions. The route runs \lang{ETR-AM} $\to$ partial order types,
by placing a point on a line for each variable and building a von Staudt gadget
for each addition and each multiplication constraint.
